# Punctuation

Source: CMOS ch 6 (rules 6.1-6.143). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch06. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch06

1. **Serial comma, always** (6.19); comma before final *etc.* / *and so forth* (6.20); drop it before an ampersand (6.21). Reword if the comma cannot cure ambiguity ("I thanked my mother and Madonna and Lady Gaga").
2. **Commas in pairs** (6.17): if you open a set-off element, close it—except inside titles. Same logic governs dates (6.41), places (6.42), appositives (6.30), and vocatives (6.57).
3. **Know which side of the closing quotation mark a mark belongs on** (6.9, 6.10, Table 6.1): periods and commas inside; semicolons and colons outside; ?/! inside only if part of the quotation.
4. **Never comma-splice** (6.23); repair with a conjunction, semicolon, period, parentheses, or dash—and only when the repair is "a clear improvement."
5. **Restrictive vs. nonrestrictive is the master test** (6.29, 6.30, 6.32, 6.33, 6.34, 6.39, 6.44, 6.53): essential → no commas; optional → commas. Use *that* for restrictive, *which* for nonrestrictive.
6. **Introductory dependent clause → comma** (6.26); **trailing restrictive dependent clause → no comma** (6.27). If meaning hinges on the comma ("He wasn't running, because he was afraid"), rephrase.
7. **No comma in a compound predicate** (6.24) or between imperative clauses (6.25); add one only to prevent misreading or for a clear afterthought.
8. **Colon only after a grammatically complete sentence** (6.71); lowercase after a colon unless a complete sentence follows (6.67). Don't put a colon between verb and object.
9. **Semicolon between independent clauses without a conjunction** (6.60), and always before *however/thus/therefore* joining clauses (6.61); semicolons in a series whose items contain commas (6.64).
10. **Comma before quoted dialogue** after *said/replied/asked* (6.43); no comma after *that/whether/if* (6.43); titles after nouns follow the appositive rule, not a blanket comma (6.44).
11. **No commas with *Jr.*/*Sr.*, *II*/*III*, *Inc.*/*Ltd.*, or month-year dates** (6.46, 6.47, 6.41).
12. **En dash = "to/through"** in ranges (6.83) and "never after *from*/*between*": write *from 2019 to 2022*, *between 11:30 a.m. and 1:30 p.m.*
13. **En dash joins two people's names in a compound modifier** (6.85: Ali–Frazier) and can stand for an open compound (6.86: post–World War II); otherwise hyphen.
14. **Em dash for an abrupt break** (6.91); never nest em-dash elements and never more than two per sentence (6.91); no comma/colon/semicolon immediately before an em dash (6.95); em dash for interruption in dialogue (6.93).
15. **Choose the punctuation by the weight of the break**: comma < dash/parentheses (6.51); parentheses for material with no grammatical tie to the sentence (6.101).
16. **No comma before closing parenthesis/bracket; punctuation goes inside a parenthesis only if it belongs to the parenthetical matter** (6.18, 6.104); avoid enclosing whole sentences inside another sentence (6.13).
17. **Brackets, not parentheses, for editorial insertions in quotes** (6.105); never "correct" a quotation by inserting a comma inside brackets (6.110). Keep angle brackets and braces out of prose (6.111).
18. **Avoid slashes in formal prose** (6.113): *and/or* → *or* (or rewrite); no all-numeral dates (6.115); no spaces in URLs (6.120).
19. **Smart quotes and smart apostrophes only** (6.123, 6.125): ’90s, ’em, rock ’n’ roll; never a left single quote where an apostrophe belongs; no punctuation between a word and its final apostrophe (6.126).
20. **One space between sentences and after a colon** (6.7, 6.66); no space before punctuation; nonbreaking space where a break would be wrong (6.129).
21. **Never double a period; never let a period accompany ? or !** (6.132, 6.133); drop the comma after a dialogue quotation ending in ?/! but keep it after a title (6.134), and never write "Woolf??" (6.135).
22. **Exclamation points sparingly** (6.75); do not add "[!]" to quoted matter (6.77); a rhetorical question may take a period instead of a question mark (6.12, 6.76).
23. **Indirect questions take no question mark, no comma, no capital** (6.73, 6.45); a polite request can end in a period (6.73).
24. **Lists: parallel items, complete-sentence colon, no end punctuation on fragments, lowercase unless ordered, semicolons when items contain commas** (6.138, 6.140, 6.141, 6.142).
25. **Repeated adjective pairs take a comma** (6.40); vocatives take commas (6.57); *too/either* usually don't (6.56).

## Overview (rule 6.1)

- **[6.1] Punctuate by function, then apply principles consistently** — Punctuation exists "to promote ease of reading by clarifying relationships within and between sentences." Subjectivity is allowed, but it "should in turn be governed by the consistent application of some basic principles lest the subjective element obscure meaning." How strictly the rules apply depends on register: "A formal register may be approached differently from a conversational tone, and fiction and other creative contexts will not have the same requirements as academic prose." **[JUDGMENT]**
  > The role of punctuation . . . is to promote ease of reading by clarifying relationships within and between sentences. This function, although it allows for a degree of subjectivity, should in turn be governed by the consistent application of some basic principles lest the subjective element obscure meaning.

## Punctuation in Relation to Surrounding Text (rules 6.2–6.11)

- **[6.2] Punctuation takes the font style of the surrounding text, not of the word it follows** — All punctuation marks should appear in roman or italic to match the main text, "except for punctuation that belongs to a title in a different style (usually italics)." The comma after an italic word used as a word ("the word *and*, which . . .") is roman. In the last examples, the comma after "Hello," the exclamation point after "Dolly," and the colon after "Prose" are italic because they belong to the italic titles; the comma after *Punctuation* in the final example is roman. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *For light amusement he turns to the* Principia Mathematica*!* (exclamation point roman)
  - ex: *Hello, Dolly!* premiered on Broadway in 1964.
  - ex: According to *Prose: A Matter of Punctuation*, periods are usually round.
- **[6.3] With boldface or color, decide punctuation case by case** — Because boldface "is sometimes an aesthetic rather than a purely logical decision," punctuation following bold or colored text is handled per instance: a period belonging to a bold glossary term stays bold; a period belonging to the surrounding sentence does not; a question mark belonging to the sentence is not bold. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: **line spacing**. See **leading**. (first period bold, second roman)
  - ex: Will the installation remain stalled until I choose **I accept**? (question mark belongs to the sentence)
- **[6.4] The traditional "match the preceding word" system is for print only** — Under the older system, periods, commas, colons, and semicolons take the style of the immediately preceding word/letter/symbol; ? and ! do so only if they belong to that word. "This system, once preferred by Chicago and still preferred by some as more aesthetically pleasing, should be reserved—if it must be used—for publications destined for print only." Electronic formats should follow 6.2. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: the commas after italic *a* and *b*, and the colon after the italic book title, would be italic under this system.
- **[6.5] Parentheses and brackets match the surrounding text, not their contents** — Keep them roman or italic to match surrounding prose even when they enclose italic matter; a thin or hair space may be needed in print where an italic letter overlaps an upright parenthesis. Exception: a parenthetical line standing alone takes the style of the phrase it encloses. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The Asian long-horned beetle (*Anoplophora glabripennis*) attacks maples.
  - ex: The letter stated that my check had been "recieved [sic] with thanks."
  - ex: *[continued on page 72]*
- **[6.6] Quotation marks match the surrounding text; one italic exception** — Quotation marks are roman or italic as the surrounding text is, not as the quoted matter is. But "in the relatively rare case of an italic title that ends in a quotation mark," a following comma or period is set in italics to avoid a style switch. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: We started by reading a book called *The Return of "Twin Peaks,"* by Franck Boulègue. (comma and closing quote italic)
  - ex: "Dressing Like Hamlet," the fourth article . . . (comma and closing quote roman)
- **[6.7] One space, not two, between sentences** — In typeset matter use a single space after a period, question mark, exclamation point, or closing quotation mark/parenthesis, and a single space after a colon. Any wider spacing is a design decision to be specified exactly. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.8] Punctuate sentences containing URLs and email addresses normally** — Wrap the sentence's punctuation around the URL/email as usual; wrappers such as angle brackets "are generally unnecessary in normal prose"; in print, readers assume terminal punctuation belongs to the sentence, and hypertext markup should exclude surrounding punctuation. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: You'll find the Q&A and other resources at https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/.
  - ex: Write to me at grammar88@parsed-out.edu.
- **[6.9] Periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks—double or single** — This is traditional US style, in use "well before the first edition of this manual (1906)." Do not confuse a final apostrophe with a closing single quotation mark (6.126). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: He described what he heard as a "short, sharp shock."
  - ex: "Thus conscience does make cowards of us all," she replied.
  - British alternative: single quotation marks (double reserved for quotations within quotations), with **only punctuation that appeared in the original inside**—‘like this’. Exceptions: British publishers usually follow US placement for fictional dialogue and for quoted speech in journalism; textual criticism and technical/coding settings may also use the British system.
- **[6.10] Colons and semicolons go outside closing quotation marks; ? and ! go inside only if they belong to the quotation** — "(This rule applies the logic that is often absent from the traditional US style described in 6.9.)" **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Take, for example, the first line of "Filling Station": "Oh, but it is dirty!" (colon outside, ! inside)
  - ex: We were told to read "Filling Station"; we ended up reciting Shakespeare instead. (semicolon outside)
  - ex: I can't believe you don't know the poem "Filling Station"! (! belongs to the sentence, so outside)
  - ex: Which of Shakespeare's characters said, "All the world's a stage"? (? belongs to the sentence)
  - ex: "What's the rush?" she asked. / "Timber!"
- **[6.11] Put a (nonbreaking) space between adjacent single and double quotation marks** — Where nested marks abut, a thin or hair space in print, or a regular/narrow nonbreaking space on screen, "aids legibility" and prevents the second mark from being stranded at a line start. Note the period precedes the single quote. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: “My favorite story,” I told them, “is called ‘Interpreter of Maladies.’” (period before the single quote)

## Periods (rules 6.12–6.15)

- **[6.12] Use a period to end declarative and imperative sentences—and sometimes a rhetorical question** — A period may follow a word or phrase standing alone and "may be used in lieu of a question mark at the end of a rhetorical question" (see 6.76). Called a dot in URLs, a point in decimals, a full stop in British usage. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Wait here.
  - ex: My answer? Never.
  - ex: When will I ever learn. (rhetorical question, period)
- **[6.13] Place the period inside a parenthesis/bracket only when the whole sentence is enclosed** — If a complete sentence sits inside another sentence, the period belongs outside the closing parenthesis/bracket. If the parenthetical matter itself ends in an abbreviation period (*etc.*), two periods are required: one for the abbreviation inside, one for the sentence outside. **[MECHANICAL for placement; JUDGMENT for structure]**
  - ex: Fiorelli insisted on rewriting the paragraph. (His newfound ability to type was both a blessing and a curse.)
  - ex: Felipe had left an angry message for Isadora on the mantel (she noticed it while glancing in the mirror).
  - ex: His chilly demeanor gave him an affinity for the noble gases (helium, neon, etc.).
  - "Whenever possible, avoid enclosing more than one complete sentence within another sentence"—move them out or combine them (semicolon, rephrasing).
- **[6.14] Omit the period after display lines, heads, captions, signatures, and addresses** — No period after a chapter title, subhead, running head, column head, caption phrase, dateline, signature, or address—unless the line ends in an abbreviation. A run-in subhead, however, is followed by a period. When an abbreviation ending in a period ends a sentence, no second period is added. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.15] Use an ellipsis (three dots) for omissions and for faltering speech** — An ellipsis may mark an omission in quoted material (fuller treatment at 12.59–69) or indicate faltering speech or an incomplete thought (12.43, 12.64). For a sudden break or interruption, use an em dash instead (6.93). **[JUDGMENT]**

## Commas (rules 6.16–6.59)

- **[6.16] Treat the comma as the smallest structural break, with logic before pause** — The comma "indicates the smallest break in sentence structure" and "usually denotes a slight pause," but "in formal prose . . . the logical considerations that are the basis of the recommendations in this chapter come first." Some authors punctuate closely, others openly; "Editors should be prepared to account for these differences in register, context, and authorial style." **[JUDGMENT]**
  > Whatever choices are made should ultimately aim to serve the reader.
- **[6.17] Commas come in pairs** — "Whenever a comma is placed before an element to set it off from the surrounding text . . . a second comma is required if the phrase or sentence continues beyond the element being set off." Exception: commas inside the title of a work are independent of the surrounding sentence. **[MECHANICAL detection; JUDGMENT when rephrasing]**
  - ex: August 18, 1920, was a good day for American women.
  - ex: Sledding in Duluth, Minnesota, is facilitated by that city's hills and frigid winters.
  - but: The title story in Bambara's *Gorilla, My Love* had been published in *Redbook* under a different title.
- **[6.18] A comma never precedes a closing parenthesis or bracket; it follows it** — A comma needed at the end of parenthetical matter goes after the closing parenthesis/bracket. Rarely a comma appears inside, before a closing bracket, as part of an editorial interpolation. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: After several drummers had tried out for the part (the last having destroyed the kit), the band decided that a drum machine was their steadiest option.
  - ex: "Conrad told his assistant [Martin], who was clearly exhausted, to rest."
  - ex: "The contents of the vault included fennel seeds, tweezers, [straight-edged razors,] and empty Coca-Cola cans."

### Series and the Serial Comma (rules 6.19–6.21)

- **[6.19] Use the serial (Oxford) comma before the conjunction in a series of three or more** — "Chicago strongly recommends this widely practiced usage, blessed by Fowler and other authorities . . . since it prevents ambiguity." If the last element is itself a pair joined by *and*, still put a serial comma before the first *and*. If the sentence continues beyond the series, add a comma only if syntax requires it. Where the serial comma cannot prevent ambiguity, **reword**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: She posted pictures of her parents, the president, and the vice president.
  - ex: Before heading out the door, he took note of the typical outlines of sweet gum, ginkgo, and elm leaves.
  - ex: I want no ifs, ands, or buts.
  - ex: Their wartime rations included cabbage, turnips, and bread and butter.
  - ex: Ahmed was configuring updates, Jean was installing new hardware, and Alan was running errands and furnishing food.
  - ex (series continues): Apples, plums, and grapes can all be used to make wine. **but** Apples, plums, and grapes, available at most large grocery stores, can all be used to make wine.
  - ex: I thanked my mother and Madonna and Lady Gaga. **not** I thanked my mother, Madonna, and Lady Gaga. (repetition of *and* avoids reading Madonna as the mother; or as an appositive)
  - ex: The team fielded one Mazda, two Corvettes, and three Bugattis, as well as a battered Plymouth Belvedere. **not** . . . two Corvettes, three Bugattis, as well as . . . — *as well as* cannot substitute for *and* in a series.
  - No commas when every element is joined by a conjunction, unless elements are long: *Would you prefer Mendelssohn or Schumann or Liszt?*
- **[6.20] Put a comma before a final *etc.*, *and so forth*, *and the like*** — Such terms are preceded by a comma as the last item of a series and followed by a comma only if the surrounding text requires it. In formal prose, Chicago prefers to restrict *etc.* to parentheses, notes, and tables. *et al.* is treated the same way; after a single item ("Jones et al.") no preceding comma is needed. Neither *etc.* nor *et al.* is italicized. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The map was far from complete (lacking many of the streets, alleys, etc. seen in earlier iterations).
  - ex: The philosopher's population studies, classic textbooks, stray notes, and so forth were found in the attic.
  - but: For a discussion of periods, commas, and the like, see chapter 6.
- **[6.21] Drop the serial comma before an ampersand** — When *&* replaces *and* (as in company names), the serial comma is omitted. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Winken, Blinken & Nod is a purveyor of nightwear.

### Commas with Independent Clauses (rules 6.22–6.25)

- **[6.22] Put a comma before a coordinating conjunction joining independent clauses** — With *and, but, or, so, yet* (or another coordinating conjunction), a comma usually precedes the conjunction. It may be omitted if the clauses are very short and closely connected—unless they are part of a series. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: We activated the alarm, but the intruder was already inside.
  - ex: Do we want to foster creativity, or are we interested only in our intellectual property?
  - ex: The bus never came, so we took a taxi.
  - but (no comma, short clauses): Electra played the guitar and Tambora sang.
- **[6.23] Never join two independent clauses with a comma alone (comma splice)** — In formal prose the comma splice "is usually considered an error"; fix it with a conjunction, semicolon, period, parentheses, or dash. But do not correct mechanically: "the occasional comma splice can be effective, especially in conversational prose and creative writing"; consider whether the "correction" is "a clear improvement." **[MECHANICAL detection; JUDGMENT to fix]**
  - ex (splice): All watches display the time, some of them do so accurately.
  - ex (five fixes): . . . the time, and some . . . / . . . the time; some . . . / . . . the time. Some . . . / . . . the time (some . . .) / . . . the time—some . . .
- **[6.24] Do not use a comma before a conjunction joining parts of a compound predicate** — "A compound predicate occurs when a subject that is shared by two or more clauses is not repeated after the first clause." Add a comma only to prevent a misreading, or (editorial discretion) when the first part is very long or the second reads as an afterthought. Use a comma before *then* used for *and then*. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: He printed out a week's worth of crossword puzzles and arranged them on his clipboard.
  - ex: Kelleher tried to contact the mayor but was informed that she had stopped accepting unsolicited calls.
  - ex (comma for clarity): She recognized the man who entered the room, and gasped.
  - ex (afterthought): Whole chapters of her novel are written in the style of a nineteenth-century travelogue, and were probably intended that way.
  - ex: She filled in the last square in Sunday's puzzle, then yawned. **but** . . . puzzle and then yawned.
- **[6.25] Do not add a comma between two imperative clauses joined by a conjunction** — A change from earlier editions: because the sentence can be read as one clause with a compound predicate, "this manual now prefers" no comma. Add one only if required for other reasons or to prevent ambiguity (e.g., a long or complex intervening phrase). Three or more imperative clauses are punctuated as a series. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Wait for me at the bottom of the hill on Buffalo Street or walk up to Eddy Street and meet me next to the Yield sign.
  - ex: Find each instance of the word *ax* and change the spelling to *axe*.
  - ex (comma needed): Find each instance of the word *ax*, including any in the index, and change the spelling to *axe*.
  - ex (series): Revise the first chapter, double-check your work, and send it back to me.

### Commas with Dependent Clauses (rules 6.26–6.28)

- **[6.26] Put a comma after an introductory dependent clause** — A dependent clause (introduced by *if, because, when*, etc.) preceding the main clause "should be followed by a comma." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: If you accept our conditions, we will agree to the proposal.
  - ex: Until we have seen the light, we cannot guarantee a safe exit from the tunnel.
  - ex: Whether you agree with her or not, she has a point.
- **[6.27] Do not put a comma before a restrictive dependent clause that follows the main clause; do put one before a nonrestrictive one** — If the clause is essential to meaning, no comma; if merely supplementary or parenthetical, a comma. "Such distinctions are occasionally tenuous. . . . If in doubt, rephrase." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (restrictive, no comma): We will agree to the proposal if you accept our conditions.
  - ex (restrictive): He wasn't running because he was afraid; he was running because he was late.
  - ex (nonrestrictive, comma): He wasn't running, because he was afraid of the dark. **or, better:** Because he was afraid of the dark, he wasn't running.
  - ex: I'd like the tom yum, if you don't mind. / At last she arrived, when the food was cold.
- **[6.28] Consecutive conjunctions (and if, but when, that if) need no separating comma** — When a dependent clause intervenes between two clauses, the coordinating and subordinating conjunctions need not be split by a comma; likewise no comma between two subordinating conjunctions. Adding one "would not be wrong" and may be preferred for emphasis or a closely punctuated style. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Burton examined the documents for over an hour, and if Smedley had not intervened, the forgery would have been revealed.
  - ex: They decided that if it rained, they would reschedule the game.

### Commas with Relative Clauses, Appositives, and Descriptive Phrases (rules 6.29–6.32)

- **[6.29] *That* clauses are restrictive and take no commas; *which* clauses are nonrestrictive and are set off by commas** — A restrictive (defining) clause is "essential to understanding the intended meaning" and is "never set off by commas"; it is usually introduced by *that* (or *who/whom/whose*) and the relative pronoun may sometimes be omitted. Nonrestrictive clauses—which could be omitted without obscuring the noun's identity—take commas and usually *which*. *Which* can replace *that* in a restrictive clause (common in British English), but "many writers preserve the distinction." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (restrictive): The manuscript that the editors submitted to the publisher was well formatted.
  - ex (restrictive, pronoun omitted): The book I have just finished is due back tomorrow; the others can wait.
  - ex (nonrestrictive): The final manuscript, which was well formatted, was submitted to the publisher on time.
  - ex (contrast pair): I prefer to share the road with drivers who focus on driving rather than on what they happen to be reading. **vs.** I prefer to share the road with illiterate drivers, who are unlikely to read books while driving.
  - ex: Boris Pasternak, whose most famous creation was a doctor, wrote what is probably the best novel about the Russian Revolution.
- **[6.30] Set off a nonrestrictive appositive with commas; leave a restrictive one bare** — If the appositive can be omitted without obscuring identity, it takes commas; if it supplies essential identifying information, "no commas should appear." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (nonrestrictive): The title of Miranda's first musical, *In the Heights*, refers to the Washington Heights neighborhood of Manhattan. (the word *first* already identifies the musical)
  - ex (restrictive): The movie version of Miranda's Broadway musical *Hamilton* is valuable for its subtitles. (he wrote more than one)
  - ex (restrictive): Former poet laureate Joy Harjo took the stage.
- **[6.31] Spousal/relationship commas depend on uniqueness** — When a name identifies the only one of its kind (usually a spouse), commas are normally required; a restrictive name takes none. Commas may be omitted when the number is unknown or irrelevant, when the tone is casual, or per an author's less-close style. Always omit to avoid an awkward possessive. Always use commas (1) when an adjective uniquely identifies the generic term and (2) when the name precedes the generic term. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Desi's wife, Shondra, has a PhD. but Dillon's dog Spot is a Dalmatian.
  - ex: Shondra's husband Desi is allergic to cats. (informal register)
  - ex (awkward possessive): Desi's wife Shondra's cats are allergic to dogs. **not** Desi's wife's, Shondra's, cats . . .
  - ex (always commas): Shondra's least friendly cat, Felix, has long hair. / Shondra, Desi's wife, loves cats.
- **[6.32] Set off a nonrestrictive descriptive phrase; leave a restrictive one unpunctuated** — A descriptive phrase essential to the noun's meaning takes no commas; a parenthetical one is enclosed in commas (or preceded by one at sentence end). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (parenthetical): Elizabeth Taylor's second marriage, to Michael Wilding, ended in divorce.
  - ex (restrictive): Elizabeth Taylor's second marriage to Richard Burton ended in divorce.

### Commas with Participial and Adverbial Phrases (rules 6.33–6.35)

- **[6.33] Set off participial phrases with commas unless restrictive** — An introductory participial phrase is normally followed by a comma; mid-sentence it is set off by commas unless it is restrictive; at sentence end a comma is used unless restrictive. Do **not** use a comma when the participial phrase modifies the subject via a linking verb (even when inverted). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Exhilarated by her morning workout, she headed for the ocean. / The actor, having forgotten his lines, was forced to ad-lib.
  - ex (restrictive, no commas): Actors forgetting their lines may be forced to ad-lib.
  - ex (restrictive at end): She always headed for the ocean exhilarated by her morning workout.
  - ex (no comma with linking verb): Running along behind the wagon was the archduke himself!
- **[6.34] An introductory adverbial phrase may take a comma but need not unless misreading is likely** — Shorter phrases are less likely to merit a comma. Mid-sentence, adverbial phrases are normally set off by commas. At sentence end, a comma is necessary only when the phrase is nonrestrictive. Never set off an adverbial phrase that introduces an inverted sentence. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: On the other hand, his vices could be considered virtues. / In 1931 Henrietta turned fifty.
  - ex (comma needed): Before eating, the members of the committee met in the assembly room. / To Anthony, Blake remained an enigma.
  - ex: Jackson, with three consecutive swings, made history. **vs.** Jackson made history with three consecutive swings.
  - ex (nonrestrictive tail): Henrietta turned fifty a decade later, in 1931.
  - ex (inverted, no comma): Before the footlights stood one of the most notorious rakes of the twenty-first century.
- **[6.35] With a phrase right after a conjunction, place the comma by clause structure** — If the conjunction joins a compound predicate, the first comma follows the conjunction; if it joins two independent clauses, the comma precedes the conjunction. A second comma after the conjunction "would not be wrong." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: We were extremely tired and, having run out of options, eager to go home.
  - ex: We were elated, but realizing that the day was almost over, we decided to go to bed.

### Commas with Introductory Words and Phrases (rules 6.36–6.38)

- **[6.36] Whether an introductory phrase takes a comma depends on type, relation, and length** — Use 6.33 for participial phrases, 6.34 for adverbial phrases, 6.26 for dependent clauses, and 6.37–6.38 for specific words. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.37] Follow an introductory *yes*, *no*, *OK*, *well*, or the like with a comma** — Except in informal prose or dialogue. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Yes, it is true that 78 percent of the subjects ate 50 percent more than they reported.
  - ex: Well then, we will have to take a vote. **but** No you will not!
- **[6.38] A comma usually follows exclamatory *oh* or *ah*—but not vocative *O*** — No comma when the interjection is followed by an exclamation point or dash, or forms part of a phrase ("oh boy," "ah yes"). No comma follows a vocative *oh* or poetic *O*. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Oh, you're right! / Ah, here we are at last! / Oh no! Ah yes! / My oh my! / Oh mighty king! / "O wild West Wind . . ."

### Commas with Two or More Adjectives Preceding a Noun (rules 6.39–6.40)

- **[6.39] Separate coordinate adjectives with commas; do not separate cumulative ones** — Coordinate adjectives could be joined by *and* without changing meaning and can usually be reversed; adjectives that form a unit with the noun take no comma. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Shelly had proved a faithful, sincere friend. / It is going to be a long, hot, exhausting summer.
  - ex (no comma): She has many faithful friends. / He has rejected traditional religious affiliations. / She opted for an inexpensive quartz watch.
- **[6.40] Put a comma between a repeated adjective and its twin** — "Many, many people have enjoyed the book." **[MECHANICAL]**

### Commas with Dates and Addresses (rules 6.41–6.42)

- **[6.41] In month-day-year dates, set off the year with commas; omit commas with month-year, holiday-year, and day+ordinal references** — In month-day-year style, commas "must be used to set off the year," applying commas-in-pairs logic. A named day of the week is separated from the month and day by a comma, but commas are unnecessary between the day name and an ordinal when no month is expressed. No commas with month and year only, or a named holiday with a year. In day-month-year style (British; useful where many full dates appear), no commas are needed. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The performance took place on February 2, 2006, at the State Theatre in Ithaca.
  - ex: Monday, May 5, was a holiday; Tuesday the 6th was not.
  - ex: Her license expires sometime in April 2027. / On Thanksgiving Day 1998 they celebrated their seventy-fifth anniversary.
  - ex (day-month-year): . . . his journal entries of 6 October 2022 and 4 January 2024.
- **[6.42] Set off run-in address and place-name elements with commas, sparingly in mailing addresses** — Commas separate elements of run-in addresses; in a mailing address use them mainly to set off separate lines and to separate city and state/province (not the postal code). If an institutional name contains a place-name set off by commas, a second comma is required mid-clause. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: A printout was sent to the author at 743 Olga Drive NE, Ashtabula, OH 44044, on May 2.
  - ex: Waukegan, Illinois, is not far from the Wisconsin border.
  - ex: California State University, Northridge, has an enrollment of . . . **but** The University of Wisconsin-Madison has an enrollment of . . .

### Commas with Quotations and Questions (rules 6.43–6.45)

- **[6.43] Use a comma to introduce a quoted independent clause after *said/replied/asked/wrote*, regardless of position** — This traditional usage treats the quoted syntax as separate from the introducing text. No comma when the quotation is introduced by *that*, *whether*, *if*, or a similar conjunction, and usually none when a form of *to be* intervenes. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: It was Thoreau who wrote, "One generation abandons the enterprises of another like stranded vessels."
  - ex: "I hope," she replied, "you aren't referring to us." / "I hope you aren't referring to us," she replied.
  - ex (no comma): Was it Stevenson who said that "the cruelest lies are often told in silence"? / He wondered whether "to think is to live."
  - ex (no comma): What they said was "The apple falls far from the tree."
- **[6.44] Commas with quoted or italicized titles follow the appositive rule** — Treat a title or quoted expression as a noun: use commas only if the title is nonrestrictive. "A common mistake is to use a comma before a title or expression whenever it follows a noun that describes it (e.g., story, novel, or poem)." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (restrictive, no comma): Elizabeth Bishop's poem "One Art" was featured in Jennifer Weiner's novel *In Her Shoes* . . .
  - ex: The motto "All for one and one for all" appears over the door.
  - ex (nonrestrictive): Tom's favorite proverb, "A rolling stone gathers no moss," proved wrong.
  - ex (nonrestrictive, already identified): In the collection's second essay, "Everybody's Protest Novel," Baldwin critiques *Uncle Tom's Cabin*.
- **[6.45] Introduce an unquoted direct question with a comma and a capital; do not punctuate an indirect question** — A direct question embedded in a sentence (not in quotation marks) is usually introduced by a comma unless it begins the sentence, and begins with a capital. No comma is needed after the question mark if the question ends mid-sentence. Indirect questions take no question mark, no comma, and no capital. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: She wondered, What am I doing? / What am I doing? she wondered.
  - ex (indirect): She wondered what she was doing. / Ursula wondered why her watch had stopped ticking.

### Commas in Personal and Corporate Names (rules 6.46–6.47)

- **[6.46] No commas around *Jr.* and *Sr.*; never around *II*, *III*, and the like** — Commas are not required with *Jr.*/*Sr.* and are "never used" to set off *II*, *III*, etc. In an inverted name (as in an index), a comma is required before the element at the end. If house style requires commas, a second comma is normally needed mid-sentence—rephrase to avoid the possessive. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: John Doe Sr. continues to cast a shadow over his son. / Jason Deer III has turned over stewardship . . .
  - ex (inverted): Doe, John, Sr. / Deer, Jason, III
  - ex: The speech made by John Doe, Sr., ran long. (not John Doe, Sr.'s, speech . . .)
- **[6.47] No commas around *Inc.*, *Ltd.*, and the like** — Follow one consistent style rather than making exceptions for individual companies. If commas must be used, a second comma is normally required mid-sentence—rephrase to avoid the possessive. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: QuartzMove Inc. was just one such company named in the suit.

### Commas with Antithetical Elements (rules 6.48–6.50)

- **[6.48] Set off an interjected *not* phrase with commas** — Use commas to set off a clarifying phrase beginning with *not*. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: We hoped the mayor herself, not her assistant, would attend the meeting. / They want you, not him.
- **[6.49] With *not . . . but* and *not only . . . but also*, commas depend on structure** — Joining a compound predicate or a pair of phrases, commas are usually unnecessary; joining two independent clauses, a comma normally precedes the second conjunction. An elided second conjunction yields a borderline comma splice "that nonetheless can be acceptable in less formal registers." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: We not only camped in the woods but canoed down the river. / Works of art are created not by inspiration but by persistence.
  - ex (independent clauses): Not only did we camp in the woods, but we also canoed down the river.
- **[6.50] Use a comma between the halves of *the more . . . the more* clauses; skip it for short phrases** — "The more I discover about the workings of mechanical movements, the less I seem to care about the holy grail of perfectly accurate timekeeping." **but** "The more the merrier." **[JUDGMENT]**

### Other Uses of the Comma (rules 6.51–6.59)

- **[6.51] Use commas for a light parenthetical break; switch to dashes or parentheses for a stronger one** — Commas may set off a parenthetical element "if only a slight break is intended"; such insertions are awkward between an adjective and its noun—reword. "If a stronger break is needed or if there are commas within the parenthetical element, em dashes (6.91) or parentheses (6.101) should be used instead of commas." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: All the test participants, in spite of our initial fears, recovered. / Commas can, we are told, add nuance.
  - ex: She was the fastest, not to mention the strongest, runner on her team. **or, better:** She was the fastest runner on her team, not to mention the strongest.
- **[6.52] Set off *however*, *therefore*, *indeed* with commas unless the adverb is essential or emphatic** — "A truly efficient gasoline-powered engine remains, however, a pipe dream." / "Indeed, not one test subject accurately predicted the amount of soup in the bowl." **but** "If you cheat and are therefore disqualified, you may also risk losing your scholarship." / "That was indeed the outcome of the study." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.53] Apply the restrictive/nonrestrictive test to phrases with *such as* and *including*** — Nonrestrictive (parenthetical) → commas; restrictive (essential) → no commas. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (nonrestrictive): The entire band, including the matutinal lead singer, overslept the noon rehearsal.
  - ex (restrictive): Words such as matutinal and onomatopoetic are best avoided in everyday speech.
  - ex (nonrestrictive): Some words, such as matutinal and onomatopoetic, are best avoided in everyday speech.
- **[6.54] Follow *that is*/*namely*/*for example* with a comma; precede them with an em dash or semicolon, not a comma** — Because they apply only to what follows, "they are best preceded by an em dash or (before an independent clause) a semicolon rather than a comma"; alternatively enclose the whole phrase in parentheses or em dashes. In formal writing, confine *i.e.* and *e.g.* to parentheses or notes, where a comma follows. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: There are simple alternatives to the stigmatized plastic shopping bag—namely, reusable cloth bags and foldable carts.
  - ex: The committee (that is, its more influential members) wanted to drop the matter.
  - ex: Luna prepared the document for editing; that is, she ran a series of macros and applied paragraph and character styles.
  - ex: Bones from various small animals—for example, a squirrel, a cat, a pigeon, and a muskrat—were found in the doctor's cabinet.
- **[6.55] Set off an explanatory alternative with commas; do not set off a nonexplanatory *or*** — "The compass stand, or binnacle, must be situated within the helmsman's field of vision." **but** "A slash or a period can be typed without using the Shift key." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.56] Do not put a comma before *too* or *either* meaning "also"—usually** — Some authors prefer the comma, so query before changing. Mid-sentence *too* is "more likely to be helpful" with commas. *(Printed number missing in OCR; rule sits between 6.55 and 6.57.)* **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: I had my cake and ate it too. / Rosario prefers Thelonious Monk over Duke Ellington; her brother does too.
  - ex: She, too, decided against the early showing.
- **[6.57] Set off names in direct address with commas (vocative commas)** — One comma at either end of the sentence, two mid-sentence. After an introductory coordinating conjunction the comma before the name is optional. In correspondence a comma (or colon, or dash) follows the greeting; a comma may also precede the name after *Hello* but is often omitted. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Ms. Jones, please come in. / Kiss me, you fool! / Are you listening, class? / I am not here, my friends, to discuss personalities.
  - ex: But Jacob, we haven't even started yet. **or** But, Jacob, we haven't even started yet.
  - ex: Dear Lucien, . . . / Hi, Ketanji, . . . **or, less formally,** Hi Ketanji, . . .
- **[6.58] Use a comma to mark an elided word or words** — The omitted verb may differ in number from the others in the sentence. The comma may be dropped when the ellipsis is clear without it. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: In Illinois there are seventeen such schools; in Ohio, twenty; in Indiana, thirteen.
  - ex: The first five candidates have chosen mathematics; the sixth, hermeneutics; and the seventh and eighth, volleyball.
  - ex: One student excels at composition, another at mathematics, and the third at sports. / Jasper missed her and she him.
- **[6.59] A comma may separate adjacent homonyms for readability** — Subject to editorial discretion, separate two identically spelled words with different functions when clarification helps. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Let us march in, in twos. / Whatever is, is good. **but** "It depends on what means means."

## Semicolons (rules 6.60–6.64)

- **[6.60] Use a semicolon between independent clauses not joined by a conjunction to signal a closer link than a period** — In regular prose this is the commonest use. Note: "many fiction authors consider semicolons to be unnatural in dialogue and may also object to their use in narrative; editors should resist adding any in such contexts without consulting the author." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: She spent much of her free time immersed in the ocean; no mere water-resistant watch would do.
  - ex: Though a gifted writer, Miqueas has never bothered to master the semicolon; he insists that half a colon is no colon at all.
- **[6.61] Before a conjunctive adverb joining independent clauses, use a semicolon, not a comma** — *However, thus, hence, indeed, accordingly, besides, therefore* require a semicolon to avoid a comma splice; a comma usually follows the adverb but may be omitted if the sentence reads well without it. The adverb *then* (for *and then*) is often preceded by a comma instead—a "borderline comma splice" common in imperative constructions and acceptable in most contexts, though a semicolon "is strictly correct." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The accuracy of Jesse's watch was never in question; besides, he was an expert at intuiting the time of day . . . (**not** . . . never in question, besides, . . .)
  - ex: The trumpet player developed a painful cold sore; thus the third show was canceled.
  - ex: Touch and hold the icon, then drag it to the trash. / First we went out for shiitake burgers; then we enjoyed vegan sundaes.
- **[6.62] A semicolon may precede *that is*/*for example*/*namely* when it introduces an independent clause** — See 6.54 for examples; see also 6.61. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.63] In formal prose a semicolon may replace the comma before a coordinating conjunction** — Use it for a stronger separation or when the second clause has internal punctuation; a period is another option. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Frobisher had always assured his grandson that the house would be his; yet there was no provision for this bequest in his will.
  - ex: Garrett had insisted on remixing the track; but the engineer's demands for overtime pay, together with the band's reluctance, persuaded him to accept the original mix.
- **[6.64] Use semicolons between items of a series that contain internal punctuation** — "When items in a series themselves contain internal punctuation, separating the items with semicolons can aid clarity"; if ambiguity is unlikely, commas may be used instead. When the sentence continues beyond the series, no additional semicolon is required. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: The membership of the international commission was as follows: France, 4; Germany, 5; Great Britain, 1; Italy, 3; United States, 7.
  - ex: The defendant . . . pleaded that he had recently, on doctor's orders, gone off his medications; that his car—which, incidentally, he had won in the late 1970s on *Let's Make a Deal*—had spontaneously caught fire; and that he had not eaten for several days.
  - ex: Marilynn, Sunita, and Jared, research assistants; Carlos, programming consultant; and Carol, audiovisual editor, provided support and prepared these materials for publication.
  - but (internal commas only, em dashes instead): She decided to buy three watches—an atomic watch for travel within the United States, a solar-powered, water-resistant quartz for international travel, and an expensive self-winding model for special occasions.

## Colons (rules 6.65–6.71)

- **[6.65] Use a colon to introduce an element or series that illustrates or amplifies what precedes it** — Between independent clauses it works like a semicolon but "puts the emphasis on the clause that follows it" and usually conveys "as follows." It may also introduce a series of related sentences in place of a period. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: The watch came with a choice of three bands: stainless steel, plastic, or leather.
  - ex: They even relied on a chronological analogy: Just as the Year II had overshadowed 1789, so the October Revolution had eclipsed that of February.
- **[6.66] Use no more than one space after a colon—and none in ratios, biblical citations, or volume:page citations** — A single space follows a colon; no space at all in constructions such as 2:1 ratios, Genesis 6:13, or a volume and page number with no intervening date or issue. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.67] Lowercase after a colon unless a complete sentence follows** — If what follows is not a complete sentence, lowercase the first word (unless a proper noun). If the colon introduces one or more complete sentences, capitalize the first word—"a departure from previous editions," which capitalized only when more than one complete sentence followed. Capitalize also when a colon introduces dialogue or a complete-sentence quotation or question. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.68] Use a colon after *as follows* and *the following*; do not use one after *namely* or *for example*** — Those latter expressions normally take a comma instead (6.54). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The steps are as follows: First, make grooves for the seeds; second, sprinkle the seeds . . .
  - ex: The following documents are required: a valid driver's license and a certified copy of your birth certificate.
- **[6.69] Use a colon to introduce dialogue, and to introduce a quotation or unquoted question when the introduction is a complete sentence** — In dialogue, speaker names take colons. For quotations introduced by *said/replied/asked/wrote*, a comma is normal (6.43, 6.45); a colon may occasionally replace it for emphasis or to set up a block quotation. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Axel: Somebody's knocking at the door. / Jalen: It's probably just a woodpecker.
  - ex: The author begins by challenging nature itself: "The trees were tall, but I was taller."
  - ex: The question occurred to her at once: What if I can't do this?
- **[6.70] Use a colon after the identification of those addressed in formal communication** — Speeches and formal letters: "Honored Guests:" / "Dear Credit and Collections Manager:". For commas in direct address, see 6.57. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.71] Do not put a colon between a verb and its object or complement** — "Many writers assume—wrongly—that a colon is always needed before a series or a list." If the colon interrupts what would otherwise be a grammatical sentence, it is probably wrong; this holds even when the introduction sits on its own line. Test: "To merit a colon, the words that introduce a series or list must themselves constitute a grammatically complete sentence." Exception: when the verb is elided after an introductory word or phrase, a colon (or dash) is required. **[MECHANICAL detection; JUDGMENT to fix]**
  - ex: The menagerie included cats, pigeons, newts, and deer ticks. **not** The menagerie included: cats, pigeons, newts, and deer ticks.
  - ex (exception): Pros: accuracy and water resistance. Cons: cheap-looking exterior . . .
  - ex (exception): First step: Remove the plastic tab. Second step: Insert the battery . . .

## Question Marks (rules 6.72–6.74)

- **[6.72] Use a question mark for a direct question, for editorial doubt, and (occasionally) for surprise** — It may also mark surprise, disbelief, or uncertainty at the end of a declarative or imperative sentence. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Who will represent the poor? / Thomas Kraftig (1610?–66) was the subject of the final essay. / This is your reply?
- **[6.73] Mark a direct but unquoted question inside a sentence with a question mark; never mark an indirect question** — An indirect question takes no question mark. A one-word question (*why, when, how*) may drop the mark and may be italicized. A polite request disguised as a question "does not always require a question mark"—it can usually be reduced to the imperative. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Is it worth the risk? he wondered. **or** He wondered, Is it worth the risk?
  - ex (indirect): He wondered whether it was worth the risk. / Where to find a reliable clock is the question of the hour.
  - ex: Will the audience please rise. / Would you kindly respond by March 1. **or** Please respond by March 1. **but** Would you mind telling me your age?
- **[6.74] Place a question mark inside quotation marks, parentheses, or brackets only when it belongs to that matter** — A question mark belonging to the enclosing sentence follows the closing mark. A question that is a fragment may be capitalized. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The ambassador asked, "Has the Marine Corps been alerted?" / "Is it worth the risk?" he asked.
  - ex: Why was Farragut trembling when he said, "I'm here to open an inquiry"? (? outside)
  - ex: The man in the gray flannel suit (had we met before?) winked at me.
  - ex: Why did she tell him only on the morning of his departure (March 18)?
  - ex: Will there be music? Dancing?

## Exclamation Points (rules 6.75–6.78)

- **[6.75] Use exclamation points sparingly** — "An exclamation point (which should be used sparingly to be effective) marks an outcry or an emphatic or ironic comment." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Heads up! / We should all be so lucky!
- **[6.76] A question-shaped sentence may be marked rhetorical with an exclamation point—or a period** — "But note that a period can be equally effective in such cases" (see 6.12). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: How could you possibly believe that! / When will I ever learn! / When will I ever learn.
- **[6.77] Do not insert a bracketed exclamation point in quoted matter; it is not *sic*** — Such a mark "risks being interpreted as contemptuous or arrogant. Unless such a sentiment is intended, this device should be avoided. Nor is it a substitute for the Latin expression *sic*," which marks an error in the source that might be mistaken for a transcription error. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.78] Place an exclamation point inside quotation marks, parentheses, or brackets only when it belongs to that matter** — **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The performer walked off the stage amid cries of "Brava!"
  - ex: I can't believe you haven't read "Bartleby, the Scrivener"! (! outside)
  - ex: Alex Ramirez (I could have had a stroke!) repeated the whole story.

## Hyphens and Dashes (rule 6.79)

- **[6.79] Distinguish the hyphen (-), en dash (–), em dash (—), 2-em dash (——), and 3-em dash (———)** — The hyphen, en dash, and em dash are the common ones; though "the differences can sometimes be subtle—especially in the case of an en dash versus a hyphen—correct use of the different types is a sign of editorial precision and care." The 2-em and 3-em dashes are two and three consecutive em dashes; single-glyph Unicode versions exist but "regular em dashes remain more readily available and are therefore preferred." **[MECHANICAL]**

### Hyphens (rules 6.80–6.81)

- **[6.80] Hyphenate compound words and names per the compound rules** — See 5.96 and chapter 7 (especially 7.36–48, 7.87–96); see also 6.86. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.81] Use a hyphen to separate noninclusive numbers and spelled-out letters** — Telephone numbers, Social Security numbers, ISBNs; and letters spelled out one by one in dialogue or in references to fingerspelling. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: 1-800-621-2376 / 978-0-226-15906-5 (ISBN)
  - ex: "My name is Phyllis; that's p-h-y-l-l-i-s." / A proficient signer can fingerspell C-O-L-O-R-A-D-O in less than two seconds.

### En Dashes (rules 6.82–6.90)

- **[6.82] Treat the en dash as a specialized hyphen** — Wherever this manual calls for an en dash in a range or compound term, it "functions as a specialized hyphen"; other style manuals may call for a hyphen there, and authors typically type hyphens, but "editors who follow Chicago style, however, should apply en dashes as described in this section." **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.83] Use an en dash for "up to and including" ranges—but never after *from* or *between*** — With continuing numbers (dates, times, page numbers), the en dash means *to/through*. For parallel construction, write *from 2019 to 2022* and *between 11:30 a.m. and 1:30 p.m.*, never *from 2019–22* or *between 11:30 a.m.–1:30 p.m.* With scores and directions the en dash means simply *to*. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The years 1993–2000 were heady ones for the computer literate. / In Genesis 6:13–21 we find God's instructions to Noah. / Join us on Thursday, 11:30 a.m.–4:00 p.m. . . .
  - ex: She was in college from 2019 to 2022. (**not** from 2019–22) / He usually naps between 11:30 a.m. and 1:30 p.m. (**not** between 11:30 a.m.–1:30 p.m.)
  - ex: The London–Paris train leaves at two o'clock. / Green Bay defeated Chicago, 13–6. / The legislature voted 101–13 . . .
- **[6.84] Use an en dash with no following space for an open-ended range** — For an ongoing range (a serial publication's dates, a living person's birth year): no space intervenes between the en dash and any mark of punctuation that follows. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The History of Cartography* (1987–) is a multivolume work published by Chicago. / Jack Stag (1950–) or Jack Stag (b. 1950)
- **[6.85] Use an en dash between the names of two people used as a compound modifier** — A "small departure from previous editions," which recommended a hyphen; the en dash clarifies that the compound names more than one person. Keep a hyphen when the compound names a single person. For common nouns, keep the hyphen unless the en dash clearly means *to*. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: the Ali–Frazier match / Epstein–Barr virus / the Mason–Dixon line
  - but: Albers-Schönberg disease (named for one person)
  - ex: "left-right asymmetry" (means *to*) but "a north-south street"
- **[6.86] Use an en dash in a compound adjective containing an open compound or two hyphenated compounds** — "Whereas a hyphen joins exactly two words, the en dash is intended to signal a link across more than two," but "because this editorial nicety tends to go unnoticed by most readers, it should be used sparingly, and only when a more elegant solution is unavailable." Join a single word or prefix to a hyphenated compound with a hyphen, not an en dash; treat an abbreviated compound as one word. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: the post–World War II years / Chuck Berry–style lyrics
  - ex: country music–influenced lyrics (or lyrics influenced by country music)
  - ex: a quasi-public–quasi-judicial body (or, better, a quasi-public, quasi-judicial body)
  - ex (hyphen): non-English-speaking peoples / a two-thirds-full cup / post-WWII
- **[6.87] Follow the institution's own usage for campus locations, where an en dash often links campus to university name** — Usage varies widely; when sources disagree, follow the institution. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: the University of Wisconsin–Madison **but** the University of California, Berkeley; the University of Massachusetts Amherst; UMass Amherst
- **[6.88] Break lines after an en dash, never before** — As with hyphens, avoid carrying a single character (as in a number range or score) to the next line; in reflowable formats, let the software decide. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.89] In contemporary British usage a spaced en dash replaces the em dash** — "an en dash (with space before and after) is usually preferred to the em dash as punctuation in running text – like this –"; some non-British publications do the same. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.90] Do not substitute an en dash for a minus sign** — Minus (U+2212) and en dash (U+2013) are distinct characters with possibly different spacing; substituting one for the other "may hinder searches," so use the correct character, especially in mathematical copy. **[MECHANICAL]**

### Em Dashes (rules 6.91–6.98)

- **[6.91] Use em dashes in place of commas, parentheses, or a colon to set off an amplifying or explanatory element—especially for an abrupt break** — "The em dash, often simply called the dash, is the most commonly used and most versatile of the dashes"; it can also replace a period or semicolon. (British usage uses spaced en dashes; see 6.89.) Never nest one em-dash element inside another—"the em dash should never be used within or immediately following another element set off by an em dash (or pair of em dashes); use parentheses or commas instead"—and, except in dialogue (12.44), "there should be no more than two em dashes in a sentence." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: It was a revival of the most potent image in modern democracy—the revolutionary idea.
  - ex: The influence of three impressionists—Monet, Sisley, and Degas—is obvious in her work.
  - ex: The chancellor—he had been awake half the night—came down in an angry mood.
  - ex: She outlined the strategy—a strategy that would, she hoped, secure the peace.
  - ex: My friends—that is, my former friends—ganged up on me.
  - ex (in lieu of period): The number of new cases has been declining—last week's daily average was the lowest since January.
  - ex (no nesting): The Whipplesworth conference—which had already been interrupted by three demonstrations (the last bordering on violence)—was adjourned promptly. **or** . . . three demonstrations, the last bordering on violence—was adjourned promptly.
- **[6.92] An em dash may set off an introductory noun or series of nouns from the pronoun that refers back to it** — "An em dash is occasionally used to set off an introductory noun, or a series of nouns, from a pronoun that refers back to the noun or nouns and introduces the main clause." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Consensus—that was the will-o'-the-wisp he doggedly pursued. / Broken promises, petty rivalries, and false rumors—such were the obstacles she encountered.
- **[6.93] Use an em dash (or a pair) for a sudden break in thought or structure, or an interruption in dialogue** — Where a faltering rather than a sudden break is intended, an ellipsis may be used (6.15). See 12.42. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: "Will he—can he—obtain the necessary signatures?" asked Mill.
  - ex: "Well, I don't know," I began tentatively. "I thought I might—" / "Might what?" she demanded.
- **[6.94] An em dash may precede *that is*, *namely*, *for example*, and similar expressions** — See 6.54 for examples; see also 6.62. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.95] Before an em dash, ? and ! may appear—but never a comma, colon, or semicolon** — A period may precede an em dash when it is part of an abbreviation or when the dash introduces an attribution. Where an em dash replaces a comma before a dependent clause, omit the comma; likewise omit the comma before a speech tag when the dash marks an interruption (6.93, 6.134). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Without further warning—but what could we have done to dissuade her?—she left the plant, determined to stop the union in its tracks.
  - ex: Only if—heaven forbid!—you lose your passport should you call home.
  - ex: No one—at least not before 11:42 p.m.—could have predicted the outcome.
  - ex: "We cannot all succeed when half of us are held back."—Malala Yousafzai
  - ex: Because the data had not been fully analyzed—let alone collated—the publication of the report was delayed.
  - ex: "I assure you, we shall never—" Sylvia began, but Mark cut her short.
- **[6.96] Break lines after an em dash, never before, and never between a dash and a following mark or attribution** — In print, no break belongs between a dash and a closing quotation mark (or any other punctuation) immediately following it, nor between a dash and a name in an attribution; in reflowable formats, let the software decide. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.97] Em dashes may replace quotation marks for dialogue, with each speech starting a new paragraph and no space after the dash** — "—Will he obtain the necessary signatures? / —Of course he will!" **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.98] Em dashes may stand in for bullets in informal vertical lists and for empty table cells** — In informal settings em dashes sometimes work like bullet points (6.141), but "such usage is best avoided in formal prose"; they may organize subentries in an index (15.27) and fill a blank data cell in a table (3.68). **[JUDGMENT]**

### 2-Em and 3-Em Dashes (rules 6.99–6.100)

- **[6.99] Use a 2-em dash for a missing word or part of a word** — Two em dashes represent material omitted to disguise a name or missing/illegible in quoted or reprinted matter. Space on both sides when a whole word is missing; no space between dash and the surviving part of a word; a space follows when the dash ends a word (unless punctuation follows). For expletives, asterisks are "generally more effective" because they show which word is meant. Do not confuse a 2-em dash with a fill-in blank, which is an underscore. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "The region gives its —— to the language spoken there."
  - ex: Admiral N—— and Lady R—— were among the guests. / David H——h [Hirsch?] voted aye.
  - ex: "I don't give a f——" **or, more precisely,** "I don't give a f\*\*k."
- **[6.100] Do not use a 3-em dash in bibliographies; repeat the author's name** — The 3-em dash traditionally stood for the same author as the preceding entry (followed by a period, or a comma before *ed.*), but "because 3-em dashes obscure important information and pose an obstacle to accessibility, this manual now recommends repeating the name(s) instead" (see 13.72–73, 13.113). **[MECHANICAL]**

## Parentheses (rules 6.101–6.104)

- **[6.101] Use parentheses to set off material from the surrounding text—stronger than a comma, similar to a dash** — Unlike commas (but like dashes), parentheses can set off text "that has no grammatical relationship to the rest of the sentence." In British English they are "round brackets." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Our final sample (collected under difficult conditions) contained an impurity.
  - ex: Wexford's analysis (see chap. 3) is more to the point.
  - ex: The disagreement between Johns and Evans (its origins have been discussed elsewhere) ultimately destroyed the organization.
- **[6.102] Use parentheses for glosses and translations; use square brackets inside quoted matter** — Parentheses enclose glosses of unfamiliar terms or translations, or the original term when the English is given. In quoted matter, use square brackets (6.105). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: A drop folio (a page number printed at the foot of a page) is useful on the opening page of a chapter.
  - ex: The term you should use for 1,000,000,000 is *mil millones* (billion), not *billón* (trillion).
- **[6.103] Avoid parentheses within parentheses; Chicago prefers square brackets inside** — Parentheses in parentheses are permitted in some fields (especially law), but Chicago prefers brackets within parentheses (6.107). British style uses parentheses within parentheses. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.104] Punctuate parentheses correctly and avoid more than one sentence inside another sentence** — Only in an enumeration may an opening parenthesis be preceded by a comma or semicolon; a closing parenthesis is never preceded by a comma, semicolon, or colon. A ? or ! and closing quotation marks go inside if they belong to the parenthetical matter, outside if they belong to the sentence; a period goes inside only when the whole sentence is parenthetical. Parentheses may follow back-to-back if the material is unrelated, though a single set separated by a semicolon is often better. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: Having entered (on tiptoe), we sat down on the nearest seats we could find.
  - ex: Come on in (quietly, please!) and take a seat.
  - ex: Five new watches were on display. (Shellahan fancied the battery-powered quartz model.)

## Brackets and Braces (rules 6.105–6.111)

- **[6.105] Use square brackets for editorial insertions, explanations, translations, and corrections in quoted matter** — Brackets enclose material added by someone other than the original writer; sometimes the bracketed material replaces the original words. If the quoted matter already contains brackets, say so in the source citation ("brackets in the original"). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "They [the free-silver Democrats] asserted that the ratio could be maintained."
  - ex: Satire, Jebb tells us, "is the only [form] that has a continuous development."
  - ex: [This was written before the discovery of the Driscoll manuscript.—Ed.]
- **[6.106] In a translation, brackets may enclose the original-language term—use sparingly** — "The differences between society [Gesellschaft] and community [Gemeinschaft] will now be analyzed." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.107] Use square brackets as parentheses within parentheses, usually for bibliographic material** — "(For further discussion see Richardson's excellent analysis [1999] and Danneberger's survey [2000].)" **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.108] Order mathematical delimiters {[( )]}** — Braces outermost, then brackets, then parentheses; displayed expressions may extend the sequence with large parentheses, brackets, and braces in that order. In running text braces are sometimes omitted. Mathematical angle brackets and vertical bars are not substitutes for this sequence. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.109] Use square brackets for phonetic transcriptions (IPA)** — The verb *entretenir* [ɑ̃tʁətnir], like *keep*, is used in many idioms. For the parallel use of slashes, see 6.119. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.110] Apply the same punctuation principles to brackets as to parentheses, but treat editorial insertions as if parenthesized** — Where a bracketed addition supplies missing wording, other punctuation may belong to the source alone; in most cases, "material added in brackets should be treated as if it were in parentheses." Do not insert a comma that belongs to the sentence inside the brackets. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: "The scholars fled Ithaca [New York] and drove south." **not** "The scholars fled Ithaca[, New York,] and drove south."
- **[6.111] Keep angle brackets and braces out of regular prose** — Angle brackets (< >) are mainly for XML tags and typesetting instructions; "although angle brackets are sometimes used to set off URLs and email addresses . . . this practice is unnecessary in regular prose." Braces { } are for programming and mathematical/specialized writing: "Braces are not interchangeable with parentheses or brackets." **[MECHANICAL]**

## Slashes (rules 6.112–6.121)

- **[6.112] Know the slash's synonyms** — Slash (/), also *virgule*, *solidus*, *slant*, *forward slash*; distinguish it from the backslash (\\). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.113] Use the slash for alternatives—sparingly and with judgment about spacing** — It is "a convenient (if somewhat informal) shorthand for *or*," also used for alternative spellings or names. When either side is an open compound, a space before and after the slash aids legibility (may be omitted where space is tight, as in tables). It can also blend *and* with *or*—but in most such cases a hyphen is better. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: my/your / and/or / Hercules/Heracles / Margaret/Meg/Maggie / World War I / First World War
  - ex: an insertion/deletion mutation / an MD/PhD program / 24/7 (or *twenty-four seven*)
  - ex (hyphen better): a mother-daughter friendship
- **[6.114] Use a slash for a two-year span** — "The winter of 1966/67 was especially severe." / "Enrollment increased between 2020/21 and 2023/24." / "The fiscal years 2005/6–2009/10 were encouraging . . ." **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.115] Avoid all-numeral dates in formal writing** — Slashes (or periods or hyphens) in dates such as 3/10/02 are informal and ambiguous (US month-first vs day-first); if an all-numeral form is required, use ISO YYYY-MM-DD. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.116] Use a slash for *per* and in place of periods in some abbreviations** — "110 km/s," "$450/week"; *c/o* (in care of), *n/a* (not applicable). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.117] Use a slash for "divided by" when a fraction bar is impractical; prefer single-glyph fractions** — Use ½ rather than 1/2 when the glyph is available. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.118] Show line breaks in quoted poetry with a spaced slash** — When two or more lines of poetry are quoted in regular text, use slashes with space on each side (see 12.29). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Thou hast not missed one thought that could be fit, / And all that was improper dost omit."
- **[6.119] Slashes may enclose a phonemic transcription (as brackets do for phonetic ones)** — Some authors distinguish brackets (exact phonetic detail) from slashes (general phonemic transcription): "The /p/ in /pet/ is not quite the same sound as the /p/ in /spɛʃəl/." **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.120] Never put spaces in URLs or file paths; keep trailing slashes** — Slashes separate directories and file names; no spaces. In fixed-layout publications, break lines before such a slash, never between two slashes. A URL ending in a file name has no trailing slash; a copied directory URL may—"there is no need to delete it," but if deleted, recheck the link. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.121] Break after a slash, not before, except in URLs** — For alternative terms, break after the slash; avoid carrying a single character over (as in a two-year span); never break a fraction; a poetry-line slash may break either side; for URLs break before a slash (after a double slash). In reflowable formats let the software decide. **[MECHANICAL]**

## Quotation Marks (rules 6.122–6.123)

- **[6.122] Place quotation marks according to 6.9–6.11** — For closing-mark placement see 6.9–11; with comma 6.43; colon 6.69; question mark 6.74; exclamation point 6.78. Dialogue and quoted matter: chapter 12. Special usage of quoted words: 7.60, 7.63, 7.66. Titles: chapter 8. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.123] Use directional ("smart"/curly) quotation marks, never the default straight mark or grave accent** — Published prose must use the correct left or right mark in every instance. "All software includes a default quotation mark ("); in published prose this unidirectional mark, though far more portable than typographer's marks, signals a lack of typographical sophistication." Neither the default apostrophe (') nor the grave accent (`) may be used in their place. **[MECHANICAL]**

## Apostrophes (rules 6.124–6.126)

- **[6.124] The apostrophe has three uses** — Possessive case, standing in for missing letters or numerals, and (rarely) forming the plural of certain expressions. See 5.22, 7.16–29 (possessives), 7.30 (contractions), 7.5–15 (plurals). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.125] Use the directional apostrophe (U+2019), not the straight mark and never the left single quote** — "Owing to the limitations of conventional keyboards and many software programs, the apostrophe continues to be one of the most abused marks in punctuation." Two pitfalls: the default straight mark, and the left single quotation mark—"the latter usage in particular should always be construed as an error." Watch for software that auto-converts an apostrophe at the start of a word. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: We spent the ’90s (**not** ‘90s) in thrall to our gadgets.
  - ex: Where’d you get ’em (**not** ‘em)? / I love rock ’n’ roll (**not** rock ‘n’ roll).
- **[6.126] An apostrophe is part of the word: put no period or comma between the word and a final apostrophe** — Do not confuse it with a closing single quotation mark. "The last car in the lot was the Smiths'." **[MECHANICAL]**

## Spaces (rules 6.127–6.130)

- **[6.127] The space is a separator, not punctuation** — One space between words and between sentences (6.7), and between a numeral and a unit of measure (10.55); widths vary (6.128) and nonbreaking spaces control line breaks (6.129). See Table 6.2. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.128] Use fixed-width spaces only for typeset (print/PDF) matter** — Beyond the word space, there are fixed-width spaces (em, en, three-per-em, four-per-em, six-per-em, thin, hair, figure, punctuation, zero width), each defined in Unicode (Table 6.2); they are applied by a professional typesetter, and in HTML they "should be used with caution, if at all." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - Table 6.2 highlights: space U+0020; no-break space U+00A0; narrow no-break space U+202F; em space U+2003; en space U+2002; thin space U+2009; hair space U+200A; zero width space U+200B; figure space U+2007; punctuation space U+2008.
- **[6.129] Use nonbreaking spaces to keep related elements on one line** — Use them for spaced ellipses, numerals that use spaces rather than commas as separators, a numeral and its abbreviated unit (11.5 km), and initials (E. B. White). Authors may apply them, but they are "generally the responsibility of either the manuscript editor or the publisher." **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.130] Set spaces around binary operation and relation signs; none in sub/superscripts or when the sign is used adjectivally** — A space precedes and follows +, −, =, <, > (as if they were words), and around summation/integral/union symbols; no space in a subscript or superscript, and none when the sign modifies a number or symbol. **[MECHANICAL]**

## Multiple Punctuation Marks (rules 6.131–6.137)

- **[6.131] Multiple marks almost always involve quotes, dashes, parentheses, or brackets plus a period, comma, colon, semicolon, ?, or !** — For quotes see 6.9–11, 6.43, 6.69, 6.74, 6.78; em dashes 6.95, 6.100; parentheses/brackets 6.91, 6.103, 6.104, 6.107, 6.110, 6.140; ellipses 12.59–69. See Table 6.1. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[6.132] Do not double a period at sentence end, but keep both periods and a following mark when needed** — If an abbreviation ending in a period ends a sentence, no second period; but when another mark is required immediately after that period (as after *et al.*), both the period and the additional mark appear. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: The study was funded by Mulvehill & Co. (single period)
  - ex: Johnson et al., in *How to Survive*, describe such an ordeal.
- **[6.133] A period never accompanies a question mark or exclamation point** — "The latter two marks, being stronger, take precedence over the period," even when they are part of a title. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Their first question was a hard one: "Who is willing to trade oil for water?"
  - ex: We ended up watching *What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?*
- **[6.134] Omit the comma after a quotation ending in ? or !—but keep it after a title or a non-dialogue quotation** — A comma that would normally follow is dropped in quoted speech/dialogue; it may be retained (1) after the title of a work ending in ? or ! and (2) after a quotation not presented as speech or dialogue (an exception "new to this edition"). If the result is awkward, reword. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Are you a doctor?" asked Mahmoud. (no comma)
  - ex: "Are You a Doctor?," the fifth story in *Will You Please Be Quiet, Please?*, treats modern love.
  - ex: All the band's soundtrack albums—*A Hard Day's Night*, *Help!*, *Yellow Submarine*, and *Magical Mystery Tour*—were popular.
  - ex: The third Smart Reply option, "Yes, please!," was clearly the best one.
- **[6.135] When a title or quotation ends in ? or !, add the sentence's own mark only if it differs—and never double the same mark** — "In the rare case of a question or exclamation ending with a title or quotation that ends in a question mark or exclamation point, include both marks only if they are different and the sentence punctuation seems essential"; when they are the same, retain only the first. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Have you seen *Help!*? / Who shouted, "Long live the king!"?
  - ex: I just love *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?!*
  - but: Who starred opposite Richard Burton in *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf*? (**not** Woolf??)
  - A "?!" or "!?" pairing for excitement or disbelief "is best avoided in formal prose."
- **[6.136] When a question introduces a list, the question mark takes precedence over a colon** — A colon may be kept only if the list is presented as part of a sentence. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Which of the following colors do you like best? / red / blue / yellow
  - ex: Which color do you like best: red, blue, or yellow?
- **[6.137] Treat a single emoji like a word: space around it except before following punctuation or after an opening mark or dash** — An emoji applying to the whole sentence follows the final punctuation (usually with a space); no space between consecutive emojis. Usage varies widely, so "editors should query before making any wholesale changes." (*emoji* is the strictly correct plural, but *emojis* is now more common.) Emoticons may be treated similarly. **[JUDGMENT]**

## Lists and Outline Style (rules 6.138–6.143)

- **[6.138] Make list items parallel; use numerals only when they serve a purpose** — "Items in a list should consist of parallel elements." Omit introductory numerals/letters unless they indicate order, chronology, relative importance, facilitate text references, or separate items in a run-in list. "Where similar lists are fairly close together, consistent treatment is essential." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.139] Run in short, simple lists; set long or multilevel lists vertically** — Short lists are usually better run in, "especially if the introductory text and the items in the list together form a sentence"; lists needing typographic prominence, length, or multiple levels should be vertical. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[6.140] In run-in lists, parenthesize numerals or letters; use a colon only after a complete sentence; separate items with commas (or semicolons if items contain commas)** — If letters are used they are sometimes italicized within roman parentheses. When each item is a complete sentence or several sentences, set the list vertically. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Compose three sentences to illustrate analogous uses of (1) commas, (2) em dashes, and (3) parentheses.
  - ex: For the duration of the experiment, the dieters were instructed to avoid (a) meat, (b) bottled drinks, (c) packaged foods, and (d) nicotine.
  - ex (semicolons): You are advised to pack the following items: (a) warm, sturdy outer clothing and enough underwear to last ten days; (b) two pairs of boots, two pairs of sneakers, and plenty of socks; and (c) three durable paperback novels.
- **[6.141] Format vertical lists consistently: complete-sentence intro plus colon; no end punctuation on fragment items; numerals take periods, letters take parentheses** — Unordered lists: fragment items get no end punctuation and usually begin lowercase (unless proper nouns), though capitalization may be chosen for prominence—"choose one approach and follow it consistently." Ordered lists: Arabic numerals take a period, letters are enclosed in parentheses, items are customarily capitalized even if not sentences, and closing punctuation is used only if items are complete sentences. Runover lines get a hanging indent aligned with the first word after the numeral; short items may be set in two columns to avoid "long, skinny lists." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (unordered): Your application must include the following documents: / a full résumé / three letters of recommendation / all your diplomas . . .
  - ex (ordered): Compose three sentences: / 1. To illustrate the use of commas in dates / 2. To distinguish the use of semicolons from the use of periods / 3. To illustrate the use of parentheses within dashes
  - When items are very long or multiple sentences and the list needs no prominence, set them as ordinary numbered paragraphs punctuated as prose. If a list mixes complete and incomplete sentences, recast them to be consistent; if impossible, give each item a final mark.
- **[6.142] A vertical list that completes a sentence uses semicolons or commas between items and a period at the end, with each item lowercase** — "If the items in a vertical list complete a sentence begun in the introductory text, semicolons or commas may be used between the items, and a period should follow the final item." Semicolons are preferred when items contain internal punctuation. Each item begins lowercase, even in a numbered list; a conjunction before the final item is optional. Such lists are often better run in. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Reporting for the Development Committee, Jobson reported that / 1. a fundraising campaign director was being sought; / 2. the salary for this director, about $150,000 a year, would be paid out of campaign funds; and / 3. the fundraising campaign would be launched in the spring of 2024.
- **[6.143] In outlines, distinguish levels by punctuation as well as indentation, and give every division at least two items** — Multilevel lists may use numerals and letters; runover lines align with the first word after the marker. Top three levels take periods (I., A., 1.); the lower four take single or double parentheses—a), (1), (a), i)—"thus distinguishing all seven levels by punctuation as well as indentation." Multi-digit numerals align on the last digit. "Each division and subdivision should normally contain at least two items," and readers should see the level at a glance. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: I. Historical introduction / II. Dentition in various groups of vertebrates / A. Reptilia / 1. Histology and development of reptilian teeth / a) Primates / (1) Lemuroidea / (a) Platyrrhini / i) Cercopithecidae
