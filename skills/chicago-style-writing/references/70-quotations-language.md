# Quotations, dialogue, and languages other than English

Source: CMOS ch 12 (rules 12.1-12.81), ch 11 (rules 11.1-11.145). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch12, ch11. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch12

1. **Quote exactly; change only the wrapper** (12.7, 12.8): marks, dash length, initial case, terminal
   mark, note refs, typography. Anything a reader could mistake for the source's words must be
   disclosed.
2. **Integrate the host sentence so it reads as if there were no quotation marks** (12.11); quote only
   as much as needed.
3. **No comma before a quotation that is a syntactical part of the sentence** (12.15); comma after
   *said*/*replied* only when the tag identifies the speaker (12.14); colon after an independent clause
   (12.16).
4. **Lowercase the first quoted word when the quotation is grammatical within the sentence; keep the
   capital when it stands apart** (12.19, 12.20). Adjust tense and pronouns to the new context (12.12).
5. **Comma (or other mark) at the end of a quotation that precedes its tag** — omit if the quotation
   ends in ? or ! (12.14, 6.9–11).
6. **American style: double marks first, single inside** (12.30); commas and periods inside the closing
   mark, ? and ! inside only if they belong to the quoted matter (12.30, 12.79, 12.80).
7. **In dialogue, a new paragraph per speaker** (12.40), and **quoting within dialogue takes single
   marks even for a whole line** (12.46).
8. **Speaker tags: verbs that can describe speech only** — *said, asked, replied, answered*; never
   *she frowned* (12.41). Action alone starts a new sentence.
9. **Interruption = em dash, inside the marks for the interrupted speaker, outside when the narrator
   breaks in** (12.42); **faltering = ellipsis, with no period and no comma before the closing mark in
   fiction** (12.43).
10. **Multi-paragraph quotation → block** (12.10, 12.22, 12.32); first quoted paragraph **flush left**;
    later paragraphs indented; **no quotation marks on the block** (12.9, 12.31).
11. **≥100 words can go to block; two or more lines of verse are best set off; three or more lines in a
    note** (12.10, 12.25).
12. **Run-in verse uses a spaced slash between lines and a spaced double slash between stanzas** (12.29,
    12.33); runovers indent one em (12.27).
13. **Ellipsis = three spaced periods, never four unless a period precedes for a sentence break**
    (12.59, 12.62); **never at the start or end of a quotation** unless the quoted sentence is
    deliberately incomplete (12.61, 12.64).
14. **Elision must never create a statement alien to the original**, and must not join statements far
    apart in the source (12.60).
15. **Brackets, never parentheses, for clarifications** (12.71, 12.52); keep them minimal and never let
    them change meaning.
16. **Silently correct obvious typographic errors; preserve archaic/unpublished idiosyncrasy; disclose
    modernization** (12.7 item 6).
17. **Sic only for an error a reader would blame on the transcriber**, and only when paraphrase or
    silent correction won't do (12.72); note unconventional spellings instead.
18. **Italics you add must be labeled** — "emphasis added," "italics mine" — and original italics noted
    as "italics in the original," consistently (12.73).
19. **Numbers may be spelled out in speech/dialogue** (years, times, addresses, brands stay numerals)
    — pick the form most recognizable to a reader (12.51).
20. **Treat speech as speech**: indirect discourse takes no marks (12.53); single words like *yes* and
    *why* take marks only as dialogue (12.47); unspoken thought may be italicized and, if it starts
    mid-sentence, capitalized (12.49).
21. **Epigraphs and blurbs carry no quotation marks; never add exclamation points or faithfulness
    brackets to a blurb** (12.35, 12.38).
22. **Drama: names in caps, no quotation marks, hanging indents, italic stage directions** (12.54);
    interview interjections italic in brackets (12.56).
23. **Attribute in text with a parenthetical at the punctuation boundary** — period after the closing
    parenthesis when the quotation is a question (12.79, 12.80); block: no period before or after the
    closing parenthesis (12.81).
24. **Never fabricate or silently tidy a quotation's sense; check against the original before
    delivering** (12.6, 12.60).

### From ch11

1. **Italicize an unfamiliar foreign word or phrase on first use, then roman it** by default if it
   recurs; keep italics if it appears only rarely (11.3). Never italicize whole foreign sentences
   (11.3).
2. **Never italicize foreign proper nouns** — people, places, institutions, brands, monuments — even on
   first mention (11.5). Only titles of works get title treatment (11.8–11.12).
3. **Preserve every diacritic, ligature, and non-Latin letter** (11.23, 11.40, 11.48, 11.60, 11.76,
   11.132). ASCII-folding *français*, *Brücke*, *Español*, *Søren*, *Dvořák*, or *Ælfric* is an error,
   not a simplification. **[MECHANICAL]**
4. **Keep the source language's capitalization, not English's** — German nouns, French lowercase
   adjectives, Dutch *IJ*, Russian lowercase months (11.3, 11.42, 11.28, 11.77, 11.108).
   **[MECHANICAL]**
5. **Form the plural in the source language** (*Blumen*, not *Blumes*) (11.3). **[MECHANICAL]**
6. **In fiction and dialogue, default to roman for code-switching** and do not "correct" an author's
   consistent choice; italics could "make the dialogue seem less realistic" (11.4). **[JUDGMENT]**
7. **Capitalize foreign titles in sentence case, in text and notes alike** (11.8). **[MECHANICAL]**
8. **Change only what 11.9 permits in a foreign title's punctuation** — period/semicolon to colon, native
   quotes to English quotes, delete a space before punctuation, capitalize the subtitle's first word.
   **Do not insert or delete commas** (11.9). **[MECHANICAL]**
9. **Gloss translations in parentheses or quotation marks, one set only**, and keep a glossed common
   noun italic inside the parentheses (11.6, 11.11, 11.14). **[MECHANICAL]**
10. **Flag a modified published translation with "translation modified" plus square brackets**; credit
    translators; never retranslate from a translation — paraphrase if the original is unavailable
    (11.16–11.19).
11. **Keep the source language's quotation and dialogue system when you quote it**: guillemets,
    inverted *¿ ¡*, em-dash dialogue in French/Italian/Spanish/Russian block quotations, Greek-alphabet
    quotations with no quotation marks at all (11.13, 11.21, 11.34, 11.54, 11.69, 11.71, 11.110, 11.126).
12. **Omit Greek accents in transliteration but keep macrons for *ē* and *ō***; render rough breathing
    as *h* (11.120). **[MECHANICAL]**
13. **Use Pinyin for Chinese, modified Hepburn for Japanese, Revised Romanization (or declared MR) for
    Korean** — and never mix systems within a document (11.89, 11.93, 11.95–96).
14. **Convert Wade-Giles/Postal Atlas names to Pinyin** except names long familiar in their older form
    (*Sun Yat-sen*, *Chiang Kai-shek*) (11.90, 11.92). **[MECHANICAL]**
15. **Hyphenate the Arabic article *al-*** and keep *hamza* and *‘ayn* distinct on every occurrence
    (11.84, 11.86). **[MECHANICAL]**
16. **Prefer the familiar English form for famous non-English names and places** — *Avicenna*, *Mecca*,
    *Damascus*, *Livorno* in an Italian context (11.5, 11.85). **[JUDGMENT]**
17. **Minimize transliteration diacritics for general readers** — *Shiva*, *Vishnu*, *apsara* — but do
    not add or drop diacritics inconsistently within one work (11.81, 11.118).
18. **Italicize transliterated terms that aren't English, but not transliterated names** (11.82).
19. **Defer to Indigenous communities and their language authorities** on spelling, capitalization, and
    naming; some orthographies have no capitals; treat *Elder*, *Longhouse*, *Oral Tradition* as
    capitalized (11.49–11.52). **[JUDGMENT]**
20. **Keep *ß*, *ð*, *þ*, *ȝ*, *æ*, *ł*, *ñ*, *ø*, *đ*, *ș/ț* as characters, not digraph approximations**
    (11.48, 11.77, 11.132). **[MECHANICAL]**
21. **Do not proofread a script you cannot read** — query rather than "fix" it; similar characters are
    easily confused (11.80). **[JUDGMENT]**
22. **Do not attempt to transliterate a language you don't know**, and don't invent a romanization for a
    name that has a standard dictionary form (11.78, 11.107).
23. **Tag foreign-language runs for accessibility** (`lang="es"`) or at least highlight them for the
    publisher (11.7). **[MECHANICAL]**
24. **Set ASL glosses in small caps (hyphenated when several words gloss one sign) and give the English
    translation in roman italics and/or quotation marks** (*VEHICLE-DRIVE-BY*) (11.138–143).
    **[MECHANICAL]**
25. **When in doubt between sentence case and title case for a foreign proper noun or title, choose
    sentence case** (11.5, 11.29, 11.61). **[JUDGMENT]**

## Overview (12.1–12.6) — from PDF 754–755, context only

- **[12.1] Scope and cross-references** — This chapter governs quoting words from other sources and
  presenting speech/dialogue; quotation marks for other purposes are in 7.49–86, titles in 8.157–204,
  citations in chs. 13–14, manuscript block quotes in 2.23–2.24, non-English quotation in 11.13–19.
- **[12.2] Quotations and modern scholarship** — Quoting well is a scholarly/ethical act:
  > "The choice between quoting, on the one hand, and merely copying, on the other, can mean the
  > difference between properly acknowledging and crediting the ideas of others and falsely
  > representing them as your own."
- **[12.3] Giving credit and seeking permission** — Always credit the source of quoted *or paraphrased*
  words and ideas (note, parenthetical, or citation). Written permission may be needed for more than a
  line or two of a poem/song lyric in copyright, prose quotes of more than ~three paragraphs, many short
  passages, or unpublished letters/email/interviews (see 4.79–98).
- **[12.4] Paraphrase rather than quote** — Decide first whether quotation or paraphrase is more
  effective; **prefer paraphrase when the source contains an error** you would otherwise have to
  reproduce, and at the author's discretion to avoid outdated or offensive language.
  > "Too many quotations with too little commentary can pose a distraction, and readers may choose to
  > skip over long or frequent quotations."
- **[12.5] When quotation and attribution are unnecessary** — Common knowledge, readily verifiable
  facts, proverbs, and familiar expressions need no quotation marks or attribution *unless the wording
  is taken directly*. Prose invented or lifted word-for-word still needs marks + source.
  - ex: *Until July 20, 1969, no one had set foot on the moon.* / *The chemical symbol for gold, Au,
    derives from the Latin word aurum.* / *No one can convince the young that practice makes perfect.*
    / *If reading maketh a full man, Henry is half empty.* — none quoted; but *It was Shakespeare's
    contemporary Francis Bacon who wrote that "reading maketh a ful man, conference a ready man, and
    writing an exact man"* — marks + source.
- **[12.6] Ensuring accuracy** — Check every direct quotation against the original (or a careful
  transcription) **before submission**; editors spot-check and query but are not assumed to verify.
  > "It is impossible to overemphasize the importance of meticulous accuracy in quoting from the works
  > of others."

## Permissible Changes to Quotations (12.7–12.8)

- **[12.7] Permissible changes to punctuation, capitalization, and spelling** — Reproduce wording
  exactly, but these six changes may be made silently to fit the quotation to surrounding syntax and
  typography: **[JUDGMENT]** on which ones apply, **[MECHANICAL]** once decided.
  1. **Quote marks** — change single to double and double to single, adjusting punctuation relative to
     the marks (12.30, 6.9); impose "smart" quotes/apostrophes (6.123, 6.125); convert guillemets and
     other non-English marks to regular double or single marks (11.13). **[MECHANICAL]**
  2. **Dashes** — change en dashes or hyphens used as em dashes into em dashes, deleting the spaces
     around them (6.91–98); change hyphens to en dashes in number ranges and other en-dash contexts
     (6.82–90). **[MECHANICAL]**
  3. **Initial letter** — change a capital to lowercase or vice versa to conform to the syntax of the
     surrounding text (12.18–21). **[MECHANICAL]** (judgment about *whether* syntax demands it)
  4. **Final punctuation** — a period or other mark at the end of the quotation may be omitted or
     changed to a period or comma as the surrounding text requires; a **question mark or exclamation
     point may be retained if it still applies** to the words as quoted (ellipses: 12.59–69).
     **[MECHANICAL]**
  5. **Note references** — original note reference marks (and their notes) may be omitted *unless
     omission would affect meaning*; if retained, best set the passage as a block quotation with the
     note in smaller type at the end, or summarize the note in text. Added note references must be
     distinguishable from the original's (13.54). **Parenthetical text references in the original are
     retained**; an *added* parenthetical reference must go in square brackets (12.70–74).
  6. **Obvious typographic errors** — correct silently, without comment or *sic* — **unless** the
     source is an older work, a manuscript, or another unpublished source, where spelling
     idiosyncrasies are generally preserved. If spelling, capitalization, or punctuation is modernized
     or altered for clarity, **readers must be told** in a note, preface, or elsewhere.
  - Archaic **long s** (ſ, U+017F) may be changed to modern *s* ("Such goodneſs of your juſtice, that
    our ſoul . . ."); a title like *Vanitie and Vncertaintie* may be read as *Vanitie and
    Uncertaintie* — but editors without classical/Renaissance background should **be wary of changing
    u→v or i→j** (see 11.63, 11.132–34).
- **[12.8] Permissible changes to typography and layout** — These five may be made to assimilate a
  quotation to surrounding text: (1) **typeface/font** conformed to the text; (2) words in **full
  capitals** reset in small caps if that is house style (10.9); (3) in drama/dialogue, **speaker names
  moved from centered to flush left**; (4) **underlining in a quoted manuscript printed as italics**,
  unless the underlining is integral to the source; (5) in **correspondence**, paragraph indents and
  the position of salutation and signature adjusted.

> **Principle:** reproduce the meaning and wording exactly; adjust only the *wrapper* (marks, dashes,
> case of the first letter, terminal mark, typography) — and disclose any change that a reader could
> mistake for the author's own words.

## Quotations in Relation to Text

### Run In or Set Off (12.9–12.10)

- **[12.9] Run-in and block quotations defined** — A quotation is either **run in** and enclosed in
  quotation marks ("like this") or **set off as a block** ("extract"): block quotations **take no
  quotation marks**, always start on a new line, and are distinguished by indentation (left, sometimes
  right) or smaller/different type. Design/format is the publisher's call; in a manuscript, authors
  simply indent block quotations from the left margin (2.23, 2.24). Fictional dialogue is usually run
  in (sometimes without quotation marks) (12.39–53). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[12.10] Choosing between run-in and block** — Length is the usual criterion: short quotations,
  especially fragments, are run in; **a hundred words or more can generally be set off**. Set off
  instead when: the quotation runs **two or more paragraphs** (12.22–24), it is **correspondence**
  including salutation/signature, it is a **list** (2.25), or it needs special formatting. Consistency
  rules: many varied quotations close together read better all run in; quotations being **compared or
  treated as entities** are better all set off, however short. **[JUDGMENT]**

### Assimilation into the Surrounding Text (12.11–12.12)

- **[12.11] Logical and grammatical assimilation** — Build the host sentence so the quoted words fit
  "logically and grammatically—as if there were no quotation marks," and **quote only as much as is
  necessary**. **[JUDGMENT]**
  > "In incorporating fragmentary quotations into a text, phrase the surrounding sentence in such a
  > way that the quoted words fit into it logically and grammatically—as if there were no quotation
  > marks."
  - ex: *The narrator's constant references to "malicious code and obsolete data" detract from a more
    fundamental issue—that we are dumping "the burden of human history" onto computer hard drives. It
    is this vision of the future that is most alarming: "If (when?) we run out of sources of
    electricity," she asks, "will we forget who we are?"*
- **[12.12] Integrating tenses and pronouns** — When quoting verbatim, shift tense and pronouns to fit
  the new context — silently if possible, with brackets only for occasional adjustments (used
  **sparingly**, 12.71). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (original): *Mr. Moll took particular pains to say to you, gentlemen, that these eleven people
    here are guilty of murder; he calls this a cold-blooded, deliberate and premeditated murder.*
  - ex (as quoted): *According to Darrow, Moll had told the jury that the eleven defendants were
    "guilty of murder" and had described the murder as "cold-blooded, deliberate and premeditated."*
  - ex: *Roosevelt was frank about her own activism, writing as late as 1952 that she'd spent "many
    years of [her] life in opposition."*

### Quotations and Punctuation (12.13–12.17)

- **[12.13] Punctuation relative to closing quotation marks** — Periods, commas, and other marks go
  according to **6.9–11** (American style: commas and periods inside; see *Quotation Marks* below);
  the rest of this section concerns punctuation *before* quoted material.
- **[12.14] Comma to introduce a quotation** — After *said, replied, asked, wrote*, and similar verbs
  used simply to identify a speaker or writer, **use a comma**. This is "more traditional than
  logical," recognizing the syntactic independence of the quoted matter. A colon is "never wrong" here
  but **should be used sparingly** (12.16). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Garrett replied, "I hope you are not referring to me."*
  - ex: *Fish writes, "What [the students] did was move the words out of a context (the faculty club
    door) in which they had a literal and obvious meaning into another context (my classroom) in which
    the meaning was no less obvious and literal and yet was different."*
  - **Inverted (quotation first):** comma at the end of the quotation, unless it ends in ? or !.
    - ex: *"I hope you are not referring to me," Garrett replied.*
  - **Interrupted:** two commas.
    - ex: *"I hope," Garrett replied, "you are not referring to me."*
  - **Subordinated/integrated:** no comma.
    - ex: *Fish observed that "what [the students] did was . . ."* (see 6.43, 6.45)
- **[12.15] No comma to introduce a quotation** — When a mid-sentence quotation forms a **syntactical
  part** of the sentence, use **no** mark of punctuation to introduce it (other punctuation may still
  be required for other reasons). Writers who comma every quotation are wrong. **[MECHANICAL]** +
  **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Donovan made a slight bow and said he was "very glad."*
  - ex: *One of the protesters scrawled "Long live opera!" in huge red letters.*
  - ex: *According to one observer, Miles Davis's recordings were "a central contribution to the idea
    of jazz as the main source of innovation in mid-twentieth-century American music."*
  - ex: *She said she would "prefer not to comment."*
  - but (dash pair, no comma needed): *Miles Davis's recordings—"a central contribution to the idea of
    jazz as the main source of innovation in mid-twentieth-century American music"—have never gone out
    of style.*
  - but (parenthetical interruption takes commas): *She said that she would, in short, "prefer not to
    comment."*
  - but (comma required by 12.14): *She said, "I prefer not to comment."*
- **[12.16] Colon to introduce a quotation** — When the quotation is introduced by an **independent
  clause** (a grammatically complete sentence), **use a colon** — often after *the following* / *as
  follows* (6.68) (exception: 12.17). A colon **may also replace the comma**, is never wrong, but must
  be used **consistently** (e.g., for quotations of more than one sentence, or for emphasis).
  **[MECHANICAL]** + **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *The role of the author has been variously described. Henry Fielding, at the beginning of his
    History of Tom Jones, defines it as follows: "An author ought to consider himself, not as a
    gentleman who gives a private or eleemosynary treat, but rather as one who keeps a public
    ordinary, at which all persons are welcome for their money."*
  - ex: *Faraday's conclusion was alarming: "Without significant intervention, your home town will
    have gone the way of Atlantis by century's end."*
  - ex: *Garrett replied: "I hope you are not referring to me. Because if you are . . ."*
- **[12.17] Period rather than colon before a block quotation** — Unless the lead-in ends with *as
  follows* or similar (6.68), a block quotation may be preceded by a **period**, not a colon — applied
  **consistently** (12.22–24). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *A paradigmatic case is "crunch time"—the days and hours before a game, a new level, or an
    important update is released.* [block] *When crunch time is called, people bring their sleeping
    bags and stay for five days to finish the release. You cannot leave the office; you cannot go
    home. We order pizza and work all the time. If there are people who want to go home and look after
    their kids, they face negative sentiments.*

### Initial Capital or Lowercase Letter (12.18–12.21)

- **[12.18] Changing capitalization to suit syntax — overview** — Most words are lowercase unless they
  begin a sentence (or a line of poetry), so the first word of a quotation often must be adjusted. In
  most works this is done **silently** (12.7, item 3). In some works — legal writing, close textual
  commentary — the change **must** be signaled by bracketing the initial letter (12.21).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.19] Initial capital or lowercase — run-in quotations** — **Lowercase** the first word when the
  quotation is a syntactical part of the sentence; **keep the capital** when the quotation stands in a
  more remote syntactic relation (subject, appositive). Conversely, **a lowercase fragment that forms a
  complete sentence as quoted may be capitalized**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Benjamin Franklin admonishes us to "plough deep while sluggards sleep."*
  - ex: *With another aphorism he reminded his readers that "experience keeps a dear school, but fools
    will learn in no other"—an observation as true today as then.*
  - ex: *As Franklin advised, "Plough deep while sluggards sleep."*
  - ex: *His aphorism "Experience keeps a dear school, but fools will learn in no other" is a cogent
    warning to people of all ages.* (see 6.44)
  - ex (fragment → sentence): *Aristotle put it this way: "Those who are eminent in virtue usually do
    not stir up insurrections, always a minority."* but *Aristotle believed that "those who are
    eminent in virtue usually do not stir up insurrections, always a minority."*
- **[12.20] Initial capital or lowercase — block quotations** — Exactly the same test as 12.19: the
  first letter of a block quotation may be lowercased if the syntax demands it.
  - ex: *In discussing the reasons for political disturbances, Aristotle observes that revolutions
    also break out when opposite parties, e.g. the rich and the people, are equally balanced . . .*
    (Aristotle, *Politics* 5.4, Jowett trans.; original began with a capital and an indent)
  - ex (capital retained/changed because the syntax requires it): *In discussing the reasons for
    political disturbances, Aristotle makes the following observations:* [block] *Revolutions also
    break out when opposite parties, e.g. the rich and the people, are equally balanced, and there is
    little or no middle class; . . .*
- **[12.21] Brackets to indicate a change in capitalization** — In legal writing, close textual
  analysis, and similar contexts, **any** change of capitalization must be bracketed; elsewhere it is
  unnecessary — but wherever adopted, **apply it consistently**. Do **not** bracket merely to match
  house style (e.g., capital *Black*): retain the source's lowercase and, if the discrepancy matters,
  note it. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *According to article 6, section 6, she is given the power "[t]o extend or renew any existing
    indebtedness."*
  - ex: *"[R]eal estates may be conveyed by lease and release, or bargain and sale," according to
    section 2 of the Northwest Ordinance.*
  - ex: *Let us compare Aristotle's contention that "[i]nferiors revolt in order that they may be
    equal, and equals that they may be superior" (Politics 5.2), with his later observation that
    "[r]evolutions also break out when opposite parties, e.g. the rich and the people, are equally
    balanced" (5.4).*
  - ex (no bracket for style, note instead): *According to Du Bois, the musical epigraphs "echo . . .
    black souls in the dark past" (viii; black is spelled with a lowercase b in the source).*

### Block Quotations (12.22–12.24)

- **[12.22] Block quotations of more than one paragraph** — Material of more than one paragraph, "even
  if very brief," is **best set off** (undesirable alternative: 12.32). Reflect the original's
  paragraph breaks, with one exception: **the first quoted paragraph begins flush left even if the
  original indented it**; later paragraphs get first-line indents (preferred) or extra line space
  (less desirable) (12.24). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*, four paragraphs): *He began to wish to know more of her . . .
    It was at Sir William Lucas's, where a large party were assembled.* [new para] *"What does Mr.
    Darcy mean," said she to Charlotte, "by listening to my conversation with Colonel Forster?"*
    [new para] *"That is a question which Mr. Darcy only can answer."* [new para] *"But if he does it
    any more I shall certainly let him know that I see what he is about. . . ."*
- **[12.23] Block quotations beginning in text** — A long quotation may begin with a few words run in
  to the text, but **only when text intervenes** between the run-in words and the continuation.
  **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *"There is no safe trusting to dictionaries and definitions," observed Charles Lamb.* [block]
    *We should more willingly fall in with this popular language, if we did not find brutality
    sometimes awkwardly coupled with valour in the same vocabulary. The comic writers . . . have
    contributed not a little to mislead us upon this point. To see a hectoring fellow exposed and
    beaten upon the stage, has something in it wonderfully diverting.* ("Popular Fallacies," *Essays of
    Elia*, 277)
  - ex: *"In short," says Crane, summarizing Gordon's philosophy,* [block] *there has been "almost a
    continual improvement" in all branches of human knowledge; . . .*
  - Permissible alternative: set off the whole quotation and put the intervening words in **brackets**
    — *There is no safe trusting to dictionaries and definitions [observed Charles Lamb]. We should
    more willingly . . .*
- **[12.24] Text following a block quotation or extract** — If the following text **continues the
  introducing paragraph**, it begins **flush left**; if it is a **new paragraph**, it takes a
  **paragraph indent**. Author decides, else editor (2.23, 2.24); in flush-left-with-space-between
  designs, signal with more space before new paragraphs than before continued text. **[JUDGMENT]**

### Poetry Extracts (12.25–12.29)

- **[12.25] Setting off poetry** — **Two or more lines of verse are best set off as an extract**; in a
  note, set off **three or more** (13.41). Isolated extracts are often visually centered (relative to
  the longest line); two or more stanzas of one poem on a page work better with a **uniform left
  indent** (12.26). Leave **half a line to a full line of space between stanzas**; **reproduce the
  original's indentation pattern**, distinguishing indents from runovers (12.27). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (Herbert, "The Collar"): *Sure there was wine / Before my sighs did drie it: there was corn /
    Before my tears did drown it. / Is the yeare onely lost to me? / Have I no bayes to crown it? / No
    flowers, no garlands gay? all blasted? / All wasted?*
  - If the quotation does **not** begin with a full line, leave space approximating the omitted part
    — *[space] there was corn / Before my tears did drown it.*
- **[12.26] Uniform indents for poetry** — Where extracts are mostly **blank verse** (e.g., studies of
  Shakespeare) or **very long**, use a uniform left indent (matching any prose-extract indent).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (*King Lear*): *I have full cause of weeping, but this heart / Shall break into a hundred
    thousand flaws / Or ere I'll weep. O Fool! I shall go mad.*
- **[12.27] Long lines and runovers** — A runover (the remainder of a line too long for the measure) is
  **indented one em** from the line above, and must be **visually distinct from a line the poet
  deliberately indented**; a unique, uniform runover indent usually suffices (2.24).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (Whitman, "Song of Myself"): *My tongue, every atom of my blood, form'd from this soil, this
    air, / Born here of parents born here from parents the same, and their parents the same, / I, now
    thirty-seven years old in perfect health begin, / Hoping to cease not till death.*
- **[12.28] Quotation marks in poems** — Line-initial quotation marks are **aligned with the other
  lines** (Chicago's practice). Some publishers instead hang them outside the alignment; Chicago does
  **not** follow that practice (though it may be necessary in some electronic formats).
  - ex (Coleridge): *He holds him with his skinny hand. / "There was a ship," quoth he. / "Hold off!
    unhand me, grey-beard loon!" / Eftsoons his hand dropt he.*
- **[12.29] Run-in poetry quotations** — If two or more lines must be run in, separate them with a
  **slash with one space on either side** (a thin space to an en space in print). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Andrew Marvell's praise of John Milton, "Thou has not missed one thought that could be fit, /
    And all that was improper does omit" ("On Paradise Lost"), might well serve as our motto.*
  - Running in more than one stanza: avoid (12.33).

## Quotation Marks

### Double or Single Quotation Marks (12.30–12.31)

- **[12.30] Quotations and "quotes within quotes"** — **Double marks** enclose quoted words, phrases,
  and sentences run in to the text; **single marks** enclose a quotation within a quotation; then double
  again; and so on (UK practice is the reverse). **American style = double first.** When the quoted
  material consists *entirely* of a quotation within a quotation, use only **one** set (usually double),
  except in quoted dialogue (12.46). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"Don't be absurd!" said Henry. "To say that 'I mean what I say' is the same as 'I say what I
    mean' is to be as confused as Alice at the Mad Hatter's tea party. You remember what the Hatter
    said to her: 'Not the same thing a bit! Why you might just as well say that "I see what I eat" is
    the same thing as "I eat what I see"!'"*
  - Note the placement of closing marks **and** of the exclamation points: a terminal ? or ! goes
    **just inside** the set of marks ending the element it belongs to (6.9–11).
- **[12.31] Quotation marks in block quotations** — The block itself carries **no** marks; quoted
  matter *inside* it takes **double** marks as it would in ordinary text. Converting run-in → block
  means **deleting the outer marks and changing internal ones** (and the reverse when converting
  block → run-in). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (run-in): *The narrator then breaks in: "Imagine Bart's surprise, dear reader, when Emma turned
    to him and said, contemptuously, 'What "promise"'?"*
  - ex (block): *The narrator then breaks in:* [block] *Imagine Bart's surprise, dear reader, when Emma
    turned to him and said, contemptuously, "What 'promise'?"*

### Run-In Quotations of More than One Paragraph (12.32–12.34)

- **[12.32] Quotation marks across paragraphs** — Multi-paragraph material should be a block (12.10);
  if it **must** be run in, put an opening mark at the start **and at the start of each new paragraph**,
  but a closing mark **only at the end of the final paragraph** (each paragraph still starts on a new
  line). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.33] Quoting more than one stanza of poetry** — Present as an extract if at all possible; if run
  in, separate stanzas with **two slashes (//), space before and after**. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.34] Quoting letters in their entirety** — Set off as a block. If it must be run in: opening mark
  before the first line (including the salutation) and before **each** new paragraph; closing mark only
  after the last line (often the signature). **[MECHANICAL]**

### Quotation Marks Omitted (12.35–12.38)

- **[12.35] Epigraphs** — **No quotation marks** around an epigraph (an ornamental quotation preceding
  a text to set its tone, not to illustrate or document); marks *within* the original are retained.
  Epigraphs get distinctive typography (smaller type, indented, sometimes italic); the source usually
  sits on a separate line, treated **consistently** across a work (12.81–83; 1.41; permissions 4.91).
  - ex: *It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune,
    must be in want of a wife.* / *Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice*
  - ex: *Hold fast to dreams / For if dreams die / Life is a broken-winged bird / That cannot fly.* /
    *—Langston Hughes, "Dreams"*
  - ex: *"Tommy," I said, quite sternly. "There's mud all over your shirt."* / *Kazuo Ishiguro, Never
    Let Me Go*
  - If the source must be run in: **no space before an em dash** — *Cogito, ergo sum.—Descartes* — or
    use parentheses: *Cogito, ergo sum. (Descartes)* **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.36] Decorative initials ("drop caps" and raised initials)** — When the drop cap is the first
  letter of a run-in quotation, the **opening quotation mark is often omitted**; if kept, it should be
  in the same size and vertical alignment as regular text. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.37] Maxims, questions, and the like** — Maxims, mottoes, rules, and familiar expressions: see
  6.44 and 7.65; questions that need no quotation marks: 6.45, 6.73; dialogue without marks: 12.48.
  (Cross-reference rule.)
- **[12.38] Publicity reviews and blurbs** — Brief endorsements (a few sentences, front matter or
  cover) may be formatted like epigraphs; unlike epigraphs, they are **often placed in quotation
  marks** at the publisher's/designer's discretion (especially if text and source share a line).
  **Brackets for capitalization changes are unnecessary and "arguably distracting"** (12.21).
  **Do not add exclamation points that are not in the original** ("Magnificent!") unless the reviewer
  has approved.
  - ex (as adjusted): *A magnificent, once-in-a-generation feat. . . . Environmentalists will be citing
    this extraordinary opus for decades to come.* / *—Joni C. Reviewer, Science First*; or
    *"Magnificent."—Joni C. Reviewer, Science First*
  - not: *"[M]agnificent."—Joni C. Reviewer, Science First*

## Dialogue and Other Forms of Direct Discourse (12.39–12.53)

- **[12.39] Fictional dialogue versus other forms of direct quotation** — Conventions for fiction
  overlap with conventions for quoting anyone anywhere, "from scholarly prose to works with a more
  conversational tone." The single goal:
  > "In any context the goal is the same: to differentiate the words of a narrator from the words of
  > others, whether those other words have been quoted from a research paper or consist of the
  > imagined dialogue of a fictional character." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[12.40] Quotation marks for dialogue** — Direct speech and other direct discourse take quotation
  marks, and **a change of speaker is marked by a new paragraph** (also when marks are omitted,
  12.48). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (Lahiri, *Interpreter of Maladies*): *"I look forward to it, actually," Mr. Kapasi said as they
    continued on their way. "The Sun Temple is one of my favorite places. . . . I have another job
    during the week."* [new para] *"Oh? Where?" Mr. Das asked.* [new para] *"I work in a doctor's
    office."* [new para] *"You're a doctor?"* [new para] *"I am not a doctor. I work with one. As an
    interpreter."* . . . [new para] *Mr. Kapasi shrugged. "It is a job like any other."* [new para]
    *"But so romantic," Mrs. Das said dreamily, breaking her extended silence.*
- **[12.41] Speaker tags** — Words identifying the speaker (**dialogue tags**) are separated from the
  speech by a comma or other punctuation; **when the speech ends in ? or !, omit the comma or period**
  (6.133, 6.134). A tag that precedes the speech is followed by a **comma** (less often a colon).
  Inverted tags ("asked Ava") are fine but **best avoided with pronouns** (*"Are we late?" asked
  she* — usually not). Restrict tags to verbs that plausibly describe speech (*said, asked, replied,
  answered*); *sighed*/*laughed* may be queried and ultimately deferred to the author, but **avoid
  verbs that cannot apply to speech at all** — *"We're late," she frowned* is wrong. When an **action**
  alone follows the speech, it usually starts a **new sentence**. **[MECHANICAL]** + **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *"We're late," Ava said, "so hurry up."* / *"What time is it?" her friend asked. "I don't have
    a watch."* / *"Look at your phone." Ava was getting impatient. "Or don't you have one?"* or
    *"Look at your phone"—Ava was getting impatient—"or don't you have one?"*
  - ex: *Ava said, "We're late."* / *"Are we late?" asked Ava.*
  - ex: *"We're late," she said, frowning.* or *"We're late." She was frowning.*
- **[12.42] Interrupted speech** — An **em dash** marks an interruption; like ? and !, **the dash alone
  ends the speech** (no comma/period after it). **The dash belongs to the interrupted speaker and goes
  inside the quotation marks**; when the **narrator** intervenes mid-speech, the dashes go **outside**
  the marks even if the speaker's words are themselves interrupted. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"Don't inter—" The egg came out of nowhere, striking the teacher on the forehead.*
  - ex: *"Why do you always—"* / *"Always what?"*
  - ex: *"Did I ever tell you about the—" he began, but nobody was listening.*
  - ex: *"Don't you dare"—Cassandra paused for a moment to glare at Ralph—"interrupt me."*
- **[12.43] Faltering speech and incomplete thoughts** — Use an **ellipsis**; it may stand alone or be
  preceded/followed by ? or !. **In fiction and other creative genres, do not add a period at the end
  of a complete sentence**, and **do not put a comma before the closing quotation mark** — "a
  departure from previous editions intended to align with common usage in fiction" (**18th-ed. change**).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"I . . . I . . . that is, we . . . yes, we have made an awful blunder!"*
  - ex: *"The ship . . . oh my God! . . . it's sinking!" cried Henrietta.*
  - ex: *I thought back to my previous life . . . It no longer seemed real.*
  - ex: *"But . . . but . . ." said Tom.*
  - ex: *"Stop looking at me . . . !" It was at once a threat and an invitation.*
  - ex: *"Have you no scruples . . . ?" The question hung in the air.*
- **[12.44] Stuttering and the like** — Indicate stuttering with **hyphens** (initial sound or entire
  word/phrase); a repeated first word may be **lowercased unless it is a proper noun** — the choice is
  "arbitrary," so **impose consistency** and otherwise defer to the author. Use **em dashes** for
  self-interruption or abrupt turns of thought, **without the usual limit of two per sentence**.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"W-when do we start?" I asked. "I c-ca-ca-can't find my n-notes."*
  - ex: *"W-w-will you t-t-talk to K-Karima? What-what about Ph-Phoebe?"* or, less commonly,
    *"W-W-Will you t-t-talk to K-Karima?"*
  - ex: *"The sky's the limit—Vegas—Miami—wherever you want—and it won't cost you a cent."*
- **[12.45] Dialogue across multiple paragraphs** — If one speaker's words run beyond a paragraph,
  **open quotation marks at the beginning of each new paragraph** and place a **closing mark only at
  the end of the final paragraph**. For a nested quotation spanning paragraphs: single marks at the
  beginning and end of the interior quotation, and **both** double and single marks before each new
  paragraph belonging to the interior quotation. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"This is the first paragraph of quoted speech," says a speaker. "This is a continuation of
    that speech.* / *"This is the second paragraph of the same speech by the same speaker. The speech
    continues.* / *"This is the end of the speech."*
- **[12.46] Quotations within dialogue** — Quoted words inside dialogue take **single** marks — **even
  when the quoted section makes up the entire line of dialogue** (this keeps clear which words belong
  to the speaker). That nesting does **not** apply in narrative text, where a standalone quotation
  takes ordinary double marks. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"What did the law clerk say?" he asked.* / *"He said, 'I would prefer not to,'" I replied.* /
    *"'I would prefer not to'?" he asked. "What's that from?"* / *"'Bartleby, the Scrivener,'" I
    replied. "It's a famous story."*
  - ex (narrative): *Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener" is perhaps best known for its protagonist's
    neurasthenic response to his boss: "I would prefer not to."*
- **[12.47] Single-word speech** — *yes, no, where, how, why* used singly take **no** quotation marks
  except as quoted dialogue (6.73). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Ezra always answered yes; he could never say no to a friend.* / *Please stop asking why.*
    but *"Yes," he replied weakly.* / *Again she repeated, "Why?"*
- **[12.48] Alternatives to quotation marks for dialogue** — Some authors omit marks (centuries-old
  practice: it "removes a layer of artifice between the reader and the narrator, though at the expense
  of clarity"). Options: drama-style (speaker's name + colon, occasionally period; 12.54); plain
  omission with **new paragraph per speaker** and the same punctuation around tags as quoted dialogue;
  **em dashes** before successive speeches (French-modeled); or no paragraph change at all, relying on
  punctuation, capitalization, and context. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (Walker, *The Color Purple*): *Miss Celie! say Sofia. Shock.* / *Girl, I'm bless, I say to
    Sofia. God know what I mean.* / *Us sit round the kitchen table and light up. . . . Pretty soon
    Sofia say, That funny, I never heard that humming before.* / *What humming? Harpo ast.*
- **[12.49] Unspoken discourse** — Thought, imagined dialogue, and internal discourse may be quoted or
  not, by context or author's preference; **when unquoted, italics may be used** (best for works with
  only short passages of interior discourse, "at most a sentence or two here and there"). **A thought
  beginning mid-sentence normally begins with a capital.** **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *"I don't care if we have offended Morgenstern," thought Vera. "Besides," she told herself,
    "they're all fools."*
  - ex: *Why, he wondered, did we choose this route?*
  - ex: *She thought, If there isn't an app for that, I'll need to program it myself.*
- **[12.50] Text messages and the like as dialogue** — Run-in messages may take quotation marks; more
  than a few are better given separate typography. For conversations: **italics for the messages,
  speakers identified in parentheses** (instead of tags), and each exchange set as its **own
  block-style line, uniformly indented** (12.9). Preserve the author's authentic stylistic quirks
  (missing ? and periods, nonstandard capitalization) — **query before changing**; enforce consistency.
  **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *(me) Are they here yet?* / *(him) Yes.* / *(me) Give me a sec. I'll be right there.*
- **[12.51] Numerals and abbreviations in quoted speech and dialogue** — In direct quotation from
  spoken sources and in written dialogue, **numbers may be spelled out** where they would otherwise be
  numerals — "this practice requires editorial discretion." **Retain numerals** for years, brand names
  containing numerals, exact times other than full/quarter/half hours, days of the month, numbers that
  correspond to actual numerals (street addresses, room numbers, signs), and passages with many large
  numbers. **When in doubt, choose the form most recognizable.** Abbreviations that are always
  abbreviated (social titles, brand abbreviations, abbreviations pronounced as such) may stay
  abbreviated. **[JUDGMENT]** (spelling out = **[MECHANICAL]**)
  - ex: *Jarred's answer was a mix of rage and humiliation: "For the last time, I do not have seven
    hundred thirty-seven dollars and eleven cents! I don't even have a quarter for the parking meter,
    for that matter."*
  - ex: *"I'm never happy with a mere ninety-nine and forty-four one-hundredths percent."* /
    *"You're so healthy I bet you live to be a hundred and ten."* / *"Set your alarm for four a.m."*
  - ex: *"Do you prefer shopping at 7-Eleven or Circle K?"* / *"I didn't get around to reading
    Nineteen Eighty-Four until 1985," he finally admitted.* / *"My mobile number is 555-0133."* /
    *"I'm at 30 Crescent Lane, in apartment 3B."* / *"He was last seen on January the 5th at 2:25
    p.m."*
  - ex: *The man at the front desk called out to me: "Dr. Jayabalan will see you now."* / *"He works
    for AT&T Inc."*
- **[12.52] Parentheses in dialogue** — **Avoid parentheses in quoted dialogue** (readers may mistake
  them for the narrator's words); use **dashes** (or move the words into the narrative). Square
  brackets for real clarifications: 12.71. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"I know it's early—my watch says 11:35—but can we please have lunch now?"*
  - not: *"I know it's early (my watch says 11:35), but can we please have lunch now?"*
- **[12.53] Indirect discourse** — Paraphrased speech takes **no** quotation marks (6.45).
  - ex: *Tom told Huck they had to do it that way because the books said so.* / *Very well, you say,
    but is there no choice?*

## Drama, Discussions and Interviews, and Field Notes (12.54–12.58)

- **[12.54] Drama** — Speaker's name in a **distinct font** (caps and small caps, or all small caps);
  **dialogue takes no quotation marks**; dialogue set with **hanging indents** (2.14). **Stage
  directions are italicized**, usually run in with the dialogue they accompany. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (Udall): *R. ROISTER DOISTER. Except I have her to my wife, I shall run mad.* / *M. MERYGREKE.
    Nay, "unwise" perhaps, but I warrant you for "mad."*
  - ex (Wilde): *ALGERNON. That is quite a different matter. She is my aunt. (Takes plate from
    below.) Have some bread and butter. . . .* / *JACK, advancing to table and helping himself. And
    very good bread and butter it is too.*
- **[12.55] Shared lines and runover lines in verse drama** — A verse line **shared** between two
  speakers is presented so the second speaker's words **continue where the first left off**; runovers
  are indented one em or more from the line above (12.27). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (*Hamlet*): *BARNARDO. It would be spoke to.* / *MARCELLUS. Speak to it, Horatio.* / *HORATIO.
    What art thou that usurp'st this time of night, / Together with that fair and warlike form / In
    which the majesty of buried Denmark / Did sometimes march? By heaven, I charge thee, speak.*
- **[12.56] Discussions and interviews** — Treat like drama: names in caps; **interjections such as
  [laughs] are italicized and set in square brackets** (drama uses parentheses; see 12.70–74).
  **Paragraph indents preferred** over hanging indents (hanging helps only with many brief
  speakers). Names take a **period** (or colon); abbreviate after first appearance to save space.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *INTERVIEWER. You weren't thinking that this technology would be something you could use to
    connect to the Office of Tibet in New York or to different Tibet support groups in Europe?* /
    *RESPONDENT. No. Nobody seemed to have anything to do with GreenNet in the Tibet world at that
    time. . . . I specifically wasn't interested in connecting to the community of Tibet martyrs and
    fellow sufferers [laughs] and the emotional pathological there-but-for-the-grace-of-God-go-I
    people.*
  - **Previously unpublished** transcriptions by the author may be edited for capitalization,
    spelling, and minor grammatical slips; **more significant alterations must be explained** (note,
    preface); **previously published transcriptions are quoted as they appear** (4.81).
- **[12.57] Interviews transcribed by authors** — Edit the author's own transcriptions for
  **consistency** in spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and treatment of numbers; if the author
  also translated them, **say so in a note**. Two caveats: (1) **do not "correct" representations of
  an individual's speech** (e.g., *goin'*), though authors should transcribe dialect "only to the
  extent they are relevant"; (2) **do not make punctuation changes that risk altering the meaning,
  tone, or pacing** of the response. Document the choices in a **style sheet** (2.61).
  **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[12.58] Case studies and ethnographic field notes** — Unpublished field notes/case material (the
  author's or a colleague's) **need not be presented verbatim**; edit for consistency with related
  material and the surrounding text. Even with signed releases, present anonymous subjects under
  **pseudonyms** and append a note saying so; modifications for anonymity are acceptable but **must
  not lead to misrepresentation**; editors query when unclear (4.81). **[JUDGMENT]**

## Ellipses (12.59–12.69)

- **[12.59] Ellipses defined** — An ellipsis is **three dots** signaling omission of a word, phrase,
  line, or paragraph (or, occasionally, adjusting the grammar of the surrounding text). **Chicago
  style is three spaced periods** (`. . .`), not asterisks or other devices; also used for faltering
  speech (12.43). **The dots must always appear together on the same line** (nonbreaking spaces) with
  any punctuation immediately following; if an ellipsis falls at the start of a line, any preceding
  punctuation stays at the end of the line above. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.60] Danger of skewing meaning** — Take particular care that elision does not lose or
  misrepresent the sense:
  > "A deletion must not result in a statement alien to the original material. And in general,
  > ellipses should not be used to join two statements that are far apart in the original. Accuracy of
  > sense and emphasis must accompany accuracy of transcription." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[12.61] When not to use an ellipsis** — **Do not** use one (1) **before the first word** of a
  quotation, even if the start of the original sentence is omitted, or (2) **after the last word**,
  even if the end of the sentence is omitted — unless the quoted sentence is **deliberately
  incomplete** (12.64). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.62] Ellipses with periods (four-dot method)** — Add a **period before** the ellipsis to show
  omission of the **end of a sentence**, unless the sentence is deliberately incomplete (12.64);
  likewise retain a period that follows material immediately preceding the omission, and the period in
  an abbreviation (*etc.*). **What precedes and (normally) follows the four dots should be
  grammatically complete sentences as quoted.** Capitalize the first word after an ellipsis if it
  begins a new sentence (bracket the change where 12.21 requires). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (Emerson, "Politics," as shortened): *The spirit of our American radicalism is destructive and
    aimless. . . . On the other side, the conservative party . . . is timid, and merely defensive of
    property. . . . It does not build, nor write, nor cherish the arts, nor foster religion, nor
    establish schools.*
- **[12.63] Ellipses with other punctuation** — A comma, colon, semicolon, question mark, or
  exclamation point **may precede or follow** an ellipsis (never a period before it, 12.62); include
  the mark **only if it aids comprehension or grammar requires it**, and place it according to whether
  the omission precedes or follows it. Use a **nonbreaking space** between the ellipsis and a
  following mark. This before/after distinction is usually **not** made with periods (12.62, 12.67).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *It does not build, . . . nor cherish the arts, nor foster religion.*
  - ex: *As to Endymion, was it a poem . . . to be treated contemptuously by those who had celebrated,
    with various degrees of complacency and panegyric, Paris, and Woman, and A Syrian Tale . . . ? Are
    these the men who . . . presumed to draw a parallel between the Rev. Mr. Milman and Lord Byron?*
  - ex: *When a species . . . increases inordinately in numbers in a small tract, epidemics . . .
    often ensue: and here we have a limiting check independent of the struggle for life. But even some
    of these so-called epidemics appear to be due to parasitic worms . . . : and here comes in a sort
    of struggle between the parasite and its prey.*
- **[12.64] Ends of deliberately incomplete sentences** — Use **three dots only** (no added period) at
  the end of a quoted sentence deliberately left grammatically incomplete; **no space** between the
  final dot and a closing quotation mark. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Everyone knows that the Declaration of Independence begins with the words "When, in the
    course of human events . . ." But how many people can recite more than the first few lines of the
    document?*
  - ex: *Have you had a chance to look at the example beginning "The spirit of our American radicalism
    . . ."?*
- **[12.65] Omission of whole or partial paragraphs** — Indicate omission of one or more paragraphs
  with a **period followed by an ellipsis at the end of the paragraph preceding the cut** (three dots
  only if that paragraph ends mid-sentence, 12.64). If the **first part of a paragraph** is omitted,
  show a **paragraph indent plus an ellipsis before the first quoted word** — so ellipses may appear
  at both the end of one paragraph and the start of the next. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (Pope, "Letter to a Noble Lord"): *I should be obliged indeed to lessen this respect, if all the
    nobility . . . are but so many hereditary fools . . . .* [new para] *Were it the mere excess of
    your Lordship's wit, that carried you thus triumphantly over all the bounds of decency, I might
    consider your Lordship on your Pegasus, as a sprightly hunter on a mettled horse. . . .* [new
    para] *. . . Unrivalled as you are, in making a figure, and in making a speech, methinks, my Lord,
    you may well give up the poor talent of making a distich.*
- **[12.66] Ellipses in poetry and verse drama** — Omitting the **end of a line**: period + ellipsis if
  what precedes is a complete grammatical sentence, otherwise three dots. Omitting a **full line or
  several lines**: a **line of widely spaced dots** about the length of the line above (or of the
  missing line). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (Poe, "The Coliseum"): *Type of the antique Rome! Rich reliquary / Of lofty contemplation . . .*
  - ex (Hardy, "One We Knew"): *She would dwell on such dead themes, not as one who remembers, / But
    rather as one who sees.* [line of dots] *Past things retold were to her as things existent, /
    Things present but as a tale.*
  - ex (*Richard II* 2.1.40–50): *This royal throne of kings, this sceptred isle, / This blessed plot,
    this earth, this realm, this England.* [the omitted run of lines is shown by a line of spaced dots
    in the book; the dots line did not survive OCR]
- **[12.67] Bracketed ellipses** — Where ellipses are frequent in the original (or the language makes
  liberal use of them), **bracket your own** inserted ellipses — but **only after explaining the
  decision** in a note/preface; alternatively identify each with "ellipsis in original" (12.73). All
  the rules above apply, with one exception: **the period goes before or after the ellipsis according
  to its placement in the original**. Spacing: **a space before the opening bracket; a space after the
  closing bracket except before a period, comma, or other mark**; inside the brackets the sequence is
  bracket–period–space–period–space–period–bracket (nonbreaking spaces only between the periods).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The spirit of our American radicalism is destructive and aimless [. . .]. On the other side,
    the conservative party [. . .] is timid, and merely defensive of property. [. . .] It does not
    build, nor write, nor cherish the arts, nor foster religion, nor establish schools.*
- **[12.68] Spaced periods versus the ellipsis character** — Chicago style is **three spaced periods**;
  apply nonbreaking spaces before and after the middle period, and a nonbreaking space between the
  third period and any following comma, semicolon, ?, or !. Some publishers use the **U+2026
  character** (won't break); authors may use it in manuscripts, or **three unspaced periods** — but
  **only one type of ellipsis per document**. With an unspaced ellipsis, put a **space before and
  after**, except before a comma or other mark. Copyeditors imposing Chicago style convert these to
  spaced periods. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.69] Ellipses in mathematical expressions** — In **elided operations and relations**, the
  ellipsis is **centered vertically** on the operation sign (aligned with +, −, =); in an **elided
  list** (comma-separated terms), the ellipsis sits **on the baseline** as in ordinary text, and
  commas follow each term **and** the ellipsis dots when a final term follows. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *x₁, x₂, . . . , xₙ* — not *x₁, x₂, . . . xₙ* [both formulas are garbled in OCR; the contrast
    is baseline vs. centered placement and the comma after the dots]

## Interpolations and Clarifications (12.70–12.74)

- **[12.70] Missing or illegible words** — Use **ellipses** (if more than a few lacunae, explain their
  function in text or note), a **bracketed comment or guess** (optionally with a question mark), or
  both; cite the source of any bracketed gloss from elsewhere (6.105). A **2-em dash**, sometimes with
  an interpolated guess, may also mark missing material — used consistently and explained.
  **[MECHANICAL]** + **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *If you will assure me of your . . . [illegible], I shall dedicate my life to your endeavor.*
  - ex: *She marched out the door, headed for the [president's?] office.*
- **[12.71] Bracketed clarifications** — Insertions in quoted material may clarify ambiguity, supply
  missing words/letters, correct an error, or (in translation) give the original word. They **must
  never change or misrepresent the original** ("or otherwise amount to misquoting the source") and
  **should be kept to a minimum** "lest they irritate or distract readers." **Always brackets, never
  parentheses** (6.105, 13.28). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Marcellus, doubtless in anxious suspense, asks Barnardo, "What, has this thing [the ghost of
    Hamlet's father] appear'd again tonight?"*
  - ex: *"Well," said she, "if Mr. L[owell] won't go, then neither will I."*
  - ex: *Saha once remarked of Nehru that "his position in this country can be described by a phrase
    which Americans use with respect to Abraham Lincoln [read: George Washington], 'first in war, first
    in peace.'"*
- **[12.72] "Sic"** — *Sic* ("so, thus, in this manner"), **italicized**, in **brackets**, follows a
  word misspelled or wrongly used in the original. **Use it only when necessary** — where readers
  might otherwise think the error is the transcriber's — and **only when paraphrase or silent
  correction is inappropriate** (12.4; 12.7, item 6). **Do not** sic unconventional spellings; explain
  them in a note or prefatory note. Where a whole text is reproduced as written (e.g., informal
  letters), a single prefatory statement makes successive *sics* unnecessary. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *In September 1862, J. W. Chaffin, president of the Miami Conference of Wesleyan Methodist
    Connection, urged Lincoln that "the confiscation law past [sic] at the last session of Congress
    should be faithfully executed" . . .*
- **[12.73] "Italics added"** — If you italicize words in a quotation for emphasis, **tell the reader**
  — "italics mine," "italics added," "emphasis added," "emphasis mine" — in parentheses after the
  quotation or in a source note. To note italics that were **in the original**, use "italics in the
  original" or "De Quincey's italics"; if the passage already contains original italics, put the note
  **in brackets directly after the added italics**. **Consistency in method throughout a work is
  essential.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *. . . we may deal freely and yet deal prudently with our fellow-subjects beyond the Channel.
    Such is your conviction.* (William Ewart Gladstone, October 1891; italics added)
  - ex: *Look at the poem of Cyder by Philips, of the Fleece by Dyer, or . . . at the Georgics of
    Virgil,—does any of these poets show the least anxiety for the correctness of your principles [my
    italics], or the delicacy of your manipulations . . . ?* (De Quincey, "Essay on Pope")
- **[12.74] Interpolations requiring quotation marks** — When a bracketed or parenthetical
  interpolation itself contains quotation marks inside material already quoted, **the double/single
  rule (12.30) does not apply** — the marks inside the brackets may stay **double**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"Do you mean that a double-headed calf ["two-headed calf" in an earlier version] has greater
    value than two normal calves? . . ."*

## Attributing Quotations in Text (12.75–12.78)

- **[12.75] Parentheses with in-text citations** — If the source is not in a note, put it **in the
  text in parentheses**; it normally follows the quotation but **may come earlier** if it fits the
  introductory text more smoothly. These forms supplement the note/bibliography system of chs. 13–14
  and suit short works without notes, frequently cited works, or informal citation (13.5, 13.102–4).
- **[12.76] Full in-text citation** — Give the whole source in parentheses after a run-in quotation,
  or work some data into the text and confine the rest to parentheses (6.107).
  - ex: *". . . Thus, in a sense, the astronaut will be 'recycled'" (Stephen W. Hawking, A Brief
    History of Time: From the Big Bang to Black Holes [Bantam Books, 1988], 112).*
  - ex: *In her foreword to the University of Chicago Press's 2022 reissue of Deep South . . . Isabel
    Wilkerson traces anthropologist Allison Davis's journey with his white coauthors to Natchez,
    Mississippi, where, "in the depths of the Jim Crow caste system . . . they would find their every
    move dictated by the very phenomenon that they were studying" (xiv).*
- **[12.77] Shortened citations or "ibid."** — For a second passage from the same source with **no
  intervening quotation from another source**, use the author's name or *ibid.* (roman) in the second
  parenthetical reference: *(Hawking, 114)* or *(ibid., 114)*. **Chicago recommends repeating the
  author's name** as less ambiguous; *ibid.* only if used consistently. **Avoid overusing either** —
  for extended discussion of one work, a **bare page number** is usually better. If another source has
  intervened, include the **title** as well *(Hawking, Brief History of Time, 114)* unless it is clear
  from context (13.32–39, 13.37). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[12.78] Frequent reference to a single source cited in a note** — Cite the full source in a note at
  first mention (e.g., "Text references are to act, scene, and line of this edition") and thereafter
  cite parenthetically in the text; **page numbers alone** for a work of fiction. With multiple such
  sources, indicate the title/edition in each reference, ideally with listed abbreviations (13.63,
  13.64).
  - ex: *"Ye light o' love with your heels! then, if your husband have stables enough, you'll see he
    shall lack no barns," says Beatrice (3.4.46–47).*

### Sources Following Run-In Quotations (12.79–12.80)

- **[12.79] Punctuation following the source** — After a run-in quotation the source usually follows
  the **closing quotation mark**, followed by the rest of the sentence (including any comma, semicolon,
  colon, or dash) or by the sentence's final punctuation. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *With his "Nothing will come of nothing; speak again" (1.1.92), Lear tries to draw from his
    youngest daughter an expression of filial devotion.*
  - ex: *It has been more than a century since Henry Adams said: "Fifty years ago, science took for
    granted that the rate of acceleration could not last. . . ." (Education, 493).*
  - A parenthetical reference **need not immediately follow** the quotation if it is clear what it
    belongs to (12.76, 12.78; 13.120).
- **[12.80] Punctuation preceding the source** — If the quotation ends the sentence and is itself a
  question or exclamation, **keep that mark inside the quotation marks** and **still add a period
  after the closing parenthesis**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *And finally, in the frenzy of grief that kills him, Lear rails, "Why should a dog, a horse, a
    rat, have life, / And thou no breath at all?" (5.3.306–7).*

### Sources Following Block Quotations and Poetry Extracts (12.81–12.83)

- **[12.81] Parenthetical source after a block quotation** — Put the source in parentheses at the end
  of the quotation, **in the same type size**; the **opening parenthesis follows the final punctuation
  of the quoted material**, and **no period precedes or follows the closing parenthesis** (6.107,
  13.120). Shortened references work the same way; a leading *line*, *vol.*, or *p.* is **lowercased**
  (*p.* often omitted). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *. . . then you may know you have been fishing in the Galapagos Islands and have taken a
    Golden Grouper.* (Gifford Pinchot, *To the South Seas* [John Winston, 1930], 123)
  - ex: *. . . but something else again entirely.* (p. 142) or (142)
- **[12.82] Parenthetical citations with poetry extracts** — Drop the citation to the **line below the
  last line** of the extract so as not to disturb the poem's layout; center it on the last letter of
  the longest line or set it flush left with the poem (an extra line space is optional). Other
  positions are acceptable if consistent and clear. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Now more than ever seems it rich to die, / To cease upon the midnight with no pain, / While
    thou art pouring forth thy soul abroad / In such an ecstasy!* / (Keats, "Ode to a Nightingale,"
    stanza 6)
- **[12.83] Shortened references to poetry extracts** — Treat like full references: once the reference
  system is established (book, canto, stanza), give the bare numbers on the line below.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Who will not mercie unto others shew, / How can he mercy ever hope to have?* / (6.1.42)

## Overview (11.1–11.2)

- **[11.1] Scope** — This chapter governs how text from other languages is presented inside
  English-language prose. The rules are *general*: "Authors or editors working with languages in which
  they are not expert should seek additional guidance from someone who is." More than two dozen
  languages are covered; ordering is roughly by writing system, then alphabetically by language.
  Personal names are handled in 8.3–19, not here.
- **[11.2] Unicode** — Non-English characters must be encoded in Unicode, not in legacy fonts.
  Identify a character by name plus code point when querying or listing it: "D with stroke [U+0110]";
  in text, prefix Unicode numbers with **U+** (e.g., U+00E0 for *à*). Tables 11.1–11.5 map the special
  characters for each language. **[MECHANICAL]**

## General Principles (11.3–11.19)

### Words and Phrases from Other Languages (11.3–11.7)

- **[11.3] Italicize isolated non-English words and phrases** — In work for an English-speaking
  readership, italicize an isolated foreign word or phrase, *especially* if it is not in
  Merriam-Webster or is unfamiliar to readers. Rationale, verbatim: "This convention is intended to
  alert readers that such terms would not normally be found in English and can help prevent a
  non-English word from being misread as an error."
  - ex: *The grève du zèle is not a true strike but a nitpicking obeying of work rules.*
  - ex: *Obligatory service duty in the Assyrian military organization—known as the ilku tax—was
    imposed on all subjects of the empire.*
  - Italicize only at **first occurrence** if the term recurs and becomes familiar; keep italics on
    every occurrence if it appears only rarely. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - Exception: the italics convention "does not necessarily apply to non-English words and phrases
    used by a multilingual author or speaker" — see 11.4.
- **[11.3 cont.] Keep the original language's capitalization** — "Unless the term appears in a standard
  English-language dictionary and is being used as such, observe the capitalization conventions of the
  original language." German nouns stay capitalized; French adjectives stay lowercase even where
  English would capitalize.
  - ex: *In German, a computer is a Computer. In French, it is an ordinateur.*
  - ex: *We were prepared to learn the nuances of la langue française.* (lowercase adjective)
- **[11.3 cont.] Form plurals as in the source language** — "The plurals of non-English words should
  be formed as in the original language" (see also 7.12).
  - ex: *some beautiful Blumen* — not *Blumes*. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
  - Scope note: this rule governs live foreign words. Greco-Latin plurals that have become English
    (*criteria*, *phenomena*, *theses*) and the possessive of foreign terms are ch. 7 business
    (7.12; 7.19), not ch. 11's.
- **[11.3 cont.] Whole sentences go roman** — An entire sentence, or a passage of two or more
  sentences, in another language is usually set in **roman**, and—unless it is a block quotation—
  enclosed in quotation marks (see 11.13). Do not italicize a full foreign sentence as if it were a
  word.
- **[11.4] Italics in multilingual narratives and dialogue** — Foreign words that would be familiar to
  a particular author, narrator, or speaker need not be italicized even if readers might not know
  them. Roman type "can help establish a narrator's or character's authenticity." The decision is the
  author's: "editors can help by imposing consistency but should query before making any substantial
  changes."
  - ex: Gabriela García, *Of Women and Salt* (2021): *"Estoy bien," she said. … "rest in the hamaca,
    won't you?"* — *Mamá*, *estoy bien*, and *hamaca* are roman in the novel though italic as words in
    the sentence describing them.
  - The principle: "By drawing attention to the switch from English to Spanish and back, italics would
    risk making the dialogue seem less realistic."
  - Working rule for an agent: in fiction/dialogue, **default to roman** for code-switched words the
    speaker would use naturally, and italicize only genuinely alien or first-use technical terms;
    never "fix" an author's consistent choice. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.5] Do not italicize non-English proper nouns** — Proper nouns from other languages are generally
  roman, even on first mention (books and other titles excepted — see 11.8–12). This covers named
  places and structures, institutions and companies, brand names, and the categories in ch. 8.
  Capitalization follows "predominant usage in the original language"; when in doubt, "opt for sentence
  case" (8.159). An initial *the* may be used if the definite article appears in the original.
  - ex: *She won the Premio Nadal for her second novel,* Viento del norte*.*
  - ex: *Mexico City's* Ángel de la Independencia *is known familiarly as "El Ángel."*
  - ex: *Leghorn—in Italian, Livorno—is a port in Tuscany.*
  - ex: *Arashiyama (Mount Arashi) shares its name with the district on the outskirts of Kyoto.*
  - ex: *The Real Academia Española was founded in 1713.*
- **[11.5 cont.] Glossed proper nouns stay roman** — Original (or transliterated) names given as a
  parenthetical gloss are **not** italicized (contrast 11.6 for common nouns).
  - ex: *the Supreme People's Court of the People's Republic of China (Zhonghua renmin gongheguo
    zuigao renmin fayuan)*
- **[11.6] Gloss translations of terms** — A translation following a foreign word, phrase, or title goes
  in parentheses or quotation marks (or both, when the parentheses carry explanatory text). See also
  6.102, 11.3, 11.5, 11.11, 13.101.
  - ex: *The word she wanted was* pécher *(to sin), not* pêcher *(to fish).*
  - ex: *The Prakrit word* majjāo*, "the tomcat," may be a dialect version of either of two Sanskrit
    words.*
  - ex: *a group of German expressionists known as* Die Brücke *(The Bridge)*
- **[11.6 cont.] Italicize a foreign common noun inside a gloss** — If the glossed item is a common noun
  (not a proper noun), keep it italic as it would be in running text.
  - ex: *He said that to fish (*pêcher*) was to sin (*pécher*).* **[MECHANICAL]** (italics+parentheses
    pattern)
- **[11.7] Language tags** — In electronic formats, tag foreign-language runs so text-to-speech and
  other assistive tech can switch pronunciation: `<html lang="en">` for the document, `<span lang="es">`
  for the phrase (ISO 639 two-letter codes). Tagging is normally the publisher's job, but authors should
  highlight or style-mark all non-English words, phrases, and excerpts in the manuscript.

### Titles of Works from Other Languages (11.8–11.12)

- **[11.8] Capitalize foreign titles in sentence case** — "Capitalize only the words that would be
  capitalized in normal prose, including the first word of the title and subtitle and all proper nouns
  and other terms that would be capitalized under the conventions of the original language." Applies to
  Latin-alphabet **and** transliterated titles, in text, notes, and bibliographies. See 8.159, 13.100;
  German 11.42; French 11.30. Chicago frames it as a deliberate simplification — "Chicago recommends a
  simple rule" — so do not carry English headline-style capitalization onto a foreign title.
  - ex: *Le rouge et le noir* — not *Le Rouge et le Noir*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.9] Make only the permitted punctuation changes to a foreign title** — (1) change a period (or,
  rarely, semicolon) between title and subtitle to a colon, and you may capitalize the first word of the
  subtitle; (2) change guillemets or other native quotation marks to English quotation marks;
  (3) remove a space between a word and following punctuation. Then stop: **do not insert or delete
  commas** (even in a series or before a date), and do not add or delete any other mark. See 8.167.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.10] Italic or roman by work type, not by language** — Foreign-language titles (including
  transliterated ones) follow 8.157–204: books and periodicals italic; poems, articles, and other short
  works roman in quotation marks.
  - ex: Stendhal's *Le rouge et le noir*; the *Neue Zürcher Zeitung*; *Annales de démographie
    historique* (periodicals italic)
  - ex: the carol "Es ist ein' Ros' entsprungen"; Hernández's poem "Casida del sediento" (short works
    roman, quoted)
- **[11.11] Gloss a foreign title in parentheses** — An English gloss may follow the foreign title in
  text (6.102). An **unpublished** translation is sentence case and in neither italics nor quotation
  marks; a **published** translation is title case (8.160) and italic or quoted according to the type of
  work (8.157–204).
  - ex: *Compendio de i secreti rationali (Compendium of rational secrets)* — unpublished translation,
    sentence case, roman.
  - ex: *À la recherche du temps perdu (Remembrance of Things Past)* — published translation, title
    case, italic.
  - ex: *La ciudad y los perros*, *which literally means "the city and the dogs," was published in
    English under the title* The Time of the Hero*.* **[JUDGMENT]** (three-way form choice)
- **[11.12] Choose original or translated title by readership** — In a general work, cite a title widely
  known in English in English first, with the original in parentheses, and you may omit the original.
  Some authors cite every foreign title in English form whether or not a translation exists; that is
  acceptable **provided the documentation makes clear what has and has not been published in English**.
  A translated title standing in for the original may be title case and treated like any English title
  (8.160, 8.164).
  - ex: *"The West" in the title of the Chinese classic* Journey to the West *(Xi you ji) refers mainly
    to the Indian subcontinent.*

### Quotations from Other Languages (11.13–11.19)

- **[11.13] Treat foreign quotations like English quotations** — Set them in roman; run in or set off as
  block quotations by length (ch. 12). Punctuate as in the original *except* that English quotation
  marks may replace guillemets and spacing/punctuation placement conforms to the surrounding text
  (11.21). Em-dash dialogue stays in a **block** quotation in the original language but may become
  quotation marks if only a phrase is quoted in running text.
  - ex: the narrator's *"treinta o cuarenta molinos de viento"* become Quixote's *"treinta, o pocos
    más, desaforados gigantes."*
- **[11.14] Place the translation relative to the quotation** — A translation may follow the original in
  parentheses (or the original may follow the translation). Do not repeat quotation marks around the
  parenthetical version, but **include its internal quotation marks**. If a long sentence or more than
  one sentence is parenthesized, keep the closing punctuation of original and translation distinct.
  - ex: *"Wer nie sein Brot mit Tränen aß" (Who never ate his bread with tears)*
  - Whether to translate at all depends on audience: classicists may be given Latin and Greek freely,
    a Goethe study may quote German only; "For a wider readership, translations should be furnished."
- **[11.15] Give source then original (or translation), separated by a semicolon** — When both a source
  and a translation are needed in text, put the source in parentheses and the other version after it,
  separated by a semicolon (see also 12.79–80). If this clutters the page, move the translation or
  original to a note, where it is enclosed in quotation marks but **not** in parentheses or brackets.
  - ex: *(letter 8:39; Ende ontrowe en laet gegherten niewers ghedueren in gheenre trowen …)*
- **[11.16] Credit the translation** — Use a published English translation when one exists and credit
  title, translator, bibliographic details, and page (13.101). For your own, say so: "(my translation)"
  or "Unless otherwise noted, all translations are my own." Credit another person's translations by
  name. Credit machine translation: "Translations generated by DeepL and edited by the author."
  **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.17] Flag adjustments to a published translation** — If you change a word or two, add "translation
  modified" in parentheses or a note, and indicate the changes with square brackets (12.70, 12.71); use
  such devices sparingly. If a published translation is unsuitable, abandon it and translate all quoted
  passages anew. Machine translations may be edited **without** square brackets, but note whether changes
  were made. **[MECHANICAL]** (detect "translation modified" + brackets)
- **[11.18] Editing a translation** — Quotations from published translations may be modified only as
  permitted by 12.7. In new translations (including an author's edited machine translation),
  capitalization, punctuation, spelling, and idiom may be adjusted for consistency with the surrounding
  text.
- **[11.19] Never retranslate a translation** — "Never should a passage from a work originally published
  in English (or any other language, for that matter) be retranslated from a version that has been
  translated into another language." Track down the original text; if it cannot be found, **paraphrase**.
  - ex: quoting a German study of Blackstone that quotes Blackstone in German → find and quote the
    English Blackstone, or paraphrase.

## Languages Using the Latin Alphabet (11.20–11.77)

- **[11.20] Assume fewer capitals than English** — "Capitalization is applied to more classes of words
  in English than in any other Western language." Except where stated otherwise, the languages in this
  section lowercase all adjectives (except proper-noun adjectives), all pronouns, months, and days of
  the week, and use capitals more sparingly for names of offices and institutions. **Translated** terms
  follow Chicago's English capitalization rules (ch. 8). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.21] Normalize punctuation to English, keep dialogue dashes** — Native quotation marks may be
  kept in examples, but spacing is adjusted to the surrounding text. In quotations and translations,
  English quotation marks "can usually replace the guillemets" (with periods and commas placed per
  6.9–11). Dashes marking dialogue are **preserved** in block quotations given in the original language
  (11.13). Spanish inverted opening marks are preserved in Spanish quotations but omitted in translation
  (11.69).
- **[11.22] Word division, general** — Applies to non-English as to English: (1) never break a
  single-syllable word; (2) never break after one letter, nor carry a single letter to the next line;
  (3) break hyphenated words and closed compounds at the hyphen or between elements. See 7.40; proper
  nouns 7.42. *(Mostly typesetting; relevant when quoting line-broken originals.)*
- **[11.23] Preserve every special character** — Words, phrases, and titles from another language "must
  include any special characters that appear in the original language": accents and diacritics,
  ligatures, and alphabet forms not used in English. Supply the publisher a list of special characters
  (2.19); identify each by name and Unicode number ("D with stroke [U+0110]"). **[MECHANICAL]**
  (an accented or ligatured form reduced to ASCII is almost always an error)
- **[11.24] IPA** — Phonetic transcription uses IPA symbols defined in Unicode; consult the International
  Phonetic Association for the current alphabet. *(Specialist only; not prose style.)*

### African Languages (11.25–11.26)

- **[11.25] Follow English conventions; capitalize language names conventionally** — Most African
  languages use the Latin alphabet and follow English capitalization and punctuation (the major
  exception is Arabic, 11.83–88). Capitalize language names the conventional way: *Kiswahili*, not
  *KiSwahili* or *KISwahili*; *isiXhosa* is the community spelling (*Isixhosa* also appears). Xhosa and
  other click languages do not follow English capitalization inside the language.
- **[11.26] African special characters** — Swahili needs none. Hausa requires hooked letters
  *ɓ ɗ ƙ ƴ*; in Nigeria an apostrophe is used instead (*'Y 'y*). Collapse: other African orthographies
  carry diacritics not listed here — do not strip them. **[MECHANICAL]**

### French (11.27–11.40)

- **[11.27] Authorities** — French capitalization and punctuation vary; consult Grevisse, *Le bon usage*,
  and the Académie française. *(Resource note; skipped as a rule.)*
- **[11.28] French capitalization** — Lowercase generic words for roadways and squares, even alongside a
  specific name: *le boulevard Saint-Germain*, *la place de l'Opéra*, *13, rue des Beaux-Arts*. In
  geographic names lowercase the generic word and capitalize the modifier: *la mer Rouge*, *le pic du
  Midi*. Buildings are capitalized: *l'Hôtel des Invalides*, *le Palais du Louvre*. In organizations,
  capitalize only the first substantive and any preceding modifier, **not** the article: *l'Académie
  française*, *la Bibliothèque nationale de France*, *la Légion d'honneur*, *le Grand Théâtre de
  Québec*. Hyphenated names capitalize both elements: *la Comédie-Française*, *la Haute-Loire*.
  Lowercase religious groups (*un chrétien*, *des juifs*). Lowercase *saint* in a person's name
  (*le supplice de saint Pierre*) but capitalize and hyphenate it in a place or church name
  (*l'église de Saint-Pierre*). Lowercase adjectives from proper nouns: *une imagination
  baudelairienne*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.29] French names in an English context** — Many of these proper nouns may be capitalized in title
  case "as an aid to legibility," at editorial discretion, but only for names **retained in French**
  (often preceded by *the*), not for translated names. When in doubt, sentence case.
  - ex: *the Boulevard Saint-Germain*, *the Académie Française*, *the Bibliothèque Nationale de France*
  - but: *the Legion of Honor (Légion d'honneur)* — the translated name takes English form.
- **[11.30] Titles of French works: sentence case** — Chicago recommends sentence case, following
  Grevisse (11.8, 11.27). Keep a superscript ordinal in superior position (*Paris au XXᵉ siècle* — the
  ordinal *e* is never lowered). One standing exception: the newspaper *Le Monde*.
  - ex: *L'Apollon de Bellac: Pièce en un acte*; *Le père Goriot*; *L'assommoir*; *L'Exil et le royaume*
  - Alternative practice (unnumbered continuation in the text): for titles beginning with a definite
    article, capitalize article + first substantive + any intervening modifier (*La Grande Illusion*);
    modifier-initial titles the same (*Mauvais Sang*); all others, including *Un/Une* titles, sentence
    case ("Un cœur simple"). If adopted, use it consistently. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.31] French spacing** — French typesetting puts thin spaces before *:* *;* *?* *!*, between
  guillemets and the text, and after an em dash introducing dialogue. In an English context you need not
  reproduce this; if you do, it must be consistent and per French practice. **[MECHANICAL]** (if used)
- **[11.32] French guillemets** — « » often with a thin or nonbreaking space. If retained in English
  text, they may be spaced like ordinary quotation marks. Speech tags (*écrit-il*, *dit-elle*) go inside
  the quoted matter with no extra guillemets. Only punctuation belonging to the quoted matter goes
  inside the closing guillemet; other punctuation follows it. In a multi-paragraph quotation, opening
  guillemets appear at each new paragraph, closing guillemets only at the end.
  - ex: *«Mission accomplie?» a-t-il demandé.*
- **[11.33] Quotation within quotation (French)** — Use double (sometimes single) quotation marks inside
  guillemets; if two quotations end at the same point, only one set of closing guillemets appears.
  Regular quotation marks instead of guillemets are sometimes seen — especially in email and other
  electronic settings — and are considered informal.
- **[11.34] French dialogue uses em dashes** — A dash introduces each successive speech and is **not**
  repeated at the end of a speech (unlike Italian, 11.54); guillemets set off a quotation inside a
  speech. In English publications the space after the dash is unnecessary.
  - ex: *—Vous viendrez aussitôt que possible? a-t-il demandé. / —Tout de suite. / —Bien. Bonne
    chance!*
- **[11.35] French ellipses** — Three unspaced dots closed up to the preceding word (*comme ça...*); in
  an English context they may be spaced per 12.59–69. Omissions: dots in brackets, with thin spaces
  inside the brackets in French practice; in English, spaced periods with **no** space between bracket
  and periods (12.67).
- **[11.36–11.39] French word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped, with two usable cautions:
  never divide immediately after an apostrophe (*jusqu'au*, *aujourd'hui*), and leave words of four or
  fewer letters undivided. Diphthongs are never split.
- **[11.40] Keep French accents and the œ ligature, including on capitals** — Needed characters:
  *à â æ ç é è ê ë î ï ô œ ù û ü*. French publishers have often dropped accents on capitals and set *œ*
  as *oe*; in English-language contexts "they should be retained wherever needed." The Académie
  française advocates this, and it helps readers unfamiliar with French typography. **[MECHANICAL]**

### German (11.41–11.48)

- **[11.41] Quote German as spelled in the source** — The 1998 orthography (mandatory in schools and
  public documents from 2005, later revised) is standard here, but some publishers keep traditional
  rules or a hybrid. "Material quoted from German should therefore reflect the spelling in the source."
  Consult a post-1998 Duden. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.42] Capitalize all German nouns** — Every noun and every word used as a noun is capitalized, in
  sentences and in titles (11.8): *ein Haus*, *die Weltanschauung*, *das Sein*, *etwas Schönes*;
  *Deutsch* (the language) but *eine Deutsche* (a German woman). Adjectives from proper names are
  generally lowercase (*die deutsche Literatur*, *die platonischen Dialoge*), except invariable *-er*
  adjectives (*eine berühmte Berliner Straße*) and adjectives that are part of a proper name (*der Nahe
  Osten*, *der Deutsch-Französische Krieg*). Capitalize the polite second-person *Sie, Ihr, Ihnen*;
  lowercase third-person *sie*. The familiar forms *du, dich, dein, ihr, euch* are now lowercase.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.43] German apostrophes** — Use an apostrophe for the colloquial dropped *e* (*wie geht's*,
  *was gibt's*, *hab' ich*) and for the omitted genitive *s* after names ending in an s sound (*Alice'
  Geburtstag*, *Cixous' Theaterstücke*, *Jaspers' Philosophie*, *Leibniz' Meinung*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.44] German quotation marks** — Native marks are reversed guillemets (» «), split-level marks
  (,, "), or, in Switzerland, ordinary guillemets (« »); single versions for quotations within
  quotations. Other punctuation goes **outside** the closing mark unless it belongs to the quoted
  matter. In English context, English quotation marks may replace them (11.21). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.45–11.47] German word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped. One usable caution: never break
  after a single vowel at the beginning or end of a word (*aber*, *Ofen*, *Treue*); the digraphs *ch,
  ck, ph, sch, th* stay together.
- **[11.48] German special characters** — You need *ä ö ü ß*. Do **not** substitute *ae/oe/ue*: umlauted
  characters are universally available. Do not confuse *ß* with the Greek beta *β*; the capital *ẞ*
  (U+1E9E) was officially adopted in 2017. New spelling replaces *ß* with *ss* in certain words (check a
  post-1998 dictionary); Swiss German rarely uses *ß*. **[MECHANICAL]**

### Indigenous Languages (11.49–11.52)

- **[11.49] Defer to the community, and capitalize what it capitalizes** — Writers and editors need
  knowledge of the language or an expert. "Editors should defer to an author's usage preferences
  whenever possible." English-style capitalization may be appropriate for proper nouns, but note that
  in English many terms are capitalized **in reference to Indigenous peoples and cultures** — e.g.
  *Elder*, *Longhouse* (the institution, not the building), *Oral Tradition*; see also *Indigenous*
  (8.39) and Gregory Younging, *Elements of Indigenous Style*. Diacritics matter especially in names of
  people and places: the macron for vowel length in te reo Māori is now more or less standard
  (*Whangārei*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.50] Learn the community's writing system; expect no single standard** — Many Indigenous writing
  systems derive from the Latin alphabet, and communities may use more than one. *Tohono O'odham* has
  officially adopted the Alvarez–Hale system (long vowels written with a colon) while significant work
  also appears in the Saxton system (vowel + *h*); neighboring Akimel O'odham communities differ again
  (*w* vs. *v*). "Indigenous languages may not have standard spellings for words," and communities'
  notions of correct/incorrect may not match English ones. Not every Latin-based orthography uses case:
  in the Coeur d'Alene Tribe's community orthography there are **no uppercase letters**, and any
  capitalization is a typographical error. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.51] Navajo (Diné Bizaad): keep the diacritics and the apostrophes** — Written Navajo uses
  accented and doubled vowels and two-letter consonants. Long vowels are doubled. Acute accents mark
  tone: a single or double acute marks high tone; on a doubled vowel a single accent marks a rising or
  falling tone. An ogonek marks nasality. *Ł* is a voiceless consonant; apostrophes mark glottal stops
  and are **part of the letters** *ch'*, *k'*, *t'*, *tt'*, *ts'*. A modifier letter apostrophe (U+02BC)
  may be used to distinguish the glottal mark from punctuation. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Diné Bizaad* ("the people's language"), *Diné* ("the people")
- **[11.52] The authority is the nation or tribe, not the writer** — For works by or about Indigenous
  peoples, "the accepted authority on language presentation, usage, spelling, and the like is usually
  not the individual writer (particularly if the writer is not a member of the community) but rather
  the applicable nation, tribe, or other governmental entity." Many Indigenous, First Nations, and
  Native American governments have language and culture departments; abide by their specifications.
  Community members may need to work differently from outsiders; practices should reflect the specific
  community's needs, preferences, and rights (see 14.136–37). **[JUDGMENT]**

### Italian (11.53–11.60)

- **[11.53] Italian capitalization** — Lowercase a title before a proper name: *il commendatore Ugo
  Emiliano*, *la signora Rossi*. In correspondence, capitalize the formal pronouns *Lei* (sing.),
  *Voi* (pl.), and the object forms *La, Le, Vi*; likewise the older *Ella (Le, La)* and *Loro (Loro,
  Loro)* — even in combined forms: *Posso pregarLa di farmi una cortesia?* *Vorrei darLe una
  spiegazione.* **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.54] Italian quotations and dialogue** — Guillemets « » usually with **no** space inside.
  Ordinary quotation marks (double or single) are also common, sometimes as scare quotes in the same
  text that uses guillemets for quotation. Periods and commas go **after** the closing guillemet or
  quotation mark. Dialogue may use em dashes: a dash before each successive speech, and — unlike French
  — another dash at the end of the speech if other matter follows in the same paragraph. Spaces around
  the dashes need not be used in English contexts.
  - ex: *—Avremo la neve,—annunziò la vecchia.*
- **[11.55] Italian apostrophes** — An apostrophe marks omitted letters. Put a **space** after an
  apostrophe that follows a vowel (*po' duro*, *de' malevoli*); **no space** after one that follows a
  consonant (*l'onda*, *all'aura*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.56] Italian ellipses** — Used for interruptions and breaks in thought; dots in brackets for
  omitted material. Italian typography prefers unspaced dots, but in English publications use spaced
  periods (12.59–69).
- **[11.57–11.59] Italian word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped, with one usable caution: never
  divide immediately after an apostrophe (*dal-l'accusa*, *quel-l'uomo*).
- **[11.60] Italian special characters and the grave accent** — Needed: *à è é ì ó ù*. The grave accent
  on capitalized vowels is sometimes dropped, but **in stressed final syllables it must be kept** to
  avoid ambiguity: *CANTO* "he sang" vs. *CANTÒ* "I sang"; *PAPA* "daddy" vs. *PAPÀ* "pope". Older texts
  use an apostrophe for the accent (*E'* for *È*, *PAPA'* for *PAPÀ*); retain that in direct quotations.
  **[MECHANICAL]**

### Latin (11.61–11.67)

- **[11.61] Latin titles: sentence case for ancient and medieval works, title case for modern** —
  Ancient/medieval: capitalize only first word, proper nouns, and proper adjectives: *De bello Gallico*,
  *De viris illustribus*, *Cur Deus homo?*. Renaissance and modern works, or English works with Latin
  titles, may take title case: *Novum Organum*, *Religio Medici*. If the era is uncertain, sentence case.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.62–11.66] Latin word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped. (Latin syllables = vowels or
  diphthongs; *ch, gu, ph, qu, th* count as single consonants.)
- **[11.67] Latin special characters** — None for ordinary copy. Elementary texts may mark long vowels
  with a macron and short vowels with a breve: *ā ă ē ĕ ī ĭ ō ŏ ū ŭ*. Do not add macrons to Latin in
  general prose. **[MECHANICAL]**

### Spanish (11.68–11.76)

- **[11.68] Spanish capitalization** — Lowercase a title before a name (*el señor Jaime López*, *la
  señora Lucía Moyado de Barba*, *doña Perfecta*) but capitalize abbreviated titles (*el Sr. López*).
  Lowercase nouns and adjectives of nationality (*los mexicanos*, *la lengua española*) but capitalize
  country names (*Inglaterra*). Capitalize organizations and institutions, historical events, buildings,
  and streets: *Real Academia Española*, *Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México*, *Plaza del Dos de
  Mayo*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.69] Keep the inverted question and exclamation marks** — *¿Qué pasa, amigo?* *¡Olvídalo en ese
  caso!* A preceding vocative or dependent clause stands **outside** the marks: *Amigo, ¿qué pasa?* *En
  ese caso, ¡olvídalo!* These opening marks are integral to Spanish punctuation and are **retained** when
  Spanish is quoted in English; **omitted** when the passage is translated. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.70] Spanish guillemets and quotation marks** — Guillemets « » traditionally; only punctuation
  belonging to the quoted matter goes inside the closing mark. Em dashes may set off speaker
  identification inside a quotation (in Spanish publications the opening dash is spaced; English contexts
  need not space them). Quotations within quotations use ordinary quotation marks. In lexical studies,
  glosses take single quotation marks with **no** preceding punctuation: *courteous* 'cortés'. Many
  Spanish publications now use ordinary quotation marks throughout; the punctuation rules stay the same.
- **[11.71] Spanish dialogue** — An em dash introduces each successive speech; matter following the
  speech in the same paragraph is generally preceded by a dash or a comma.
  - ex: *—Esto es el arca de Noé, afirmó el estanciero. / —¿Por qué estás aquí todavía?—pregunté Juana
    alarmada.*
- **[11.72] Spanish ellipses** — For interruptions and breaks in thought; unspaced in Spanish
  publications, may be spaced in English contexts (12.59–69); brackets for omitted material.
- **[11.73–11.75] Spanish word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped, with one orthographic fact:
  *ch*, *ll*, and *rr* represent single sounds and are **never divided** (though *ch* and *ll* are now
  alphabetized as two-letter groups).
- **[11.76] Spanish special characters** — Needed: *á é í ñ ó ú ü* (and capitals *Á É Í Ñ Ó Ú Ü*). Note
  *ñ* is a separate letter, not *n* plus a diacritic. **[MECHANICAL]**

### Other Languages Using the Latin Alphabet (11.77)

- **[11.77] Language-by-language cautions** (all in one rule, alphabetically):
  - *Albanian*: unified orthography since 1972; for older texts, decide whether a spelling reflects its
    period or should follow the current norm.
  - *Croatian, Bosnian, Serbian, Montenegrin*: the successor languages of Serbo-Croatian distinguish
    *đ* from *dj* consistently in standard practice (*dj* appears only informally); Serbian uses
    Cyrillic officially but Latin as regulated; Montenegrin needs *ś* and *ź* in addition.
  - *Czech*: many diacritics; the single-glyph *ď* and *ť* are often written with an apostrophe instead.
  - *Danish*: capitalize the polite *De, Dem, Deres* — do not confuse with third-person *de, dem,
    deres*; common nouns were capitalized until the mid-twentieth century.
  - *Dutch*: capitalize both letters of initial *IJ* (*IJsland*); a one-letter word opening a sentence
    stays lowercase and the next word is capitalized (*'k Heb niet...*); particles in names per 8.11.
  - *Finnish*: alphabet includes Swedish *å*.
  - *Hawaiian*: five macroned vowels plus the *‘okina*, a glottal stop written as a left single
    quotation mark (U+2018) — *Hawai‘i*; some prefer U+02BB. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - *Hungarian*: many accented vowels, including double acutes (*ő, ű*).
  - *Icelandic*: *ð* (eth) and *þ* (thorn); *Ð* is capitalized only in all-caps contexts; *á é í ó ú ý
    æ ö*.
  - *Norwegian*: capitalize polite *De, Dem, Deres* (vs. *de, dem, deres*).
  - *Polish*: capitalize *Państwo* and related forms in formal address (*Czekam na Twój przyjazd*;
    *Pozdrawiam Cię!*); the conjunction *i* must never end a line.
  - *Portuguese*: capitalize titles and nationality words as in Spanish; **accented capitals must always
    be used** in an English context even though Portuguese running text sometimes drops them.
    **[MECHANICAL]**
  - *Romanian, Moldovan*: *ș* and *ț* take a **comma below**, not a cedilla; *î* and *â* represent the
    same sound with different histories — check whether a spelling reflects its period or current norms.
  - *Swedish*: *Ni* and *Er*, once capitalized in correspondence, are now lowercase everywhere.
  - *Turkish, Azeri*: *İ* retains its dot when capitalized and must not be confused with dotless *ı*
    (a separate letter); months and days **are** capitalized in Turkish; Azeri adds *ə, q, x*.
  - *Turkmen, Uzbek*: Uzbek needs no special characters besides the left single quotation mark in
    *o‘, g‘*.

## Languages Usually Transliterated (or Romanized) (11.78–11.119)

- **[11.78] Transliterate, but never guess** — In nonspecialized work it is customary to romanize words
  and phrases from non-Latin scripts. Use the ALA-LC *Romanization Tables* and Daniels & Bright, *The
  World's Writing Systems*. "**Do not attempt to transliterate from a language unfamiliar to you.**"
  Capitalization (11.20), punctuation (11.21), and word division (11.22) rules apply to transliterated
  text exactly as to Latin-alphabet languages.
- **[11.79] Choose a Unicode font** — Modern software accepts non-Latin alphabets; pick a font that
  includes the needed Unicode characters, in consultation with the publisher (see 2.19).
- **[11.80] Do not proofread a script you cannot read** — "Anyone unfamiliar with a language that uses a
  non-Latin alphabet should exercise extreme caution in proofreading even single words set in that
  alphabet"; similar characters are easily mistaken for one another. Query the author; check the Unicode
  name and number when referring to a character. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.81] Use the minimum diacritics the audience needs** — Except in linguistic or other highly
  specialized works, "a system using as few diacritics as are needed to aid pronunciation is easier on
  readers, publisher, and author." Prefer *Shiva* to *Śiva* and *Vishnu* to *Viṣṇu* for general readers;
  for nonspecialized works, the diacritic-free transliterations listed in Merriam-Webster are usually
  preferred. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.82] Italicize transliterated terms, not names** — Transliterated terms other than proper names
  that have not entered English are italic (but see 11.4); a term used throughout may be italic at first
  appearance and roman thereafter. Dictionary words are roman.
  - ex: "The preacher pointed out the distinction between agape and eros." — *agape* and *eros* roman,
    as dictionary words.
  - ex: "Once the Greek words erōs and agapē had been absorbed into the English language, it became
    unnecessary to italicize them or to use the macrons."

### Arabic (11.83–11.88)

- **[11.83] Pick one Arabic system and stay with it** — There is no universally accepted transliteration;
  ALA-LC and the *International Journal of Middle East Studies* system are both acceptable. "Having
  selected a system, an author should stick to it with as few exceptions as possible."
- **[11.84] Distinguish hamza from ‘ayn every time** — Hamza (ʾ) and ‘ayn (ʿ) are different characters
  (U+02BE, U+02BF), and writers "must on every occurrence make it clear, by coding or by careful
  instructions," which is meant. Nonspecialized works often use an apostrophe for hamza (*Qur'an*) and a
  left single quotation mark for ‘ayn; use the latter with caution since ‘ayn often begins a word. Most
  systems drop an initial hamza (*anzala*, not *'anzala*).
- **[11.85] Use the familiar English form of famous names** — *Avicenna* (not *Ibn Sina*), *Damascus*
  (not *Dimashq*), *Mecca* (not *Makka*/*Makkah*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.86] Hyphenate the definite article** — Chicago recommends joining *al* to the noun with a hyphen:
  *al-Islam*, *al-Nafud*, *Bahr al-Safi*, *al-Qaeda* (or *al-Qaida*); an exception may be made for a
  personal name better known without it (8.15). Where the sound matters (e.g., transliterating poetry),
  show assimilation: *an-Nafud*, *Bahr as-Safi*. Some authors elide the *a* after a long syllable
  (*Abu 'l-Muhallab*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.87] Capitalize only the first word and proper nouns** — Arabic script has no case, so practice
  varies; Chicago applies 11.8 to titles and to names of journals and organizations. *al-* may stay
  lowercase at the start of a title while the noun it joins is capitalized.
  - ex: *Avicenna's* Kitab al-shifa'*, known in English as* The Book of Healing
- **[11.88] Arabic word division and direction** — Avoid breaking transliterated Arabic; if unavoidable,
  break after *al* or *Ibn*, or after two letters when the second has an underdot. Never break the
  digraphs *dh, gh, kh, sh, th* (unless both letters carry underdots), and never before or after a
  hamza. Untransliterated Arabic reads right to left; if a phrase breaks across lines, each line is
  still read right to left.

### Chinese, Japanese, and Korean (11.89–11.99)

- **[11.89] Use Hanyu Pinyin for Chinese** — Pinyin has largely supplanted Wade-Giles and Postal Atlas
  spellings; "Chicago joins librarians in urging that Pinyin now be used in all writing about China or
  the Chinese language." You may add the Wade-Giles spelling in parentheses at first use. Use ALA-LC
  Pinyin tables with caution if you don't know Chinese.
- **[11.90] Keep only genuinely established older spellings** — A few place-names, personal names, and
  proper nouns long familiar in older forms may stay that way (or the old spelling may follow the Pinyin
  in parentheses). Check Merriam-Webster; names not listed there in older form take Pinyin. Editors who
  want to change spellings must consult the author. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.91] Chinese apostrophes, hyphens, tone marks** — Personal names are usually spelled without
  apostrophes or hyphens; an apostrophe may separate syllables that would otherwise merge (*Xi'an* vs.
  *Xian*). The Library of Congress Pinyin system has **no tone marks**, and most English-language
  publications omit them; tone marks are appropriate in textbooks.
- **[11.92] Common name conversions (Wade-Giles → Pinyin)** — Dynasties: *Chou → Zhou*, *Chin → Qin*,
  *Ching → Qing*, *Sung → Song*, *Tang → Tang*, *Yuan → Yuan*. Personal names: *Fang Li-chih → Fang
  Lizhi*, *Hua Kuo-feng → Hua Guofeng*, *Lin Piao → Lin Biao*, *Lu Hsün → Lu Xun*, *Mao Tse-tung → Mao
  Zedong*, *Teng Hsiao-ping → Deng Xiaoping*. *Sun Yat-sen* and *Chiang Kai-shek* keep the old spellings.
  Places: *Kuang-tung/Kwangtung → Guangdong*, *Pei-ching/Peking → Beijing*, *Su-chou/Soochow → Suzhou*,
  *Ta-lien/Dairen → Dalian*. **[MECHANICAL]** (substitution list)
- **[11.93] Use modified Hepburn for Japanese** — Written Japanese mixes *kanji* with two *kana*
  syllabaries; modified Hepburn (*hyōjun*) is the standard romanization, used by Japanese-English
  dictionaries and the Library of Congress.
- **[11.94] Hepburn diacritics** — Apostrophe after syllabic *n* before a vowel or *y*: *Gen'e*, *San'yō*.
  Macron over long vowels (usually *ō, ū*) in all words **except** well-known place-names (*Tokyo*,
  *Hokkaido*, *Kobe*) and words in English (*shogun*, *daimyō*); macrons may be restored when
  pronunciation matters. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.95] Know which Korean system you are in** — Korean is written in Hangul; romanization systems
  differ sharply. McCune-Reischauer (1930s) was replaced in 2000 by **Revised Romanization** as South
  Korea's official system, but MR remains the basis of the Library of Congress table and is the most
  widely used system in North America; North Korea uses a version of MR; linguists often prefer Yale.
- **[11.96] Revised vs. McCune-Reischauer forms** — Revised Romanization uses no diacritics or
  apostrophes; hyphens separate ambiguous syllables (*jung-ang*) but are rare in place-names (*Jungang*
  line), common in given names (*Chang Wang-rok*, strictly *Wangrok*; see 8.18), and used before *do*,
  *si*, etc. (*Uijeongbu-si*). MR uses apostrophes for aspirated *ch', k', p', t'* and breves for rounded
  vowels (*ŏ, ŭ*); Revised writes *eo, eu*. *Seoul* is *Sŏul* in MR. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.97] Capitals and italics in romanized CJK** — Chinese, Japanese, and Korean have no capital
  letters, but romanization introduces capitals where English would use them (ch. 8). Capitalize personal
  names and place-names; in hyphenated names capitalize only the first element in Chinese, though both
  may be capitalized in Japanese (and sometimes Korean). Lowercase **and italicize** common nouns used in
  an English sentence (11.3, 11.6). Names of institutions, schools of thought, religions, and official
  titles are capitalized if roman, lowercased if italic.
  - ex: Donglin Academy; the Donglin movement (roman, capitalized)
  - ex: Buddhism, Taoism, *feng shui* [see 7.56], and other forms… (religions capitalized roman; a
    borrowed common noun lowercase and italic)
  - ex: the Board of War (*bingbu*); the Seiyōkai and Minseitō
  - ex: "It was Shogun Ieyasu (the shogun were military governors in Japan until 1868) who said …" —
    *shogun* is both singular and plural. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.98] Titles of CJK works** — Books and periodicals italic; articles roman in quotation marks
  (8.157–204). Capitalize the first word of a romanized title and proper nouns. Supply a bracketed
  English translation for titles in notes and bibliographies.
  - ex: *Chen Shiqi,* Mingdai guan shougongye de yanjiu *[Studies on government-operated handicrafts
    during the Ming dynasty]*
- **[11.99] Non-Latin characters in citations** — Chinese, Japanese, and Korean characters may follow the
  romanized form to help readers identify a work; they are largely confined to bibliographies and
  glossaries, and in running text may be parenthesized (not required). Alert the publisher to the font
  needs (11.2).

### Hebrew (11.100–11.106)

- **[11.100] Any Hebrew romanization, used consistently** — Several systems are acceptable, including
  ALA-LC; consistency is the author's responsibility.
- **[11.101] Hebrew diacritics** — In specialist material: underdots (*ḥ, ḳ, ṭ, ṿ*), macrons (*ā, ē, ī,
  ō, ū*), acute (*ś*), hačeks (*ǎ, ě, ǒ, š*), superscript schwa; *'alef* and *'ayin* are handled like
  Arabic hamza and ‘ayn (11.84); a prime may separate consonants that would read as a digraph.
- **[11.102] Hebrew prefixes** — Prepositions, conjunctions, and the article attach as prefixes; some
  writers mark them with an apostrophe or hyphen in romanization, some don't (Hebrew uses no marker).
  "Either approach is acceptable if used consistently." **[MECHANICAL]** (consistency check)
- **[11.103] Hebrew capitalization and italics** — The script has no capitals and no universally used
  system for romanized capitalization: follow normal English usage (proper names, book titles — 11.8,
  11.20), or eschew capitalization, "as always, the author must ensure internal consistency." Italics
  follow English usage (11.10).
- **[11.104] Hebrew word division** — Follow 7.36–48 for romanized Hebrew; at a double consonant, split
  one consonant per line: *Rosh Ha-shana*, *Yom Kip-pur*.
- **[11.105] Unromanized Hebrew in an English sentence** — Keep Hebrew word order right-to-left within
  the sentence; if a line break falls inside the phrase, each line is still read right to left (so the
  visual order looks reversed). Highlight the Hebrew and give the typesetter explicit line-break
  instructions.
- **[11.106] Hebrew vowels** — Most vowels are marks attached to consonants, not letters; they rarely
  appear outside prayer books, printed Bibles, and poetry. Do not "restore" them in ordinary prose.

### Russian (11.107–11.117)

- **[11.107] Use the US Board on Geographic Names system for general readers** — Journals of Slavic
  studies prefer a "linguistic" system rich in diacritics and ligatures; for a general audience, avoid
  diacritics and ligatures and follow BGN. Whatever the system, "the spellings for names listed in
  Merriam-Webster should prevail": *Catherine the Great*, *Dnieper River*, *Nizhniy* (or *Nizhni*),
  *Novgorod*, *Chekhov*, *Moscow*, *Tchaikovsky*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.108] Russian capitalization resembles French** — Lowercase pronouns, days of the week, months,
  and most proper adjectives; capitalize geographic designations only when they name formal institutions
  or political units: *Tverskaya guberniya* but *tverskoye zemstvo*; *Moskovskiy universitet*;
  *russkiy kompozitor*. Preserve these conventions in transliteration. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.109] Titles of Russian works** — Capitalize only the first word and proper nouns: *Lenin i
  Trotskiy*; *Kak ya vosprinimayu i predstavlyayu okruzhayushchiy mir*. In Cyrillic, titles are set in
  ordinary type (Cyrillic *kursiv* is used sparingly and never for book titles), but **transliterated
  titles take italic**. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.110] Russian quotations and dialogue** — Guillemets « » for quotations; dashes for dialogue, as
  in French (11.32, 11.34); guillemets for a quotation within a speech.
  - ex (transliterated): *«Bozhe, bozhe, bozhe!» govorit Boris.* / *—S kem ya rabotayu? /
    —S tovarishchem.*
- **[11.111] Russian ellipses** — As in French (11.35), for interruptions and breaks in thought. Russian
  often puts *!* or *?* in place of one of the dots (*Mitya! ... Gde vy byli?...*); this may be
  regularized to three dots in English publications.
- **[11.112] The Russian dash** — A spaced dash replaces an omitted *is/are* between subject and
  complement (*Moskva — stolitsa Rossii*) and an omitted repeated verb (*Ivan i Sonya poyedut v Moskvu
  poyezdom, Lev i Lyuba — avtobusom*). Keep it when quoting; do not convert it to a colon.
- **[11.113–11.117] Russian word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped. Two cautions: never divide
  the combinations that spell single Cyrillic letters (*ch, kh, sh, shch, ts, ya, ye, yé, yu, zh*) or a
  vowel plus *y*; divide compounds and prefixed words between their parts (*bes-poryadok*,
  *radio-priyomnik*), not inside the prefix.

### South Asian Languages (11.118–11.119)

- **[11.118] Simplified transliteration is acceptable** — Full transliteration may need underdots,
  macrons, and acute accents; many writers use a simplified style with no diacritics at all — *sh* for
  the various sibilants, no underdots for dentals, no macrons: *Shiva* not *Śiva*, *Vishnu* not *Viṣṇu*,
  *apsara* not *apsarā*. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.119] Capitalization and titles of South Asian works** — The writing systems have no case, but
  romanized text may be capitalized by English conventions for proper nouns; titles take sentence case
  (11.8). Some authors prefer title case, especially in simplified transliteration (*Aksharon kay
  Saayee*, with the Hindi postposition *kay* kept lowercase); others capitalize every word. Editors may
  query apparent inconsistencies "but should otherwise follow the author's lead." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Amrita Pritam's works include the novel* The Thirteenth Sun *(Terhawarm suraja) and the
    autobiographical* Shadows of Words *(Aksharom ke sāye).*

## Classical Greek (11.120–11.131)

- **[11.120] Transliterate isolated Greek; omit accents; mark long vowels** — Isolated Greek words and
  phrases in works not about ancient Greece are usually transliterated. In transliteration **all Greek
  accents are dropped**; a macron distinguishes long *eta* (*ē*) and *omega* (*ō*) from short *epsilon*
  (*e*) and *omicron* (*o*); the iota subscript becomes *i* on the line (*anthrōpoi*); rough breathing
  becomes *h* before a vowel or diphthong and **after** *r* (*rhythm*); smooth breathing is ignored; a
  diaeresis in the Greek is kept. Transliterated Greek is italic unless the words recur, in which case
  italicize first mention only. **[MECHANICAL]** (macron/breathing pattern)
- **[11.121] Typesetting Greek** — Use a Unicode Greek font; Greek is normally **not** italicized when
  set in Greek type (but see 7.73); extra space may be needed over vowels carrying more than one
  diacritic.
- **[11.122] Breathings are obligatory in Greek type** — Every initial vowel, initial diphthong, and
  initial rho carries a rough (῾) or smooth (᾿) breathing, placed over a lowercase vowel or the second
  vowel of a diphthong and to the left of a capital. "A single quotation mark cannot function as a
  breathing" — use the real character. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.123] Greek accents** — Three: acute (*oxia*), circumflex (*perispomeni*), grave (*varia*); only
  over vowels; circumflex only on the last two syllables; grave only on the last; placed like breathings.
  A diaeresis marks two vowels pronounced separately.
- **[11.124] Some Greek words carry no accent** — Proclitics (forms of the definite article, the
  prepositions *eis, en, ek*, the conjunctions *ei, hōs*, the adverb *ou*) and enclitics (which usually
  lose their accent, sometimes throwing a second accent onto the preceding word).
- **[11.125] Greek vowels with breathings and accents** — Every publishing font must supply the full set
  (e.g., for lowercase eta and uppercase eta) in all combinations; scholarly work on manuscripts needs
  further symbols. *(Font checklist, not prose.)*
- **[11.126] Greek punctuation** — Period and comma as in English; colon **and** semicolon are both the
  raised dot (*ano teleia*, U+0387); the **question mark is a semicolon**; the apostrophe (*coronis*)
  marks elision. In English texts, quoted words or passages set in the Greek alphabet are **not**
  enclosed in quotation marks, at any length. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.127] Greek numerals** — Alphabetic numerals with the numeral sign; table 11.5. *(Not
  writing-relevant; convert to Arabic numerals in English prose.)*
- **[11.128–11.131] Greek word division** — Typesetting detail; skipped. (Diphthongs never split;
  single consonant goes with the following vowel.)

## Old English and Middle English (11.132–11.134)

- **[11.132] Use the real characters, not digraphs** — Old and Middle English use *ð/Ð* (eth), *þ/Þ*
  (thorn), *ȝ/Ȝ* (yogh), and the *æ/Æ* ligature. Eth and thorn both represent *th*; yogh represents *g*,
  *y*, or *gh* depending on period; *æ* "should not be printed as two letters in Old English names and
  text" (*Ælfric*). Use the correct Unicode characters and list them for the publisher (11.2).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.133] Ampersand and wynn** — The Tironian *et* (⁊, U+204A) may be replaced by the modern
  ampersand; Old English *ƿ* (wynn) may be replaced by modern *w*.
- **[11.134] Old English vowel length** — Modern editors may mark long vowels and diphthongs with a
  macron (*ā, ǣ, ē, ēa, ī, īo, ō, ū, ȳ*); with the exception of *ǣ*, mark a diphthong with a macron over
  the **first** vowel only. Do not add macrons to Modern English quotations.

## American Sign Language (ASL) (11.135–11.145)

- **[11.135] Signed languages are languages** — Visual-gestural and tactile languages of deaf and
  deafblind people; they are "quite different from spoken languages" and need not share national
  boundaries with them. Their lexical elements are **signs**; deafblind users also use tactile forms and
  Protactile.
- **[11.136] Five components of a sign** — Handshape, location, orientation, movement, and (sometimes)
  nonmanual signals. *(Descriptive; no prose rule.)*
- **[11.137] No standard written form** — Formal notation systems exist, but none is in widespread use
  among deaf signers; transcription conventions vary. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[11.138] Gloss signs in small capitals; hyphenate multiword glosses; translate in quotation marks** —
  A gloss is the written-language transcription of a sign, set in small caps (regular caps acceptable):
  *WOMAN*, *SCHOOL*, *CAT*. When one sign needs several words, join them with hyphens; put the
  translation in double quotation marks.
  - ex: *The sign for "a car drove by" is written as* VEHICLE-DRIVE-BY*.* **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[11.139] Compound signs** — Mark with a close-up mark or a plus sign: *MOTHER+FATHER* ("parents").
- **[11.140] Fingerspelling** — Transcribe as *fs-JOHN*, *J-O-H-N*, or *j-o-h-n*.
- **[11.141] Lexicalized signs** — Fingerspelled forms that have become distinct signs take the pound
  sign: *#WHAT*, *#BACK*, *#DO*.
- **[11.142] Handshape labels** — Describe with the corresponding manual-alphabet or number handshape
  (*APPLE* = X handshape; *CREATE* = 4 handshape; *YELL* = Bent 5; *SARCASTIC* = HORNS handshape).
- **[11.143] Transcribe signed sentences as gloss sequences, with the translation beneath** — The
  translation appears underneath in italics, quotation marks, or both. Omit punctuation from the gloss
  line (not from the translation), though some writers add *?* and *!* and a comma for a short pause.
- **[11.144] Pronouns and reference in ASL transcription** — Gloss pronouns as *IX* or *PRO* plus person
  (and number), possessives as *POSS*; subscript indices mark coreference: *IX3p_i LOSE POSS3p_i HOUSE* =
  "He lost his house."
- **[11.145] Nonmanual signals** — Label them in a smaller font on a rule above the gloss line (*whq* for
  the question face, *t* for topic); "Correct alignment is critical to an accurate transcription."
  *(Specialist notation.)*

## Decision procedure for a writing agent (synthesis of 11.3–11.23)

1. **Is it a proper noun?** → roman, no italics, keep the source language's capitalization (11.5).
   If you are glossing it, still roman (11.5).
2. **Is it a title?** → sentence case; keep native punctuation except the permitted changes; italic
   or roman by work type, not by language (11.8–11.10). Add an English gloss in parentheses only if the
   reader needs it (11.11–11.12).
3. **Is it a common noun or phrase?** → italic on first use, roman thereafter if it recurs; keep source
   capitalization and source plural (11.3). In fiction/dialogue, prefer roman for what the speaker would
   naturally say (11.4).
4. **Does it need a gloss?** → parentheses or quotation marks; italic inside parentheses if it is a
   common noun; do not double the quotation marks (11.6, 11.14).
5. **Does it contain an accent, diacritic, ligature, or non-Latin letter?** → restore it; never ASCII-fold
   (11.23, 11.40, 11.48, 11.60, 11.76, 11.77). **[MECHANICAL]**
6. **Is it a full sentence?** → roman, in quotation marks unless set off as a block (11.3, 11.13).
7. **Are you translating?** → credit the translator; flag modifications; never retranslate a translation
   (11.16–11.19).
