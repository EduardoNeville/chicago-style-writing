# Writing and revising prose: clarity, syntax, grammar, word usage

Source: CMOS ch 2 (manuscript editing), ch 5 (Grammar and Usage, Bryan A. Garner). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch02, ch05a, ch05b. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch02

1. **[2.57]** Apply a light hand: change only what is wrong or inconsistent; suggest rather than impose when a choice is defensible. "A light editorial hand is nearly always more effective than a heavy one."
2. **[2.57]** Respect the author's voice — "whether it is baroque or minimalist" — and never "mindlessly enforce 'the rules'" against style, narrator voice, quoted speech, or genre convention.
3. **[2.57]** Do not tamper with "unusual figures of speech or idiomatic usage"; know the difference between making a change and suggesting one.
4. **[2.56]** Fix ambiguity and looseness by rewriting for word choice, reorganizing, and tightening — but only with agreement when the change is substantive.
5. **[2.123]** At proof stage rewrite nothing beyond "the correction of fact or of gross syntactical error"; all stylistic work belongs at the editing stage.
6. **[2.78]** Except for style adjustments, "the author's version should prevail"; if unacceptable, seek a compromise.
7. **[2.49]** The only silent corrections allowed to previously published text: renumber notes, delete obsolete cross-references, fix "obvious typographical errors and minor grammatical slips that were clearly not intended."
8. **[2.75]** Impose consistent style and correct errors without comment; query only what the author must decide, concisely and without sounding "casual, pedantic, condescending, or indignant"; "often, a simple 'OK?' is enough."
9. **[2.11]** One space between sentences and after colons — never two. **MECHANICAL**
10. **[2.13]** Flush left, ragged right in manuscript; no justification before typesetting. **MECHANICAL**
11. **[2.16]** No optional/end-of-line hyphens; never hyphen-break URLs. **MECHANICAL**
12. **[2.17]** Em dashes unspaced as a single character; en dashes only for ranges and certain compounds. **MECHANICAL**
13. **[2.18]** Real italics, not underlining; boldface only where it must print. **MECHANICAL**
14. **[2.21]/[2.64]** Title case for chapter/part titles, matching the TOC exactly; upper- and lowercase, never full caps. **MECHANICAL**
15. **[2.22]/[2.65]** Subheads on their own lines, one consistent case per level, parallel in structure and tone, no terminal periods. **MECHANICAL + JUDGMENT**
16. **[2.40]** Cross-reference by chapter/section number, never by page number, and avoid gratuitous "see chapter 2 above." **JUDGMENT**
17. **[2.37]/[2.38]** Verify quotations against sources and cross-check every title, caption, note, citation, and link before calling a manuscript final.
18. **[2.70]** Don't impose a new citation style on a consistent, clear existing one — consult first. **JUDGMENT**
19. **[2.67]** "the editor must do nothing to quoted material" beyond house-standard quotation marks and ellipses; query errors instead of fixing silently.
20. **[2.27]** Glossary/list-of-abbreviations punctuation: one separator (period, colon, or em dash) used consistently; definitions capitalized; terminal periods. **MECHANICAL**
21. **[2.29]** Abstracts: one paragraph, ≤250 words, no tables/lists/illustrations/notes; they become promotional copy, so write them carefully.
22. **[2.30]** Keywords: 5–10 single words or short compounds, drawn from the abstract, never unrelated to the subject.
23. **[2.85]** Run the 18-step cleanup in order and review every global change before saving: double spaces, stray tabs, multiple hard returns, underlining, quote/apostrophe direction, comma–quote order, em dash/ellipsis regularity, numeral hyphens→en dashes, optional hyphens, l/1 and O/0 confusion, closed-up initials. **MECHANICAL**
24. **[2.115]** Proofread against copy ("cold read" with the manuscript at hand for questions) and "mark only the proofs, never the manuscript."
25. **[2.121]** Watch the typographic killers: no page ending on a subhead, no widow (short last line at the top of a page), no paragraph-final hyphen with fewer than four letters carried over, no more than three consecutive hyphenated line-ends.

### From ch05a

Ranked by (frequency × harm) for drafting or editing any prose.

1. **Subject–verb agreement with intervening material** (5.20, 5.143, 5.147, 5.192): the subject governs, not the nearest noun. Fix *the bride as well as her bridesmaids were* → *was*; *a group of cyclists are* → *is*; *the number of options keep* → *keeps*. Then recast if the singular sounds odd (5.147).
2. **Dangling participles and gerunds** (5.119, 5.120): every sentence-initial *-ing*/*-ed* phrase must be performed by the main clause's subject. Flag *Reviewing the suggestions, it is clear…* (5.119) and *While driving to San Antonio, my phone ran out of power* (5.120) — recast, don't patch.
3. **Dangling infinitives** (5.113): *To repair your car properly, it must be sent to a mechanic* → supply the actor (5.113).
4. **Pronoun case in compounds** (5.38, 5.40, 5.44, 5.47): *for you or I*, *tell Laura and I* → *me*. Apply the delete-the-compound test. Never "correct" toward the nominative.
5. **Misplaced adverbs, especially *only*** (5.172, 5.177): *only* modifies what immediately follows it; rigorous placement is mandatory in writing because intonation is unavailable.
6. **Misplaced modifier phrases (adverb/prepositional/relative)** (5.184, 5.63, 5.64): put the phrase next to what it modifies; a relative clause separated from its antecedent (*that runs through our neighborhood*) is a defect, and remote *which* is the classic ambiguity.
7. **Incomplete comparisons** (5.49, 5.92): *than I* vs. *than me* changes meaning; *as … as* needs both terms; a superlative needs three things, a comparative two.
8. ***One of those who* agreement** (5.66, 5.145): *one of those employees who work tirelessly* — plural verb, always.
9. **Singular *they*** (5.51, 5.52): Chicago endorses it; takes a plural verb even for one person (*they have a certificate*); respect stated pronouns; *themself* is available; never substitute *he or she*; neopronouns take singular verbs. Do not "fix" it.
10. **Possessive apostrophes, and the four homophones** (5.22, 5.54): *its/it's, your/you're, whose/who's, their/there/they're*. Mechanical, high frequency, safe to auto-correct after a clause check.
11. **Hyphenate compound modifiers before nouns** (5.96, 5.97): *small-animal hospital* vs. *small animal hospital*; suspended hyphen in *middle- and upper-class*; no hyphen after -ly adverbs or linking verbs; drop plurals inside (*3,000-pound truck*, but *two-thirds majority*).
12. **Coordinate vs. cumulative adjectives — the *and* test** (5.95): if *and* fits, add the comma; *a lethargic soccer player* and *white brick house* take none.
13. **Adjective after linking verbs, not adverb** (5.86, 5.175): *feels bad*, *tastes delicious*, *seems honest*; *feels badly* is an error.
14. **Passive → active when the actor matters** (5.122, 5.159, 5.198): *the matter will be given careful consideration* → *we will consider the matter carefully*; also remember a be-verb alone is not passive (5.122).
15. **Delete nominalizations and their prepositions** (5.193, 5.195): *efforts toward maximization of profits* → *efforts to maximize profits*; target one preposition per 10–15 words.
16. **Replace phrasal prepositions with one word** (5.180): *with regard to*, *in connection with*, *by means of* → *about*, *with*, *by*.
17. **Fix prepositional idioms** (5.199, 5.201): *based on* (not *off of*), *different from*, *comply with*, *independent of*, *center on* (not *around*), *enamored of*, *dissimilar to*, *acquit of*, *preferable to*.
18. **Keep *that* when it prevents a miscue** (5.212): after verbs other than *say/think*, unless the clause opens with a pronoun.
19. **Don't enforce the false rules** (5.112, 5.176, 5.186, 5.209, 5.210): split infinitives, terminal prepositions, and sentence-initial *And/But/So* are all fine; sentences opening with *However* are grammatical but ponderous — prefer *but* or move *however* inside.
20. **Keep *each other* for two, *one another* for more than two** (5.57) where it's easy; don't enforce it universally.
21. **Use the subjunctive where it's still live** (5.128, 5.130, 5.131): *if I were you*, *I recommend that she take*, *if I had gone* (not *if I would have gone*); but keep the indicative when the condition may be true.
22. **Historical present for plot summary** (5.133): characters *do*, not *did*; use the present perfect to mark genuine pastness within the work.
23. **No double comparatives/superlatives; no comparison of absolutes** (5.91, 5.93): *more greener*, *least greenest*, *very unique*, *more impossible* are errors (with the deliberate rhetorical exception of *more perfect*).
24. **Reach for `-er/-est` vs. `more/most` correctly** (5.89, 5.90): participles take *more/most* (never a suffix); one- and two-syllable adjectives take suffixes; three-plus take *more/most*.
25. **A/an by sound** (5.78): *a historic*, *an hour*, *an MBA*, *a PDF*; and don't repeat an article needlessly in a series (5.79).
26. **Don't "fix" participial prepositions and elliptical openers** (5.181, 5.188): *considering*, *regarding*, *barring*, *owing to*, and *in any event* need no subject and no rewrite — they are the standard exception to the dangling-modifier rule.
27. **Watch register markers** (5.106, 5.109, 5.166, 5.191): colloquial *like* for *as/as if*, exotic contractions (*I'd've*), compound adverbs (*heretofore*), and compressed phrasal verbs all read as informal or stuffy — decide deliberately.

### From ch05b

1. **Make every series parallel — element type *and* grammatical function.** (5.249) Then repeat the preposition when the series mixes prepositions (5.250), put correlatives immediately before the elements they join (5.251), and don't let one auxiliary try to govern verb phrases that need another (5.252).
2. **Never omit a relative pronoun or *that* where a reader could misparse.** Omit only when the pronoun is not the clause subject and not preceded by a preposition; keep *that* after verbs that would otherwise take a false object. (5.233, 5.212)
3. **Place *not* (and other scope words) immediately before the element being negated** — "He definitely did not accept" ≠ "He did not definitely accept." (5.238)
4. **After a negated verb, use *any*-words, not *some*-words.** (5.245) And prefer a negative pronoun/adverb to *not* + *any*: "We saw no one." (5.240)
5. **Avoid double negatives and accidental cancellation** — they read as a weak positive or as ambiguity. (5.243)
6. **Cut expletive scaffolding** ("It is important to note that…", "There are many…") when the real subject is short; and never let expletive *it* collide with pronoun *it* in one passage. (5.246–5.248)
7. **Delete empty filler nouns** — *area, basis, case, eventuality, parameters, space, individual* — and the turgid phrase if a preposition or adverb will do (*currently*, not "at the present time"; *because*, not "due to the fact that"; *before*, not "prior to"). (5.254)
8. **Prefer the plain verb**: *use* not *utilize*, *affect/influence* not *impact*, *finish* not *finalize*, *harm* not *prejudice*; avoid *impactful*, *enthused*, *orientate*, *precondition*, *cohabitate*, *preventative*. (5.254)
9. **Strip legalese**: *shall* → *must/will/may/is*; *such* → *the/this/that*; no *and/or*; no *as per*; no *in the affirmative*; *reason … is because* → *reason … is that*. (5.254)
10. **Prune *of*-phrases**: "try removing every of-phrase that you reasonably can" — *all the houses*, *half the furniture*, *June 2022*, *outside the shop*. (5.254)
11. **Get the high-frequency confusables right**: *affect/effect*, *imply/infer*, *comprise/compose* ("the whole comprises the parts"), *lay/lie*, *principal/principle*, *that/which*, *who/whom*, *less/fewer*, *amount/number*, *flout/flaunt*, *discreet/discrete*, *complement/compliment*, *adverse/averse*, *assure/ensure/insure*, *elicit/illicit*, *eminent/imminent*, *precede/proceed*, *prescribe/proscribe*. (5.254)
12. **Don't grade the ungradable and don't inflate**: *literally*, *unique*, *decimate*, *fulsome*, *plethora*, *nonplussed*, *peruse*, *noisome*, *begging the question*. (5.254)
13. **Attach every dependent clause; test appositives by removal.** Strip the introductory *that*: if what remains is a sentence, it is an appositive clause, not a relative clause. (5.232–5.234)
14. **Match conditional form to condition type** — open conditions in the indicative, hypothetical/unreal ones with past or past-perfect + modal. (5.235)
15. **Frame *neither … nor* around exactly two parallel elements**, or use *neither … nor … nor* for three. (5.241)
16. **Ellipsis only where recovery is certain** — fine in answers, commands, exclamations; not where the reader must guess the missing verb. (5.236)
17. **Keep register consistent**: formal prose may open sentences with *and*/*but* but not with *moreover*-scaffolding by reflex, and *hopefully* / *like* as conjunction / *get* / *quote* for *quotation* still read as casual. (5.254, 5.253)
18. **Treat a usage guide, not a dictionary, as the authority** for whether a word is fit for edited prose. (5.254)
19. **Apply inclusive-language reasoning case by case**: person over characteristic, the group's preferred label, no euphemism, no disability metaphors, no *-ess*/*-man* defaults, singular *they* where needed. (5.255–5.266)
20. **Use current references and read for judgment** — no rulebook substitutes for "wide knowledge, discerning judgment, and some degree of literary taste." (5.267)

## Overview and Process Outline (2.1–2.3)

- **[2.1] JUDGMENT** — Treat the chapter as three audiences: the *author* (2.4–2.52), the *manuscript editor/copyeditor* (2.53–2.104), the *proofreader* (2.105–2.144). "Author" is conceived broadly to include compilers, translators, volume editors, editors of journals, and contributors.
- **[2.2] JUDGMENT** — The canonical seven-step publication pipeline. Use it when advising an author where a document currently sits:
  > "1. Manuscript submission. In addition to the final manuscript, the author submits to the publisher all artwork and any necessary permissions to reproduce illustrations or previously published material or to cite unpublished data or personal communications."
  1. **Manuscript submission** (2.4–2.7).
  2. **Manuscript editing (copyediting)** — "The manuscript editor suggests changes to the manuscript (and, where necessary, queries the author) and demarcates or checks the order and structure of the elements (e.g., illustrations, headings, text extracts)." (2.63–2.73, 2.75)
  3. **Author review** — "The author reviews the edited manuscript and answers any queries. **All remaining changes and adjustments to the manuscript need to be indicated by the author at this stage.**" (2.77, 2.93)
  4. **Final manuscript** — editor incorporates the author's review and "double-check[s] each element in the manuscript against a design template for completeness, consistency, and proper markup." (2.79–2.82)
  5. **Proofreading and indexing** — after typesetting/conversion; check "for errors and inconsistencies" (2.105–2.144; index cf. ch. 15).
  6. **Final revisions** — "publishers usually set firm limits on changes beyond the first round of revisions" (2.110). "the occasional error in a published work is inevitable."
  7. **Prepress or final review** — check typesetter's final files, then F&Gs (folded-and-gathered signatures) (2.112).
- **[2.3] JUDGMENT** — Self-publishing authors must themselves do: final title/subtitle; author bio and book description; back-cover/jacket copy; cover design (front, back, spine, jacket flaps); interior text design; EPUB files. "All but the last of these should be attended to as soon as the manuscript is ready for copyediting."

## Manuscript Preparation Guidelines for Authors

### Basic Manuscript Submission Requirements (2.4–2.7)

- **[2.4] JUDGMENT** — Submit every element that belongs in the work, final and up to date: title page, dedication, epigraph, table of contents, list of illustrations, list of tables, preface, other front matter, all text matter (introduction, part titles), acknowledgments, appendixes, glossary, notes, bibliography/reference list, other back matter, **all illustrations and tables**, captions **and alt text**, a list of special characters, abstracts and keywords, and **all permissions in writing** (including interviewee and photo releases). "All elements should be final and up to date—including any URLs cited in the work."
- **[2.5] MECHANICAL** — Submit the latest electronic file(s); keep a secure backup; "avoid making any further changes to it." Any change made to the electronic file must also appear in the hard copy/PDF supplied. Cover letter must state: author name, title, **total word count (rather than a page count)**, file names, and software used. Non-electronic material (artwork) and unprintable material (video, animations, large data files) must be noted and described.
  - ex: "a total word count (rather than a page count)"
- **[2.6] JUDGMENT** — After submission the publisher holds the version of record; the author "must therefore alert the publisher immediately" to further changes. Minor changes go on the edited document at author review; major changes may require resubmitting a revised manuscript "before editing begins."
- **[2.7] MECHANICAL** — Paper-only manuscripts: two copies submitted, a third kept; all text double-spaced; everything legible; handwritten additions in upper- and lowercase letters above the line or in the margin; never write on the backs of pages; corrections longer than a short phrase go on a separate sheet keyed to the page; use 8½ × 11 in. or A4 paper.

### Manuscript Formatting (2.8–2.30)

- **[2.8] JUDGMENT** — Follow the publisher's requirements "to the letter. Consistency and simplicity in all matters is essential."
  > "the words themselves and the order in which they are presented are more important than the style of presentation. Provided the basic structure of chapters and sections and the like is clear, a simple presentation is always preferable to an elaborately formatted manuscript."
- **[2.9] MECHANICAL** — Use a widely supported Unicode font: Times New Roman, Calibri (or Aptos, Microsoft's newer default), or Arial. Body text ≥10-pt Arial or ≥11-pt Calibri/Aptos/Times New Roman; slightly larger for chapter titles and subheads; notes and table text same size or slightly smaller.
  - ex: "Many publishers prefer Times New Roman for its serifs, which can make capital letter *I* and lowercase *l* distinct."
- **[2.10] MECHANICAL** — Double-space everything in a printout, "including all extracts and lists, footnotes or endnotes, bibliographies or reference lists, and any other material." No extra blank lines between paragraphs; mark a deliberate break with three asterisks on a line by themselves (two levels: three asterisks = stronger, one = weaker, with a note to the editor).
- **[2.11] MECHANICAL** — **Use one space, not two, between sentences and after colons within a sentence.**
  > "a well-structured electronic document will never include more than one consecutive character space."
- **[2.12] MECHANICAL** — Standard page size (letter or A4); margins of at least one inch on all four sides "to leave enough room for handwritten queries"; page numbers may sit half an inch from the top right.
- **[2.13] MECHANICAL** — Set all text flush left (ragged right), not justified — "to avoid the appearance of inconsistent spacing between words and sentences." Justification requires end-of-line hyphenation and should not be applied at the manuscript stage.
- **[2.14] MECHANICAL** — Use indents, not tabs, except for a first-line tab at a new paragraph or after a number/symbol in a vertical list. "Avoid using two or more consecutive tabs." Never use tabs to achieve left indents or hanging indents; default value (usually half an inch) is fine. Three indent types: first-line, left/right, hanging (flush and hang).
- **[2.15] MECHANICAL** — Format each new paragraph with a first-line indent applied with the indent feature, "do not use the space bar." Never use Enter or Tab inside a paragraph. After an extract, list, or equation, the continuing text starts **flush left**; only a genuine new paragraph gets a first-line indent. (Published works often set the first paragraph after a subhead flush left; not required in the MS.)
- **[2.16] MECHANICAL** — Turn hyphenation off. "The only hyphens that should appear in the manuscript are hyphens that would appear regardless of where the text falls on the page." Do not manually break long words, "including URLs, whether long or short," with a hyphen. Do not fret over a very ragged right margin.
- **[2.17] MECHANICAL** — Insert em dashes as a true em dash character (or two consecutive unspaced hyphens). Leave the 3-em dash to the manuscript editor. **En dashes are the manuscript editor's responsibility; authors can generally use hyphens instead** (cf. 6.82–90, 6.91–98).
- **[2.18] MECHANICAL** — Use actual italics rather than underlining wherever italics are meant; use boldface "only for words that must appear thus in the published version."
- **[2.19] MECHANICAL** — "insert the character they intend rather than any keyboard substitute."
  - ex: wrong → right: type a prime symbol (′) not an apostrophe (’) where a prime is needed.
  - If a character is unavailable, insert a placeholder and add a detailed comment; always submit "a list of any special characters" showing how each should look.
- **[2.20] MECHANICAL** — Insert page numbers with the word processor's page-numbering feature, top of page flush right. A table of contents lists every section title (front matter, chapters, back matter); page numbers "may be omitted—and they should never be added manually."
- **[2.21] MECHANICAL** — Chapter/part titles begin on a new page, in upper- and lowercase (not full caps); Chicago prefers **title case** for chapter and other titles (8.160). Titles must match the table of contents. Put "Chapter 1," "Chapter 2," etc. above numbered chapter titles.
- **[2.22] MECHANICAL** — Each subhead begins on a new line, flush left; every level must be clearly distinguished (e.g., by size). Upper- and lowercase, not full caps; sentence case or title case, "provided they are consistent at each level." **No period follows a subhead** (except run-in heads, 1.62).
- **[2.23] MECHANICAL** — Prose extracts (block quotations): use indentation, never tabs; first line takes **no** additional paragraph indent, but each subsequent paragraph inside the extract does. Hard return only at the end of the extract and after internal paragraphs. Same line spacing and same font size as surrounding text; following text flush left unless it's a new paragraph.
- **[2.24] MECHANICAL** — Poetry extracts: indent (left indent plus hanging indent), never center; hard return at the end of each full line, never mid-line; runovers must be visually distinguishable from indented lines; deeper-indented lines get a larger left indent; stanza break = one extra hard return; source in parentheses on its own line indented like the poem's first line.
- **[2.25] MECHANICAL** — Lists: unnumbered items get left + hanging indent (no tabs for runovers). In numbered/lettered lists and outlines, "each number or letter should normally be followed by a period or other punctuation and a tab"; bullets likewise followed by a tab. Text after a list starts flush left unless it's a new paragraph.
- **[2.26] MECHANICAL** — Insert notes with the footnote/endnote function (for automatic renumbering); notes get the same line spacing; no extra hard return between notes. In the manuscript notes may be footnotes or chapter/book endnotes "regardless of how they are to appear in the published version," but **do not mix footnotes and endnotes** unless truly necessary. Never append note references to chapter titles. Table notes are numbered separately, without the note feature (2.36).
- **[2.27] MECHANICAL** — Glossary/list of abbreviations: one entry per line; capitalize a term only if capitalized in the text; separate term from definition with a period, colon, or em dash — "choose one and use it consistently." In a glossary, begin the definition with a capital letter "as if it were a new sentence"; in a list of abbreviations, case the expansion as it appears in the text. End each glossary entry with a period "unless all definitions consist of incomplete sentences." Italicize an entry if the term is consistently italicized in the text.
- **[2.28] MECHANICAL** — Bibliography/reference list: one entry per line with a hanging indent, never tabs for runovers, same line spacing as the rest.
- **[2.29] JUDGMENT** — Abstracts: usually a separate file; "A book abstract normally consists of a single paragraph of no more than 250 words," with no tables, lists, illustrations, or notes; chapter abstracts shorter. The abstract should "give a clear summary of the book's main arguments and conclusions."
  > "Authors should take care in writing abstracts; though they usually do not appear in the book itself (except in the sciences), they often form the basis of the promotional copy or descriptive metadata shared with libraries and booksellers."
- **[2.30] JUDGMENT** — Keywords: typically five to ten; each "a single word (e.g., *Olympics*) or an accepted compound of no more than a few words (e.g., *table tennis*)"; they should repeat significant terms from the abstract, may add an expected synonym, and should **not** be "terms that are unrelated to the subject of the book."

### Preparing Illustrations and Tables (2.31–2.36)

- **[2.31] MECHANICAL** — Submit illustrations as separate files, not embedded; call out their approximate placement in the text (exact placement is settled at typesetting). Captions in a separate file; list of illustrations follows the table of contents. Supply **alt text** for any image requiring it, "preferably in a separate file to facilitate copyediting."
- **[2.32] MECHANICAL** — Supply artwork to the publisher's specifications; label glossy prints on the back or on a self-sticking label "in a manner that does not damage them."
- **[2.33] MECHANICAL** — Number illustrations either consecutively or by **double numeration** (chapter number + period + figure number: fig. 1.1, 1.2 … 2.1, 2.2) in scientific/technical, heavily illustrated, or multi-author books — double numeration "will simplify the work needed to renumber" if a figure is dropped or added. Illustrations are numbered separately from tables; plates numbered separately from in-text figures. Assign working numbers even if numbers won't print.
- **[2.34] MECHANICAL** — Number tables either consecutively or by double numeration (table 1.1, 1.2 … 2.1, 2.2); separately from illustrations. "Very simple tabular material (e.g., a two-column list) may be presented unnumbered along with the text."
- **[2.35] JUDGMENT** — Distinguish a **text reference** (addressed to the reader, appears in print: "see table 5," "see fig. 3.2") from a **callout** (an instruction that never prints). Enclose callouts in angle brackets on a separate line after the paragraph of first mention — "`<table 5 about here>`"; "`<fig. 3.2 about here>`."
- **[2.36] MECHANICAL** — Table notes: **source notes first**, "preceded by the word *Source* followed by a colon"; then general notes, optionally preceded by "*Note*" and a colon; then specific notes; table notes numbered separately from text notes.

### Cross-Checking the Manuscript (2.37–2.40)

- **[2.37] JUDGMENT** — Before submission the author must cross-check: titles/subtitles against the TOC; subheads against the TOC; illustrations against captions, text references, and callouts; captions against the list of illustrations; tables against text references and callouts; table titles against the list of tables; internal cross-references/hyperlinks against their targets; URLs against their destinations; **quotations against their original sources**; notes against text references; notes against bibliography; author-date citations against reference list; abbreviations against the list; in multiauthor works, contributor names against chapter headings and the list of contributors.
- **[2.38] JUDGMENT** — "Authors should check all quoted matter against the original sources, for both content and source citations, before submitting" — "because manuscript editors will not have access to all the sources that the author has used."
- **[2.39] MECHANICAL** — Double-check every URL and external link "just before a manuscript is submitted"; update dead links; "in most cases, readers should be able to find the resource with or without the link."
- **[2.40] JUDGMENT** — Make cross-references to **chapter or section numbers**, not page numbers ("the pagination of a published work will not correspond to that of the manuscript," and ebooks lack fixed pages).
  > "references to whole chapters are often gratuitous and unhelpful; it's best to avoid peppering a manuscript with 'see chapter 2 above' and 'see chapter 4 below.'"
  - Where a page number truly is needed, use three bold zeros ("see p. **000**") as a flag.

### Preparing the Electronic Manuscript Files (2.41–2.45)

- **[2.41] MECHANICAL** — Follow publisher guidelines on one file vs. separate files (front matter, chapters, appendixes); illustrations "should always be in separate files"; tables made in a word processor may not need to be. Self-publishing-platform guidelines "may be more detailed and specific."
- **[2.42] MECHANICAL** — Name files to match the parts of the manuscript as listed in the TOC; include author name and a descriptive label, with two-digit numerals so files sort in book order:
  - ex: `01 Jones contents`, `02 Jones preface`, `03 Jones chap01` … `12 Jones biblio`; caption file starts with "00"; figures `Jones fig 1.1`. "whether or not these [extensions] are visible, they should never be changed or deleted."
- **[2.43] MECHANICAL** — Number every page. Multiple files need not run 1–end consecutively; instead add a descriptive header next to each number so no two pages share a number ("Introduction: 1," "chapter 1: 2"). Arabic numerals may be used for front matter even if it will print as roman.
- **[2.44] MECHANICAL** — Before submitting, delete all embedded comments and any text formatted as "hidden," and remove all revision marks/tracked changes; the file must be "the very latest version." Outstanding queries go in the cover letter. Editors "should always check for hidden text, comments, and revision marks."
- **[2.45] MECHANICAL** — Keep a backup of the version sent for editing, "in at least two separate locations."

### Preparing a Manuscript for a Multiauthor Book or Journal (2.46–2.47)

- **[2.46] JUDGMENT** — Fix responsibilities in writing before submission; the volume editor must "ensur[e] that the contributors furnish their chapters in a uniform style agreed to by all parties," secure permissions, edit each contribution "for sense," check citations for uniformity, get contributor approval, supply the contributor list, title page, TOC, and prefatory material, and send only the latest version of each chapter.
- **[2.47] JUDGMENT** — Optional volume-editor duties: distributing/executing publishing agreements, answering or distributing queries, proofreading the volume or delegating it and checking corrections, and preparing the index. "Most if not all of these responsibilities also apply to journal editors."

### Compiling a Manuscript from Previously Published Material (2.48–2.52)

- **[2.48] JUDGMENT** — Retyped or OCR'd text "must be proofread word for word against the original material before the final manuscript is submitted"; the material must be entirely legible.
- **[2.49] JUDGMENT** — **The compiler may make these three changes "without editorial comment":** notes may be renumbered; cross-references to parts of the original work "no longer relevant" may be deleted; "obvious typographical errors and minor grammatical slips that were clearly not intended may be silently corrected." Any wholesale change (spelling, capitalization, notes style) "should note such changes in a preface or elsewhere."
- **[2.50] JUDGMENT** — Original footnotes may become footnotes or endnotes; compiler notes added alongside originals "should be intermingled with but distinguished from the original notes."
- **[2.51] JUDGMENT** — Each reprinted selection gets a headnote or an unnumbered footnote on its first page giving the source, the copyright owner, and the original title if changed. If the piece appeared in several versions, "the source note need not give the entire publishing history but must state which version is being reprinted."
- **[2.52] MECHANICAL** — "Photocopies of illustrations are not acceptable for reproduction"; procure glossy prints or the original publisher's scans.

## Manuscript Editing

### Principles of Manuscript Editing (2.53–2.62)

- **[2.53] JUDGMENT** — Know the **four levels of edit**; do not confuse them:
  - *Developmental editing* — "directly shapes the content of a work, the way material should be presented, the need for more or less documentation of sources"; "does not focus specifically on details at the sentence or word level"; may involve "total rewriting or reorganization"; always done before copyediting.
  - *Line editing* — "focuses on word choice, phrasing, and other matters of writing style at the sentence level"; "a separate line edit is best undertaken (if needed) before copyediting begins."
  - *Copyediting* — "prepares an otherwise final manuscript for publication"; both mechanical (2.55) and, where needed, substantive (2.56) editing.
    > "As the final stage of editing before publication, copyediting requires attention to every word and mark of punctuation in a manuscript, a thorough knowledge of the style to be followed, and the ability to make quick, logical, and defensible decisions."
  - *Proofreading* — after typesetting/conversion, before publication (2.105–44).
  - "Each of these levels is subject to the approval of the author."
- **[2.54] JUDGMENT** — Sensitivity and authenticity readings: an expert flags "material that may be deemed inappropriate or harmful, particularly to underrepresented groups" or checks period detail and content outside the author's experience. Such readers "usually provide suggestions directly to authors in the form of queries rather than edits. It is then up to the author to implement them or not." Do them before copyediting.
- **[2.55] MECHANICAL** — Mechanical editing = "the consistent application of a particular style to a written work": capitalization, spelling, hyphenation, abbreviations; punctuation (ellipsis dots, parentheses, quotation marks); treatment of numbers; plus "attention to grammar, syntax, and usage." The style, "as well as occasional deviations from it, must be determined by the author, editor, and publisher before editing begins."
- **[2.56] JUDGMENT** — Substantive editing = "the organization and presentation of existing content":
  > "It involves rewriting to improve word choice or to eliminate ambiguity, reorganizing or tightening disorganized or loosely written sections, adjusting or recasting tables, and other remedial activities."
  - "no substantive editing should be undertaken without agreement between the publisher and editor, especially for book-length works"; for major substantive work "the author should be consulted and perhaps invited to approve a sample before the editing proceeds." Journal editors on rigid schedules may have to do it without prior consultation.
- **[2.57] JUDGMENT — the chapter's central principle; apply to every text:**
  > "A light editorial hand is nearly always more effective than a heavy one, and a flexible approach is usually better than a prescriptive one for anything beyond the correction of outright errors and inconsistencies."
  > "an editor should never mindlessly enforce 'the rules' without accounting for an author's style, the viewpoint of the narrator and any other voices in the manuscript (including in quoted passages and dialogue), and the conventions of the discipline or genre in which the author's work is situated."
  > "An experienced editor will recognize and not tamper with unusual figures of speech or idiomatic usage and will know when to make an editorial change and when simply to suggest it, whether to delete a repetition or an unnecessary recapitulation or simply point it out to the author, and how to suggest tactfully that an expression may be inappropriate or that an assertion might not be accurate."
  > "In general, an author's style should be respected, whether it is baroque or minimalist."
  - Counterweight: editors must still "be aware of any requirements of a publisher's house style and impose these within reason," including inclusive-language policies (5.255–67).
- **[2.58] JUDGMENT** — Estimate editing time from a **word count, not a page count**.
  - ex: "A 100,000-word academic book manuscript, edited by an experienced editor, might take seventy-five to one hundred hours of work before being sent to the author, plus ten to twenty additional hours after the author's review." Adjust for citations, tables, illustrations, and markup; when in doubt, edit a sample as the basis of the estimate.
- **[2.59] JUDGMENT** — **Three passes:** (1) initial editing, "easily the longest stage"; (2) review/refine/correct the editing; (3) after the author's review.
  - Begin the first pass "by looking through the entire document to assess the nature and scope of the work that will be required, to identify any matters that should be clarified with the author before editing begins, and to reduce the number of surprises."
  - "all elements must be compared to ensure that the notes match their text references, citations correspond to the entries in the bibliography or reference list, tables correspond to any discussion of them in the text."
- **[2.60] MECHANICAL** — For spelling, "Chicago prefers the dictionary entries at Merriam-Webster.com" (7.1); keep at hand reliable references for persons, places, events, technical terms, and the applicable style manual if citations are non-Chicago.
- **[2.61] JUDGMENT** — **Keep an alphabetical style sheet** for every manuscript listing "words or terms to be capitalized, italicized, hyphenated, spelled, or otherwise treated in any way unique to the manuscript." Changes made only for house style need not be logged — "It is enough to note, for example, 'In all other respects, Chicago style is followed.'" Add first-occurrence page numbers for paper manuscripts, plus "Special punctuation, unusual diacritics, and other items." For fiction, add characters (with pronouns), places, and a narrative timeline.
  - ex: fig. 2.3 includes entries such as `co-citizen`, `ghetto-like`, `Somali, Somalis`, `Third World`, `Western Europe`, `minister of child and family affairs`, plus "Mechanical matters" notes ("Ellipses: three-dot method, not three-or-four"; "Quoted newspaper headlines: sentence case"; "'emphasis mine'").
- **[2.62] JUDGMENT** — "In book publishing, the author is finally responsible for the accuracy of a work"; most publishers don't fact-check systematically. Still, "obvious errors, including errors in mathematical calculations, should always be pointed out to the author," and questionable names/citations should be checked and irregularities queried. Fact-checking "should be limited to what is needed to form an effective and judicious query to the author" (2.75). Fiction editors add continuity checking.

### Editing Specific Parts of a Manuscript (2.63–2.73)

- **[2.63] JUDGMENT** — Front matter: check half title, title page, TOC, and list of illustrations against text and captions; query discrepancies. Consult the author if subheads are to be dropped from the TOC. Label the manuscript for roman-numeral pagination (2.80).
- **[2.64] MECHANICAL** — Part/chapter titles and subtitles must be consistent with the text "in spelling, hyphenation, and italics"; "Chicago recommends that all titles be in title case" (8.160); every title must be checked against the TOC and any discrepancy queried.
- **[2.65] JUDGMENT** — Subheads: check "for consistency with the text in spelling, hyphenation, and italics, and for parallel structure and tone"; adjust the sentence that follows a subhead "as needed for proper wording relative to the subhead"; check the hierarchy, and "If there are more than three levels of subhead, determine whether the lowest level can be eliminated."
  > "If in doubt about which capitalization style to apply, or if the majority of subheads consist of full sentences, choose sentence case as the more readable alternative."
  - **No period at the end of a subhead, "even if the subhead is a complete sentence"** (except run-in heads).
- **[2.66] JUDGMENT** — Check every cross-reference to tables, figures, appendixes, bibliographies.
  - ex: if the text cites a statistic for 2024 and sends readers to table 4, "which gives statistics only through 2023, the editor must point out the discrepancy."
  - Place-names on a map "must be spelled as in the text"; minimize or eliminate cross-references to specific pages.
- **[2.67] JUDGMENT** — Quotations: apart from adjusting quotation marks and ellipsis dots to house style, "the editor must do nothing to quoted material" — except when the author is translating or modernizing it (11.18), or for transcribed interviews/field notes (12.56–58). "Misspelled words and apparent transcription errors should be queried"; a careless transcriber "should be asked to recheck all quotations for accuracy, including punctuation." Ensure every quotation has a source and that stylistic changes are noted in a preface (2.49).
- **[2.68] JUDGMENT** — Notes: check each against the text for correct reference and placement and for parallel treatment of terms. For footnote publication the author may be asked to shorten an overlong note or fold part of it into the text. "Lists, tables, and figures should be placed not in footnotes but in the text or in an appendix." Editors may combine or delete unneeded notes "in consultation with the author."
- **[2.69] JUDGMENT** — Citations: check documentation style; "every subsequent reference to a work previously cited … must be given in the same form as the first reference or in the same shortened form" (13.32–39). Check notes against the bibliography and query "in both contexts so that the author can easily compare them." "A bibliography need not include every work cited in the notes and may properly include some entries that are not cited." Check alphabetical (and where applicable chronological) order. "Many editors find it helpful to edit the bibliography or reference list before the text and notes."
- **[2.70] JUDGMENT** — Be flexible about citation style when the existing form is consistent and clear:
  > "Imposing house style on notes prepared in another style can be immensely time-consuming and, if the existing form is consistent and clear to the reader, is often unnecessary."
  - "Before making sweeping changes, the manuscript editor should consult with the author or the publisher or both." Journals: no such flexibility (linking requires predictable format).
- **[2.71] JUDGMENT** — Wording in diagrams, charts, and maps "should generally conform to the spelling and capitalization used in captions or the text"; "Captions in turn must conform to the style of the text." Source information follows the publisher and the letters of permission — "(If permissions are outstanding, the publisher, not the editor, should take up the matter with the author.)" Make a final check of illustrations against text references, callouts, captions, and the list of illustrations, "and that the illustrations show what they say they do."
- **[2.72] JUDGMENT** — "Tables are usually best edited together, as a group, to ensure consistent style and presentation"; check numbering and correspondence with text references and callouts (3.82–89).
- **[2.73] JUDGMENT** — Index editing must "correspond to the schedule for reviewing corrections to proofs" (index depends on final pagination) (15.131–33).

### Communicating with Authors (2.74–2.78)

- **[2.74] JUDGMENT** — Contact the author early, after an initial review — "especially important if an editor has questions or plans to make significant changes that, in the event the author proves not to be amenable, might take time and effort to undo."
  > "Most authors are content to submit to a house style; those who are not may be willing to compromise. Unless usage is determined by journal or series style, the author's wishes should generally be respected."
  - For a manuscript needing extensive changes, "send a sample of the editing for the author's approval before proceeding" (2.56).
- **[2.75] JUDGMENT — what an editor may change without asking:**
  > "Editors may generally impose a consistent style and correct errors without further comment—assuming these changes are apparent on the edited manuscript. Corrections to less obvious problems may warrant a comment."
  > "Comments should be concise, and they should avoid sounding casual, pedantic, condescending, or indignant; often, a simple 'OK?' is enough. Comments that are not answerable by a yes or a no may be more specific: 'Do you mean X or Y?'"
  - Comment/query when: noting that a global change was made silently after the first instance; pointing out a discrepancy (two spellings of a name; a source cited differently in notes vs. bibliography); an apparent omission (missing quotation mark or citation); a possible error in a quotation; repetition ("Repetition intentional?" / "Rephrased to avoid repetition; OK?"); needing verification of a name or term; ambiguity or garbled text; pointing to sources consulted for a factual correction.
- **[2.76] JUDGMENT** — The cover letter should explain: the nature and scope of the editing ("adjustment of spelling and punctuation to conform to house style … and occasional rephrasing for clarity or to eliminate inadvertent repetition"); how edits are shown (redlining or pencil) with instructions for reading the marks; how to respond to queries, veto unwanted editing, and make further adjustments; **a warning that the author's review "constitutes the last opportunity to make any substantive changes, additions, or deletions"** and that quotations and citations should be rechecked; a reminder to review carefully "since even editors are fallible" and errors caught only at proofs "may be deemed 'author's alterations' and charged to the author" (2.141); a reminder to retain a copy; the deadline; the index plan; and confirmation of contact details.
- **[2.77] JUDGMENT** — Deliver the edited manuscript as hard copy, a print-and-mark PDF, or a marked-up word-processing file. Because of the risk of undocumented changes, "most copyeditors do not allow authors to collaborate with them by making simultaneous edits in a shared document online (e.g., using a cloud-based platform like Google Docs), preferring instead to maintain control of their own version." Editors may "lock the document for editing such that any changes the author makes will be visibly tracked." Scan or photocopy a pencil-edited manuscript before sending it, and tell authors to photocopy theirs before returning it.
- **[2.78] JUDGMENT** — On return: verify every query has been answered, edit new material, and run another pass plus author review if the rewrite was extensive (inform the publisher).
  > "Except for style adjustments, the author's version should prevail; if that version is unacceptable for any reason, a compromise should be sought."

### Preparing a Final Manuscript for Production (2.79–2.82)

- **[2.79] MECHANICAL** — Verify markup for every component (title, TOC, chapters, sections, subheads, paragraphs, extracts, lists, notes, illustrations and captions, tables) and at the character level (words to be italicized, cross-references to be hyperlinked). "Manuscript editors are closest to the content and will be able to spot any missing or incorrectly identified elements."
- **[2.80] MECHANICAL** — Mark where roman numerals end and arabic begin (even if the number won't print); specify "Arabic p. 3" at a chapter opening that follows a part title; check that all elements are in correct order and that the order is reflected in the TOC.
- **[2.81] JUDGMENT** — Running heads: list suggested copy, clearly indicating verso (left) and recto (right). "To fit on a single line, usually containing the page number as well, a chapter or article title may have to be shortened for a running head but **must include the key terms in the title**" (often in the subtitle). Get the author's approval where possible.
- **[2.82] MECHANICAL** — A production checklist accompanies the final manuscript: author/title; component parts; software and file names; special characters/fonts; markup scheme and labels; material still to come; how notes are to be set (footnotes / chapter endnotes / book endnotes); elements of front/back matter and which must start recto; who receives proofs and in what format.

### The Mechanics of Electronic Editing — Preparation and Cleanup (2.83–2.85)

- **[2.83] MECHANICAL** — Save and back up in stages with descriptive names, e.g. "author's original," "clean unedited," "first edit," "to author"; use folders for complex projects; avoid saving over or working on an earlier version; "take care not to inadvertently edit with change tracking turned off"; save frequently; back up each significant version to a second location.
- **[2.84] MECHANICAL** — Learn "find-and-replace options, including the use of pattern matching (with wildcards or regular expressions), and … macros."
- **[2.85] MECHANICAL — the cleanup routine, in CMOS's own order.** Confirm first that the files are the latest version ("the presence of embedded revision marks may be a sign that this is not the case"). "Always review—and be prepared to undo—any global change before saving a permanent version."
  1. Convert files to the required software.
  2. Consider combining separate files into one (double-check the beginning/end of each component and its notes); or use macros across files.
  3. Change language setting (e.g., British → American English) so the spell/grammar checker behaves.
  4. Scroll through with nonprinting characters showing; fix formatting problems (hard returns mid-paragraph) and conversion errors (mangled special characters).
  5. Identify and handle graphic elements and tables (e.g., move to separate files).
  6. Apply markup to elements "whose visual cues may be lost as the formatting is cleaned up": chapter titles, subheads and levels, epigraphs, text and poetry extracts, extra line space (stanza breaks).
  7. Delete or fix extraneous spaces and tabs — "two or more consecutive spaces (between sentences or anywhere else) or spaces or tabs at the ends of paragraphs"; replace multi-space indents with tabs or, preferably, software indents.
  8. Change multiple hard returns to single hard returns.
  9. **Change underlining to italics** (keep true underscore, e.g. transcribed letters, with a note to the publisher).
  10. Fix quotation marks and apostrophes — including `‘em` for "*them*" — but first check whether left/right single quotation marks are standing in for breathing marks in transliterated languages (ch. 11).
  11. Fix commas and periods relative to quotation marks (table 6.1).
  12. Regularize em dashes and ellipses (6.91–98; 12.59–69).
  13. "Replace hyphens between numerals with en dashes as appropriate" (6.82–90).
  14. Convert footnotes to endnotes, or vice versa.
  15. Delete optional/conditional hyphens.
  16. "Find any lowercase els used as ones and any ohs (capital or lowercase) used as zeros—or vice versa—and fix" (pattern matching).
  17. Fix other global inconsistencies amenable to pattern matching — ex: closed-up initials → "changing `E.B. White` to `E. B. White`."
  18. Adjust line spacing, font, and margins.
  - Then "save a copy of the resulting clean, unedited manuscript."

### Electronic Markup (2.86–2.88)

- **[2.86] MECHANICAL** — Generic angle-bracket/bracket labels (`<A>`, `<B>`) are **no longer recommended** by this manual as a markup strategy: "because such identifiers must be replaced for publication and risk finding their way into the published work." Exception: placement callouts for figures may sit in angle brackets.
- **[2.87] MECHANICAL** — Prefer word-processing styles. "Paragraph-level style names should be descriptive, corresponding to the type of element (e.g., 'chapter number' or 'A-head') rather than to its format or appearance."
  > "accuracy and consistency in applying styles is more important than any formatting in the manuscript."
  - Authors reviewing the edited manuscript "should be advised not to modify styles." An annotated list of styles goes to production.
- **[2.88] MECHANICAL** — Formal markup (XML and the like): tags nest, each element delimited by opening/closing tags, "identified according to what it is rather than by how it will ultimately be presented."

### Tracking Changes and Inserting Queries (2.89–2.94)

- **[2.89] MECHANICAL** — Redlining: additions underlined, deletions struck through, queries in comments. "it is best to avoid making changes that might be missed; when in doubt, strike out the entire term and replace it with the corrected or preferred version."
  - ex: to change *Du Bois* to *DuBois*, strike out "the entire term and replace it with the corrected or preferred version" — ~~Du Bois~~ **DuBois** — rather than a partial strike that "may be mistaken for a hyphenated term."
- **[2.90] JUDGMENT** — "To avoid irritating or distracting the author, some editors will prefer to track only the first instance of a global change (such as capitalization of a certain term) and alert the author to the change in a comment." Some changes "should almost never be shown" (margins; a global application of smart quotes/apostrophes). "In general, most of the things listed in the cleanup checklist at 2.85 may be done silently." If changes were untracked, "the editor must delineate for the author the nature of the editing either in a comment or in the cover letter."
- **[2.91] JUDGMENT** — Use document-comparison software only for narrow purposes (e.g., confirming you have the latest version). "Comparing an edited document against the author's original manuscript may result in too many changes being reported, or worse, the results may be unintelligible."
- **[2.92] MECHANICAL** — Insert queries with the commenting feature; ask authors to reply in the same feature. "Avoid using footnotes or bracketed text to insert queries." And:
  > "As a matter of principle, it is best to avoid typing anything directly into the document that is not intended for publication."
  - Exceptions: image callouts, and embedded notes whose applications don't support comments.
- **[2.93] MECHANICAL** — Authors reviewing on-screen must use a compatible software version; the editor may password-protect the file "such that any changes the author makes will be visibly tracked"; include detailed instructions; authors should use comments rather than typing notes in the text, "lest any of their comments inadvertently make it into the published version."
- **[2.94] MECHANICAL** — On return: read each query first, then accept/reject each tracked change, edit new material, check continuity, delete all remaining comments, and "a spelling and grammar check should be run again as a final step." "The final manuscript should have no remaining tracked changes or comments" (publisher-facing notes belong in the cover letter).

### The Mechanics of Editing on Paper (2.95–2.104)

- **[2.95] MECHANICAL** — Keep a clean copy of the unedited manuscript (or archive the electronic original).
- **[2.96] MECHANICAL** — Paper editing uses proofreading-like marks but "paper manuscripts are usually double-spaced so that edits can appear above the word or words they pertain to, rather than in the margin." Use a color that reproduces clearly when photocopied or faxed; ask the author to respond in a different color.
- **[2.97] MECHANICAL** — Write queries in the margin so they can be crossed out when answered. "Chicago discourages the use of sticky notes for queries: They cannot be easily photocopied or scanned, and they may have to be detached." Long queries go on a separate sheet keyed by letter or symbol.
- **[2.98] MECHANICAL** — Circling has three meanings: (1) circle a number or abbreviation to be spelled out; (2) circle a comma or colon that is to become a period (and circle an inserted period so it isn't missed); (3) circle a marginal comment to show it is **not** to be set. "a query to the author or an instruction for typesetting … is always circled."
- **[2.99] MECHANICAL** — Distinguish caret (∧, insertion/subscript) from inverted caret (∨, superscript/apostrophe). "do not use a caret to indicate added text that is being substituted for deleted text; simply cross out the deleted text and write the text to be substituted above it."
- **[2.100] MECHANICAL** — "Special attention should be paid to punctuation when words are transposed or deleted"; mark added/changed punctuation explicitly (circle an added period; caret over an added comma; inverted caret under added quotation marks); if needed write and circle "colon," "exclamation point," etc.
- **[2.101] MECHANICAL** — Mark dashes: two unspaced hyphens are self-evident as an em dash; mark actual em dashes (M), 2- or 3-em dashes (2M/3M), and en dashes (N) so they aren't mistaken for hyphens. Mark end-of-line hyphens to distinguish **soft** (conditional) from **hard** (permanent, as in *cul-de-sac*).
- **[2.102] MECHANICAL** — Triple-underline a lowercase letter to capitalize; double-underline for small caps; slash through a capital to lowercase; straight underline = italics; wavy = boldface.
- **[2.103] MECHANICAL** — Bracket marks (⌐/¬) to move text left/right, ¶ for paragraph indents, up/down brackets for vertical space; write "one-line #" (and circle it) for a blank line; "In typographic usage the sign # means space, not number."
- **[2.104] MECHANICAL** — Identify components of a paper manuscript with circled labels at the start of the element or in the margin — e.g., circled "A" for a first-level subhead.

## Proofreading

### Introduction (2.105–2.107)

- **[2.105] JUDGMENT** — Define the job precisely:
  > "Proofreading is the process of reading a document and scrutinizing all its components to find errors and mark them for correction."
  - In this chapter it means review "after it has been converted to a format for publication but before it is published" — page proofs, proof, or the full text of an electronic edition; covers, jackets, abstracts, and other components are also proofread.
- **[2.106] JUDGMENT** — "For the majority of publications, authors are considered the primary proofreaders, and it is they who bear final responsibility for any errors in the published work." A professional proofreader may be hired by author or publisher; the manuscript editor, designer, and other personnel ensure corrections are actually incorporated.
- **[2.107] JUDGMENT** — "a few days' delay in returning proofs to the publisher or typesetter can cause a major delay in publication." If time and accuracy conflict, "the proofreader should immediately confer with the publisher." Journal deadlines "are generally nonnegotiable."

### Stages of Proof (2.108–2.114)

- **[2.108] JUDGMENT** — Keep a record of when each stage was corrected and by whom. Use a **primary (master) set**; for PDF, name iterations and "collate all corrections in the primary file" (e.g., "rev01," "rev02"). "At each stage, the author and other proofreaders should each be required to sign off."
- **[2.109] JUDGMENT** — First proofs (first pages) are read by the author or a designated proofreader, "preferably against the edited manuscript." "galley proofs" is "an anachronism"; galleys are loose-paginated and exist only to avoid expensive page re-corrections on complex books. The index is prepared from first proofs, not galleys.
- **[2.110] JUDGMENT** — Revised proofs (second pages): compare "for all pages against the first pages (now known as 'foul' proofs)," check for other differences and for "hyphenation errors or other page makeup problems." **"nothing must be marked on the pages or in the PDF file for the foul proofs at this stage; any further corrections must be marked only on the revised printout or revised PDF."** "Any additional rounds of revision should be kept to a minimum."
- **[2.111] JUDGMENT** — Index proofs must be read "quickly, in the same time that the revisions to the first proofs are being checked"; "editors rather than authors usually proofread indexes."
- **[2.112] JUDGMENT** — Prepress proofs are "a 'now or never' opportunity"; check "completeness of contents; page sequence; margins; and location, sizing, position, and cropping (if any) of illustrations." "publishers will generally allow only the correction of grave errors at this stage, such as an incorrect title or a misspelled author's name." F&Gs: since correction means reprinting a signature, "the publisher may decide to tolerate any remaining errors (while compiling a list of corrections for any future reprint)."
- **[2.113] JUDGMENT** — Cover and jacket proofs are read "word for word (if not letter by letter)": cover must match the interior (author name and title must match the title page everywhere — cover, spine, flaps; subtitle may be dropped); bio checked against interior bio; series title/volume number matched; price, ISBN, and credit lines verified.
- **[2.114] JUDGMENT** — Journal covers: check cover 1 for volume/issue/date accuracy, spine for inclusive page numbers, cover 2 for subscription and editorial-board information, covers 3 and 4 for ads, instructions to authors, and forthcoming-articles lists.

### How to Proofread and What to Look For (2.115–2.123)

- **[2.115] JUDGMENT** — **Read against copy.** "In proofreading parlance, *copy* refers to the edited manuscript." Check proofs against the manuscript containing the author's final changes and answers to queries, though "authors and dedicated proofreaders typically do a 'cold read,' checking against the manuscript only to resolve any questions."
  > "the proofreader must mark only the proofs, never the manuscript, which at this point in the process is known as 'dead' or 'foul' copy. To assign responsibility for errors correctly (see 2.140), the manuscript as earlier approved by the author must be kept intact."
  - When proofs were set from paper or from edited hard copy (math, tables), read word for word, "noting all punctuation, paragraphing, capitalization, italics, and so forth."
- **[2.116] JUDGMENT** — Hunt the errors spell-checkers miss: "it's where *its* is meant or *out* where *our* is meant, to more subtle errors like *lead* for *led* or *breath* for *breathe*." Consult the editor's style sheet. **"a change to the spelling of a particular term should never be indicated globally; instead, each change must be marked throughout the proofs."**
- **[2.117] JUDGMENT** — Check end-of-line hyphenation, "especially in proper names and terms in any language that may be outside the range of the dictionaries that automatically assign line breaks." Follow Merriam-Webster's syllabification dots (7.1); "a liberal approach is advisable" for nondictionary words; a nonstandard break may be preferred over a loose or tight line. **"No more than three succeeding lines should end in a hyphen"** (7.48).
- **[2.118] MECHANICAL** — Check every element for consistent typeface and style (heads per level, set-off material for font/size/indentation, and that italic/bold/small-cap spans "start and stop as intended"). Conversion "can result in unexpected errors, such as the dropping or transmutation of a special character throughout the work or the inadvertent incorporation of a comment or other 'invisible' electronic material." For a systemic error, "it may be preferable to indicate the change globally with a single instruction" — and alert the publisher immediately if it will repaginate.
- **[2.119] MECHANICAL** — Page numbers and running heads must be "present where they are supposed to be and absent where they are not," correct after a blank page, and correct as to recto/verso/spread. Running heads are "both proofread and checked for placement."
- **[2.120] MECHANICAL** — Illustrations: right location, right size, right side up, "not 'flopped' (turned over left to right, resulting in a mirror image) or distorted, and with their own captions." "Captions should be read as carefully as the text." Tables are proofread "both for content and for alignment." No running head or page number on a full-page illustration or table (unless a drop folio).
- **[2.121] JUDGMENT — typographic quality checks a writer/editor should know:**
  - No more than three consecutive lines that end in a hyphen, or that begin or end with the same word.
  - "A page should not end with a subhead."
  - "Nor should a page begin with the last line of a paragraph unless it is full measure; a short line in this position is sometimes called a **widow**." A page *can* end with the first line of a new paragraph — "an **orphan**."
  - "The last word in any paragraph must not be hyphenated unless at least four letters (in addition to any punctuation) are carried over to the final line."
  - A word may break across a spread (verso→recto) "but usually should not break at the end of a spread (recto to verso)."
  - Query unclear type: "Type OK?" or "Too dark?"
- **[2.122] JUDGMENT** — Facing pages must align, but "it is usually acceptable for both pages to run a line long or short to avoid widows … or to accommodate corrections." Type may be rerun "more loosely or more tightly to add (gain) or eliminate (lose) a line."
- **[2.123] JUDGMENT — the proof-stage limit on rewriting:**
  > "The proofreader must query—or correct, if possible—illogical, garbled, repeated, or missing text. Any rewriting, however, must be limited to the correction of fact or of gross syntactical error, since all source checking and substantive and stylistic changes should have been done at the editing stage."
  - "Changes that would alter page makeup across more than a couple of pages in printed works should be avoided, since repagination not only is expensive but, for books, can affect the index."

### How to Mark Proofs (2.124–2.138)

- **[2.124] MECHANICAL** — Use standard English proofreaders' marks (figs. 2.6, 2.7); they "form the basis of the tools for annotating PDF files in Adobe Reader."
- **[2.125] MECHANICAL** — "Corrections to paper proofs must always be written in the margin, left or right, next to the line concerned," with a mark in the text (caret for an addition; strike-through for a deletion). **"Never should a correction be written or marked only between the lines, where it could be missed."** Multiple corrections to one line are marked in order, separated by vertical lines.
- **[2.126] MECHANICAL** — Circle every verbal instruction on a proof ("see attached typescript," "ital," "rom") so the words aren't set as text.
- **[2.127] JUDGMENT** — If a line needs more marks than are legible, "cross out the whole passage and rewrite it correctly in the margin"; if the margin is insufficient, use a separate sheet and mark the insertion point in both places.
  > "To avoid repagination of print works, every effort must be made to match the word count (or, for tight fits, the character count) of new material to that of the old."
  - "Most types of global changes should be marked individually to ensure that each change is made correctly."
- **[2.128] MECHANICAL** — Write corrections "clearly (such that they can be spotted at a glance) in upper- and lowercase letters." Red is often preferred, but any color distinct from the publisher's/typesetter's will do. "Messy corrections may lead to further errors; indistinct corrections may be overlooked."
- **[2.129] MECHANICAL** — Deletion: diagonal line through a letter, straight line through a word or phrase, plus the delete mark in the margin. **"No part of the text should be obliterated, and a punctuation mark that is to be deleted should be circled rather than crossed through, so that it is still visible."** When substituting, "only the substitution is written in the margin."
- **[2.130] MECHANICAL** — Unequal word spaces: carets plus the "eq #" mark in the margin; close-up mark to delete space; space mark (#) to add space or leading.
- **[2.131] MECHANICAL** — New paragraph: L-shaped mark plus ¶ in the margin. Run paragraphs together: line from one to the next plus "run in." Indent one em: small square in text and margin (a divided rectangle for multiple ems).
- **[2.132] MECHANICAL** — Move right (→) / left (←); both for centering; up/down marks for vertical placement; "fl" and "fr"/"justify" (circled) for flush left/right; ‖ or ≡ for alignment in tables.
- **[2.133] MECHANICAL** — Transposition: circumscribe exactly the items including punctuation and write circled "tr" in the margin.
- **[2.134] MECHANICAL** — Spell-out: circle the abbreviation/numeral and write circled "sp"; write the full word if ambiguous.
- **[2.135] MECHANICAL** — To restore something: dots under the material, cross out the marginal mark, and write circled "stet" — "or to avoid any ambiguity, 'stet as set.'"
- **[2.136] MECHANICAL** — Case/font in the margin: "lc," "cap," "sc," "ital," "rom," "bf," "not bf"; underline in the text accordingly (3 lines = caps, 2 = small caps, 1 = italics, wavy = bold). "Remember to circle all marginal instructions."
- **[2.137] MECHANICAL** — Punctuation in the margin must be disambiguated: comma with a caret over it; apostrophe/quotation mark with an inverted caret under it; parenthesis with two short horizontal lines through it; period circled; semicolon and colon followed by a short vertical line; ? and ! accompanied by the circled word "set." **"If an accent or a diacritical mark is missing or incorrect, the entire letter should be crossed out in the text and written in the margin with its correct accent; never must the accent alone appear in the margin."**
- **[2.138] MECHANICAL** — PDF proofreading tools generate a sortable annotation list (type, reviser, date/time, page, text). "It is important to avoid redundant markup (e.g., the use of one tool to draw a line through a word and a different tool to insert a correction in the same place); choose a single tool wherever possible."

### Double-Checking Proofs and Assigning Responsibility (2.139–2.141)

- **[2.139] JUDGMENT** — Additional checks: titles/subheads against the TOC (and query or delete any TOC item missing from the work); each footnote appears on the page carrying its reference; missing cross-references filled in; half title, title page, and copyright page verified; for journals, covers, spine, issue data, and front/back matter checked against the previous volume.
- **[2.140] MECHANICAL** — Where required, tag corrections: **PE** (printer's/typesetter's error), **AA** (author's alteration), **EA** (editor's alteration), **DA** (designer's alteration) — "All such indications should be circled to prevent their being incorporated into the corrected proofs."
- **[2.141] JUDGMENT** — "Any rewriting or adding of new material by the author is considered an AA"; page numbers added to cross-references are usually AAs; "Corrections of errors uncaught or even introduced in editing are considered AAs if the author reviewed and approved the edited manuscript"; an error introduced by the publisher *after* the author's review "is an EA and not chargeable to the author."

### Proofing and Testing Electronic Formats (2.142–2.144)

- **[2.142] MECHANICAL** — Every component must be checked in its final format (PDF, EPUB, online); the full text "must be proofread word for word … in at least one format." Checklist: layout and missing elements; special-character conversion; links and interactive features; illustrations/tables outside the text run; whether each format's content matches (or varies as intended, and says so); alt text completeness; valid EPUB/HTML markup; proofread recent changes for "spelling, sense, and proper integration into each new context."
  - EPUB QA list (fig. 2.8) items a writer/editor controls: metadata title must match the title page and cover; notes linked both ways and, in reflowable formats, "footnotes are usually converted to endnotes"; "Do notes with 'ibid.' appear out of context (e.g., in a pop-up)? Consider replacing with a short form of the citation"; bibliography "left-justify the text of the whole bibliography to avoid large gaps between words" if long URLs appear; retain the index with working links.
- **[2.143] JUDGMENT** — Check accessibility against WCAG; human reviewers, including people with print disabilities, should verify: "clear and logical page titles, appropriate alternative text for images and captions or transcripts for audio or video, logical headings and page structure, text that resizes as needed and that has sufficient contrast and appropriate colors, and accessible keyboard navigation."
- **[2.144] JUDGMENT** — Audiobook notes: mark pronunciation ("for 'Wilkes-Barre' say 'WILKS bare-uh'"), special emphasis, shifts in tone, and pacing. "though most audiobooks follow the text of the book word for word, the occasional adjustment might be needed for something that works on the page but not out loud."
  - ex: "the 'wrong' answer" → glossed for audio as "the quote-unquote wrong answer."

## Grammar (5.1–5.5)

- **[5.1] Grammar is the rules for putting words together** — treat grammar as the set of rules governing how words combine to communicate ideas; native speakers learn most of it unconsciously, with the residue falling under "idiom and customary usage."
- **[5.2] Prescriptive vs. descriptive** — prescriptivism is about how people *should* write; description is about how they *do*. The chapter's stance, worth adopting in edits: recommendations are "merely descriptions of Standard English as evidenced in published works," so justify a correction with evidence of settled usage, not with a rule's existence.
  > "the better prescriptivists have acknowledged that their recommendations are merely descriptions of Standard English as evidenced in published works"
- **[5.3] Standard English vs. Standard Written English** — *Standard English* includes speech; *Standard Written English* does not. Both mean the form "traditionally taught in schools and most widely used for communication among English users outside their dialectal communities."
  > "Learning and using Standard English does not mean rejecting one's origins or one's native dialect; most people shift between differing varieties of language according to social context. But it does expand one's ability to communicate clearly across communities."
- **[5.4] Label usage honestly** — when CMOS says a usage is "not preferred," "nonstandard," or the like, it means only that the usage does not comport with Standard English — not that the speaker is deficient. Use the same restraint in editorial comments.
- **[5.5] Eight parts of speech** — work with the traditional eight: noun, pronoun, adjective, verb, adverb, preposition, conjunction, interjection. (Modern grammarians disagree on the count — three, nine, "about fifteen" have all been claimed — but the traditional eight is the shared vocabulary, and this chapter uses it deliberately to stay accessible.)

## Nouns (5.6–5.29)

### Traditional classifications (5.6–5.9)

- **[5.6] Nouns generally** — a noun names something abstract or concrete; common vs. proper; count vs. mass. Concrete count nouns name countable things; mass nouns name the uncountable or collectively taken.
- **[5.7] Common nouns** — generic names {a chemical} {a river} {a pineapple}; not capitalized unless sentence-initial or in a title; usually take a determiner. Subdivide into concrete {a building} {the wind} {honey}, abstract {joy} {expectation} {neurosis}, and collective {a crowd of people} {a flock of birds} {a herd of rhinos}.
- **[5.8] Proper nouns** — names of specific persons, places, things, or works {Michelle Obama} {Moscow} {the Hope Diamond} {Citizen Kane}; always capitalized unless the bearer deliberately flouts the rule {k.d. lang}. A proper name used attributively does not capitalize the noun it modifies. Determiners attach only when part of the name contains a common noun or for emphasis {the Savoy Hotel} {Sam? I knew a Sam Hill once}. Eponyms (sandwich, china) have developed common-noun senses.
- **[5.9] Mass nouns** — uncountable nouns (abstract or aggregate) that take no indefinite article and normally no plural: *a team*, but not *an evidence*; *two groups*, but not *two luggages*. Enumerate with *pair of* {a pair of scissors} or a unit noun {seven pieces of stationery}; quantify with *any, less, much, some*.
  - MECHANICAL: mass nouns are the common source of "a information," "two advices," "many evidences."

### Properties of nouns (5.10–5.14)

- **[5.10] Properties** — nouns have case and number (some grammarians also gender and person).
- **[5.11] Case** — English nouns have common case (uninflected) and genitive/possessive (-'s or bare apostrophe); grammarians also distinguish the common-case noun in nominative vs. objective role. Keep noun labels parallel to pronoun labels (who/whom, she/her) to avoid confusion.
- **[5.12] Number** — singular *clock* vs. plural *clocks*.
- **[5.13] Gender** — English nouns have no true grammatical gender; a few human/animal nouns are inherently masculine {uncle} {rooster} {lad} or feminine {aunt} {hen} {lass}. Most are common in gender {relative} {chicken} {child}, and many formerly masculine job words are now common {author} {executor} {proprietor}. Feminine suffixes have been abandoned {aviatrix} {poetess}. Cf. 5.255–67 (inclusive language; out of range).
- **[5.14] Person** — attributing person to nouns is a stretch of logic (nouns don't change form for it), but person in the subject does govern the verb's form.

### Plurals (5.15–5.18)

- **[5.15] Plurals generally** — check a dictionary or usage guide for the standard plural; exceptions abound. (Formation is ch. 7, 7.5–15.)
- **[5.16] Plural form, singular sense** — plural-looking nouns take singular verbs {good news is always welcome} {economics is a challenging subject} {measles is potentially deadly}; a plural word *as a word* is singular {"mice" is the plural of "mouse"}. *Data* and *media* have acquired mass-noun singular uses *{the data shows the hypothesis to be correct}* alongside the traditional plurals *{the data are inconclusive}*; in the sciences *data* is always plural.
  > "In formal contexts, the most reliable approach is to retain the plural uses unless doing so makes you feel as if you're being artificial, stuffy, and pedantic. ... But make your play and be consistent."
- **[5.17] Plural-form proper nouns** — plural geographic names for a single entity are singular {the United States is a relatively young nation} {the United Arab Emirates favors the measures}, with exceptions {the Alps have never been totally impassable}. Company/institution names are collective and take singular verbs in American English {General Motors reports that it will earn a profit} {American Airlines has moved its headquarters}; British English prefers plural for teams and groups acting in concert {Manchester United have won the FA Cup} {England are now leading}.
- **[5.18] Anomalies** — mass nouns with only a singular form (furniture, spaghetti, wheat); plural-only nouns (oats, scissors, slacks); singular-looking but invariably plural {the police were just around the corner} {the vermin seem impossible to eradicate}; American-singular collectives that are British-plural {the committee are discussing the report they received}; plural-looking but singular {the news is good} {linguistics is my major}. *Person* has two plurals (*persons*, *people*), and *people* has *peoples*.

### Case (5.19–5.25)

- **[5.19] Function of case** — case marks the relation between a noun/pronoun and other words in the sentence.
- **[5.20] Nominative (subjective) function** — the doer about which an assertion is made; controls the verb, usually precedes it {the governor delivered a speech} {the shops are crowded because the holiday season has begun}, though inversion can move it {high up in the tree sat a leopard}. A noun/pronoun after a be-verb referring to the same thing is a predicate nominative {my show dogs are Australian shepherds}. MECHANICAL: the commonest agreement error is mistaking an object of *of* for the subject {an assembly of strangers was (*not were*) outside}.
- **[5.21] Objective (accusative) function** — the thing acted on by a transitive verb in the active voice {the balloon carried a pilot and a passenger}, or the thing related by a preposition {place the slide under the microscope}; it is never the subject of a following verb and usually doesn't control the verb's number.
- **[5.22] Genitive case** — covers (1) ownership {the architect's drawing board}, (2) relationship {the philanthropist's secretary}, (3) agency {the company's representative}, (4) description {a summer's day}, (5) subjective role {the boy's application [the boy applied]}, (6) objective role {the prisoner's release [someone released the prisoner]}, (7) idiomatic shorthand for an of-phrase {one hour's delay}. "Possessive" is a misleadingly narrow label. Form: singular + -'s {driver's seat}; plural ending in -s/-es + apostrophe {parents' house} {foxes' den}; irregular plural + -'s {women's rights} {mice's cage}; compound takes the ending on the last word {parents-in-law's message}. (See 7.16–29.)
  - MECHANICAL: bare apostrophe for -s plurals; -'s for irregular plurals; -'s on the last element of a compound.
- **[5.23] The of-genitive** — *of* before a noun expresses relationship, agency, or possession. Choose by style: proper nouns and nouns of higher status prefer the inflected genitive {Hilda's adventures} {the lion's paw}; inanimates often allow either {the theater's name} / {the name of the theater}, but some take only *of* {the end of everything}. Use the double genitive for partitive/indefinite possession {an idea of Hill's} {a friend of my grandfather's} (see 7.26).
- **[5.24] Joint vs. separate genitives** — shared possession: inflect only the last noun {Peter and Harriet's correspondence [between the two]}; separate possession: inflect each {Peter's and Harriet's correspondence}. With a noun plus a pronoun, both must show possession {Hilda and Eddie's vacation} → *Hilda's and his vacation* (or *Hilda's and my vacation* if Eddie is speaking).
- **[5.25] Distributive possessives** — plural subject with individual possession normally takes a plural object {my parents bobbed their heads in time with the music}; joint possession takes a singular object {the coauthors discussed their plan for a second book}. If a plural object is ambiguous, use the singular.
  - ex: *participants had to wear their watches* (did each need more than one?) → *participants had to wear a watch*
  - ex: collective ownership → singular: *the students expressed their relief after the test*

### Appositives (5.26)

- **[5.26] Appositives** — a noun element immediately following another to define or identify it {George Washington, the first US president, was born in Virginia}. Nonrestrictive appositives are framed by commas; restrictive ones are not.
  - ex: *Robert Burns, the poet, wrote many songs about women named Mary* (nonrestrictive) vs. *the poet Robert Burns wrote many songs about women named Mary* (restrictive — it fixes which poet)
  - Rule of thumb: if the appositive can be removed without obscuring identity, it's nonrestrictive → commas. (See also 6.30.) MECHANICAL for the comma test, JUDGMENT for restrictiveness.

### Functional variations (5.27–5.29)

- **[5.27] Nouns as adjectives (attributive nouns)** — a noun modifying a noun {the morning newspaper} {a state legislature} {a varsity sport}. Attributional nouns stay singular: *car dealership*, not *cars dealership*. Watch ambiguity (*fast results* = "rapid results" or "outcome of a fast"; *a study group* needn't be studious; *voter awareness* — by voters or of voters?). Add a preposition if the relation is unclear.
- **[5.28] Nouns as verbs** — denominal verbs are ancient (husband, 1420) but recent jargon conversions should be used cautiously in formal prose (e.g., *mainstreaming pupils*).
- **[5.29] Nouns as adverbs (adverbial objectives)** — omitted prepositions produce adverbs from nouns {we rode single file} {Sam walked home}; they often modify adjectives {the team is four members strong}. Keep them only where idiomatic; otherwise restore the preposition.

## Pronouns (5.30–5.71)

### Definition and uses (5.30–5.34)

- **[5.30] Pronouns defined** — a substitute for a noun (or another pronoun), used either for an expressed antecedent (to avoid repetition: *The father told his daughter that he wanted her to walk the dog* replaces *The father told the father's daughter that the father wanted …*) or for an understood one (*Are you bringing your boots?*; in *It is too hot*, *it* is understood to mean the temperature). Phrasal pronouns: *each other, one another, no one*.
- **[5.31] Antecedents** — an antecedent is an earlier noun, pronoun, phrase, or clause in the same or a previous sentence; pronouns with antecedents are "anaphoric."
  > "An antecedent may be explicit or implicit, but it should be clear."
  - Three classic miscues to hunt: (1) missing antecedent — *The clown's act with his dog made it a pleasure to watch* (*it* = the circus, never mentioned); (2) multiple candidates — *Scott visited Eric after his discharge from the army* (who was discharged?); (3) too many pronouns for the antecedents — *When the bottle is empty or the baby stops drinking, it must be sterilized with hot water or it could become ill.*; JUDGMENT.
- **[5.32] Adjective as antecedent** — a possessive can antecede a nominative/objective pronoun; don't "fix" it.
  - ex: *Mr. Blain's background qualified him for the job* is fine (and more economical than *Mr. Blain had a background that qualified him*).
- **[5.33] Pronouns without antecedents** — *I/we/me/us* (speaker), *you* (addressee), expletive *there/it*, relative *what*, and interrogatives never need antecedents. Colloquial *they* without antecedent {they say she's a good golfer} is idiomatic but invites the question "who?"
- **[5.34] Four properties** — number, person, gender, case. A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number, person, and gender; only third-person singular *he/she/it* marks all three. *You* marks person only (no longer number); *they/them* likewise — singular when it denotes one person, plural when more than one (5.51, 5.52).

### Agreement and case of pronouns (5.35–5.41)

- **[5.35] Pronoun number and antecedent** — singular antecedent → singular pronoun of the same person; plural → plural {a book and its cover} {the dogs and their owner}. A collective noun takes a singular pronoun as a unit {the audience showed its appreciation} but plural when members act individually {the audience rushed back to their seats}. A singular noun modified by two adjectives denoting different varieties may take a plural pronoun {British and American writing differ in more ways than just their spelling}. Two singulars joined by *and* are joint → plural {the boy and girl left their bicycles outside}.
- **[5.36] Five refinements on pronoun number** — (1) two singulars for the *same* thing joined by *and* take a singular {a lawyer and role model received her richly deserved recognition today}; (2) singulars modified by *each, every, no* take a singular {every college and university encourages its students to succeed}; (3) *or, nor, either–or, neither–nor* treat antecedents separately → singular {neither the orange nor the peach smells as sweet as it should}; (4) antecedents of different numbers joined by *or/nor* → agree with the nearest (put the plural last) {neither the singer nor the dancers have asked for their paychecks}; (5) different numbers joined by *and* → plural regardless of order {the horses and the mule kicked over their water trough}. MECHANICAL in most instances.
- **[5.37] Multiple antecedents of different persons** — the pronoun takes one person only: first beats second, second beats third.
  - ex: *you or I should get to work on our experiment* (first person); *you and she can settle your dispute* (second); *you and Marian have discussed her trip report* (the pronoun referring to one antecedent takes that one's person).
  - ex: *neither they nor I could do his work* (*his* refers to someone in a previous sentence — number follows the real antecedent).
- **[5.38] Pronoun case** — subject of a finite verb → nominative {they went to town}; object of a verb or preposition → objective {they gave her a farewell party} {they gave it to him}; possessive pronouns agree with the *possessor's* gender {President Barack Obama took his advisers with him to Hawaii}; after an intransitive verb or participle, a pronoun agrees with the preceding co-referential noun {it is I} (see 5.48); in an absolute construction → nominative {she being disqualified, our best hope is gone}.
- **[5.39] Pronouns in apposition** — case is set by the function of the word the pronoun is in apposition with: {we three—Bruce, Felipe, and I—traveled to Augusta} vs. {she asked us—Barbara, Sarah, and me—to move our cars}.
- **[5.40] Nominative misused for objective (the #1 pronoun error)** — verbs and prepositions take the objective case; the compound is where it breaks.
  - ex: *The test would be simple for you or I.* → *The test would be simple for you or me.*
  - ex: *Read this and tell Laura and I what you think.* → *Read this and tell Laura and me what you think.*
  - The reciprocal error: objective in a compound subject — *Jim and me want to go swimming.*
  - **Foolproof check (teachable to an agent):** delete the compound and read the pronoun alone — *tell I what you think*, *Me want to go swimming*. MECHANICAL (detectable by pattern: preposition/*and* + *I/we/he/she/they* in object slot).
- **[5.41] Seven classes of pronouns** — personal, demonstrative, reciprocal, interrogative, relative, indefinite, adjective. Non-personal pronouns often do double duty (a given *that* may be demonstrative, relative, or adjective) depending on use.

### Personal pronouns (5.42–5.50)

- **[5.42] Form** — a personal pronoun's form marks person (source, goal, topic of the utterance), number, gender, and case.
- **[5.43] Identification** — first person = speaker {I need some tea} {we heard the news}; second = addressee {you should write that essay tonight}; third = spoken of {she is at work} {it is in the glove compartment} {they are the teacher of my first-year class} (singular *they*; 5.51, 5.52). *I* is always capitalized; other pronouns only sentence-initially or in an honorific {His Majesty, the King of England}.
- **[5.44] Changes in form of personal pronouns (incl. the four essential rules of case)** — (1) subject of a clause → nominative {he is vice president}; (2) object of a verb → objective {she thanked him}; (3) object of a preposition → objective {please keep this between you and me}; (4) subject of an infinitive → objective {Jim wanted them to sing}. **From the tables (case × person × number, incl. reflexives):** *you* and *they* can be singular in sense but **always take plural verbs**, even for one person; the third-person singular genitive of *they* is *their/theirs*; the reflexive is *themself* or *themselves* (fn.: *themself* now serves a nonbinary person or, in some circumstances, someone whose gender is unknown or unimportant — see 5.51, 5.52). MECHANICAL.
- **[5.45] Agreement with the noun** — the pronoun matches the gender and number of the noun it stands for {John writes, and he will soon write well} {the pilot was highly skilled; they flew the jet safely despite a storm} {the dog growled; it wouldn't come when called}.
- **[5.46] Gender** — only *he/she/it* express gender directly; in nominative/objective the pronoun takes the antecedent's gender {the president is not in her office today}; in the genitive it takes the **possessor's** gender, not the possessed {the woman loves her spouse} {Thomas is visiting his sister} {the kitten pounced on its mother}. Personification licenses gendered pronouns (ships as *she*) but is a deliberate figure.
- **[5.47] Case rules for personal pronouns** — (1) subject of a clause, or after a conjunction but before the verb → nominative {she owns a tan briefcase} {although Delia would like to travel, she can't afford to}; (2) object of a verb or preposition → objective {the rustic setting helped him relax} {that's a matter between him and her}; (3) every object in a multi-object prepositional phrase → objective {will you send an invitation to him and me?}; (4) subject of an infinitive → objective {does Tina want me to leave?}.
- **[5.48] After a linking verb** — strictly, the complement of a be-verb is nominative {it was she who asked for a meeting} (an "attribute pronoun"); on the telephone, *This is he*, not *This is him*. JUDGMENT: formal prose keeps it, most dialogue doesn't.
- **[5.49] After *than* or *as … as*** — case encodes the comparison: *my sister looks more like our father than I [do]* vs. *… than [she looks like] me*. Because readers can't recover the intent either way, **reword and avoid the elliptical comparison**; with *as … as* it's only register {you're just as nervous as I [am]} (formal) / *… as me* (informal).
  - This is a directly detectable "incomplete comparison" defect. JUDGMENT in diagnosis, mechanical in the fix (add the verb or the object).
- **[5.50] Special uses** — (1) generic *he/him/his* for a person of unspecified gender {if the finder returns my watch, he will receive a reward} — "criticized as sexist when used generically, and such uses are declining (see 5.255–67)"; (2) neuter *it* removes gender consideration even when sex is identifiable {the baby is smiling at its mother} {the mockingbird is building its nest}; (3) *we/you/they* used indefinitely ("the editorial *we*"; *they say that Stonehenge may have been a primitive calendar*); *we/you* before nouns as limiting adjectives {we socialists reject that} {us students}; (4) *it* for a phrase/clause/implied thought, as a subject without antecedent, for time/weather/distance {it is almost midnight} {it is beginning to snow}, and as an anticipatory expletive {I find it hard to accept this situation}.

### Singular *they* (5.51–5.52) — the 18th-edition position, in full

- **[5.51] Generic singular *they*** — a singular antecedent may take *they/them/their/themselves* (*themself*); long common in speech and informal prose {somebody forgot their coat}.
  > "In recent years this usage has become accepted in more formal contexts (see also 5.266), and Chicago now endorses it."
  - **18th-edition change:** Chicago now *endorses* generic singular *they*; do not edit it out, and do not "correct" it to *he* or *he or she*. (The 17th edition tolerated it; earlier guidance preferred *he*.)
  - Agreement consequence: singular *they* takes a plural verb even for one person.
- **[5.52] Referential singular *they*** — *they* and its forms are the standard alternative to *he/she* for people who don't identify with gender-specific pronouns; it follows the pattern of *you* (one form, singular and plural).
  - ex: *they have a certificate in culinary arts* (one person, plural verb)
  - *themself* (like *yourself*) may signal the singular when it's the logical choice, though some prefer *themselves*: *they blamed themself [or themselves]*.
  > "If an individual is known to use they and its forms as their personal pronouns rather than the gendered he or she, this usage should be respected."
  - Neopronouns (*ze/zir/zirs/zirself*) exist; unlike singular *they*, they generally take **singular** verbs.
  - Editorial rule for an agent: match a named person's stated pronouns; use singular *they* when gender is unknown, irrelevant, or the person is nonbinary; never rewrite to *he or she*; never make the verb agree with the notional number.
- **(Pointer, out of range)** Person-first vs. identity-first, disability, race/ethnicity, age, and the *he or she* problem are rules **5.255–5.267** (pp. ~393–400), cited from 5.13, 5.50, 5.52, and 5.82; not in this chunk.

### Possessive, reflexive, demonstrative, reciprocal (5.53–5.57)

- **[5.53] Possessive pronouns** — *my, our, your, his, her, its, their* are limiting adjectives, and they express far more than possession {my professor} {your argument}. Independent forms: *mine, ours, yours, his, hers, its, theirs* — these stand alone as subject, object, or object of a preposition {hers is on the table} {put your coat with theirs}, and with *of* form an unobjectionable double possessive *{that letter of hers}*. No possessive personal pronoun takes an apostrophe.
- **[5.54] Possessive vs. contraction** — the apostrophe is the universal possessive signal *except* for pronouns (and *whose*), which is exactly why the four homophone pairs get botched: *its/it's*, *your/you're*, *whose/who's*, and *their/there/they're*. MECHANICAL — highest-frequency mechanical error in English prose; safe to auto-correct (verify context for *its/it's* and *whose/who's*, which need a clause check).
- **[5.55] Reflexive vs. intensive** — *-self* forms are either reflexive objects pointing back to the subject {the cat scared itself} {Gayo took it on herself to make the first move} {don't repeat yourself}, or intensive appositives for emphasis {I myself don't care} {did you knit that yourself?}. Any other use is wrong:
  - ex: *The staff and myself thank you for your contribution.* → *The staff and I thank you for your contribution.*
  - ex: *Deliver the equipment to my partner or myself.* → *Deliver the equipment to my partner or me.*
  - MECHANICAL (pattern: *myself/yourself* in subject or non-reflexive object position).
- **[5.56] Demonstratives** — *this/that* (singular), *these/those* (plural); *this/these* = near in space, time, or thought; *that/those* = remote. The antecedent may be a noun, phrase, clause, sentence, or implied thought, as long as it's clear. *Kind/sort* take singular demonstratives *{this kind of magazine}*, plurals *kinds/sorts* take plural *{these kinds of magazines}*. A demonstrative cannot stand alone for a human: *I heard Mike's son playing. That child is talented* — dropping *child* is an error. MECHANICAL.
- **[5.57] Reciprocals** — *each other* and *one another* express mutual relationship {the two finally understood each other} {all of us here love one another}; contrast reflexive vs. reciprocal: *we all checked ourselves for ticks* (each inspected themself) vs. *we checked one another for ticks* (each inspected others). They take -'s for possession {one another's watches}. Careful writers reserve *each other* for two and *one another* for more than two (never universally observed).

### Interrogative and relative pronouns (5.58–5.71)

- **[5.58] Interrogatives defined** — *who, what, which*; only *who* declines: *who* (nom.), *whom* (obj.), *whose* (poss.). *Who* as subject {who washed the dishes today?} or predicate nominative {it was who?}; *whom* as object of a verb {whom did you see?} or preposition {for whom is this building named?} (see 5.70).
- **[5.59] Referent of interrogatives** — *who* is universal/general (any person, any identity) {who wants to see that movie?} {who were your greatest inspirations?}; *which/what* + noun are selective/limited to a group {which explorers visited China in the sixteenth century?} {what ice-skater is your favorite?}; either *which* or *what* for a person, animal, or thing {what kind of bird is that?}; *what of a person* asks character/occupation {what do you think of aged cheddar?} (their example, oddly).
- **[5.60] Relatives defined** — *who, which, what, that* introduce a dependent clause and relate it to the independent clause. Only *who* declines. *Who* = human (any person); *which* = animal or thing; *what* = nonliving thing, and only 2nd/3rd person; *that* = human, animal, or thing, any person. A relative qualifying a noun in its own clause is a "relative adjective."
- **[5.61] Relative + personal pronoun** — the relative agrees with its antecedent in gender, person, number; a following personal pronoun referring to the same antecedent matches it {Grady thanked the baker, who brought her signature cinnamon bread to her neighbors' party} {we waved to Aliyah, who was training their dog to carry their groceries}; if it refers elsewhere, it matches *that* antecedent {I saw the boy and also the girl who pushed him down}. A personal pronoun does **not** govern the relative's case: *she was referring to me, who never graduated from college* (technically correct, sounds archaic) — when correctness sounds artificial, use "preventive grammar": {I have been wronged; I have a grievance}.
- **[5.62] Positional nuances** — nominative when nothing intervenes between the relative and its verb {the professor who lectured was brilliant}; with intervening words, case comes from the verb/preposition inside the intervening clause {the person whom I called is no longer there} {it was John who they thought was in the bleachers}. JUDGMENT; do not auto-correct *whom*.
- **[5.63] Antecedent of a relative** — usually a noun/pronoun in the independent clause; **it should immediately precede the pronoun** {the diadem that I told you about is in this gallery}. A phrase or clause antecedent can be ambiguous: *In the bedroom of the villa, which was painted pink* — the bedroom or the villa?
- **[5.64] Remote relative clauses (dangling *that/which/who*)** — separating the relative clause from its antecedent is a defect; the longer the separation and the more nouns in between, the worse.
  - ex: *the guy down the street that runs through our neighborhood* (does the *that*-clause modify *guy* or *street*?)
  - ex: AMBIGUOUS *Stress caused her to lose the freedom from fear, which she once enjoyed.* → CLEAR *Stress caused her to lose what she once enjoyed: freedom from fear.*
  - ex: AMBIGUOUS *After the news came out, the CEO fired the aide, a friend of the president, who was the target of the investigation.* → CLEAR *After the news came out, the CEO fired the aide, who was the target of the investigation and also a friend of the president.*
  > "For clarity, pronouns must have unambiguous antecedents."
- **[5.65] Omitted antecedent** — with no expressed antecedent, use *what* = "that which" {is this what you were looking for?}; with an antecedent, use *who*, *that* (restrictive clause) {where are the books that Jones told us about?}, or *which* (nonrestrictive) {the sun, which is shining brightly, feels warm on my face}. (See 6.29.) MECHANICAL *that/which* split, JUDGMENT on restrictiveness.
- **[5.66] Relative with antecedent *one*** — take the plural noun phrase as antecedent, not *one*: *Lily is one who cares deeply* (simple) but *Lily is one of those people who care deeply* (plural verb). Proof by reordering: *of those people who care deeply, Lily is one.* MECHANICAL — a very common agreement bug.
  - ex: *She is one of those employees who works tirelessly.* → *… who work tirelessly.*
- **[5.67] Genitive relatives** — *of whom* / *of which* are bookish {the child, the mother of whom we talked about, is in kindergarten}; rephrase {the child whose mother we talked about is in kindergarten}. *What* forms *of what*; *that* forms *of that* (preposition at the end) {no legend that we know of} or *of which* {no legend of which we know}.
- **[5.68] *Whose* vs. *of which*** — *whose* substitutes for *of which* for things as well as persons; the objection to nonhuman *whose* is baseless and the possessive is smoother:
  - ex: *a movie whose conclusion is unforgettable* over *a movie the conclusion of which is unforgettable*; *the company whose stock rose faster* over *the company the stock of which rose faster*. JUDGMENT (prefer *whose*).
- **[5.69] Compound relatives** — *whoever, whomever, whichever, whatever* (-ever forms) apply universally to any/all persons or things {whatever you do, let me know} {whoever needs to write a report about this book may borrow it}.
- **[5.70] *Who* vs. *whom*** — *who/whoever* nominative (subject {whoever said that?}, predicate nominative); *whom/whomever* objective (object of a verb {you called whom?} or preposition {to whom are you referring?}). Three traps: (1) inverted interrogative syntax — rearrange to find the function: *Whom should I say is calling?* → *Who should I say is calling?* (*who* is subject of *is*); (2) the pronoun's case is fixed **inside its own clause**, whatever the clause's job in the main sentence:
  - ex: *I'll talk to whomever will listen.* → *I'll talk to whoever will listen.* (*whoever* is subject of *will listen*)
  - ex: INFORMAL *Whoever you choose will suit me.* → FORMAL *Whomever you choose will suit me.* (object of *choose*)
  (3) nested attribution clauses distract (*you choose*). Usage note: outside the most formal prose, *whom* survives mainly as object of a preposition {for whom is it intended}; in dialogue and informal prose *who* displaces it — except after a preposition {for whom the bell tolls}. JUDGMENT: never blanket-replace *whom*.
- **[5.71] Indefinites** — *another, any, both, each, either, neither, none, one, other, some, such*; compounds *anybody, anyone, anything, everybody, everyone, everything, nobody, no one, oneself, somebody, someone*. *Each/either/neither* are "distributive." As subjects they are **singular** {everyone is enjoying the dinner} {everybody takes notes during the first week}, though informal prose allows a plural follow-up pronoun {nobody could describe the music; they hadn't been listening to it} {everyone understood the risk, but they were lured by promises of big returns}. Possessive: -'s {that is no one's fault} or *else* + -'s {don't interfere with anybody else's business}. MECHANICAL for the verb, JUDGMENT for the resumptive pronoun (singular *they* is fine there).

## Adjectives (5.72–5.100)

- **[5.72] Adjectives defined** — a modifier of a noun/pronoun telling what sort, how many, how large, whose; it may modify an understood noun {he is a good as well as a wise man}. Descriptive {red wagon} {human error} or limiting {three pigs} {this time}. Suffix inventory: -able, -al, -ary, -ed, -en, -esque, -ful, -ible, -ic, -ish, -ive, -less, -like, -ly, -ous, -some, -y — but many have no marker {old} {tall} {brilliant}.
- **[5.73] Proper adjectives** — capitalized {a New York minute} {a Cuban cigar} {a Canadian dollar} (but see 8.62); capitalizing the adjective does not capitalize the noun. Avoid comma-bearing place names as adjectives {we met in a Moscow, Idaho, restaurant} → use a prepositional phrase {we met in a restaurant in Moscow, Idaho}, or shorten if the place is known (*a New Delhi marketplace*, *a DC office*). MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT.
- **[5.74] Articles defined** — limiting adjectives marking definite (*the*) or indefinite (*a/an*) {a road} {an elaborate design} {the yellow-brick road}.
- **[5.75] Definite article** — points to (1) the already-understood {the package is here = the package that you expected is here}, (2) the about-to-be-described {the sights of Chicago}, or (3) the important {the grand prize}; singular or plural.
- **[5.76] Indefinite article** — a nonspecific member of a class; singular {a student at Princeton}, uncountable {a multitude}, or generalized {an idea inspired by Milton's Paradise Lost}.
- **[5.77] Indefinite article with specific reference** — occasionally *a/an* marks a specific thing {I saw a great movie last night}, while *the* can be generic {the Scots are talking about independence}.
- **[5.78] *A* or *an* by sound, not spelling** — *a* before consonant sounds, including /y/, /h/, /w/ {a eulogy} {a historic occasion} {a onetime pass}; *an* before vowel sounds {an insurance agent} {an X-Files episode} {an hour ago}; same rule for abbreviations and numerals: *a BTU calculation*, *a PDF file*, *an MBA degree*, *an ATM* (see 7.32–33, 10.10). MECHANICAL — safe to auto-correct.
- **[5.79] Articles with coordinate nouns** — one article can serve a series when the same type applies {the rosebush and hedge need trimming}; especially when the things form one idea {the horse and rider appear to be one entity}; and never repeat the article when a plural noun covers all {in the first and second years of college}. Repeat for distinction or emphasis {the time, the money, and the effort were all wasted}. With indefinites, only the first article need match its noun: *bring an umbrella, raincoat, and hat* (not *a raincoat, an hat*). MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT.
- **[5.80] Articles change meaning** — *an officer and gentleman* (one man, two traits) vs. *an officer and a friend* (two people); *the red and blue cloth* (one fabric, both colors) vs. *the red and the blue cloth* (two fabrics). For one fabric, hyphenate as a compound modifier: *red-and-blue cloth*; for two, repeat the noun (*the red cloth and the blue cloth*) or attach it to the first adjective (*the red cloth and the blue*). JUDGMENT.
- **[5.81] Omitted article / zero article** — dropping an article can change meaning: *the news brought us little comfort* (no comfort) vs. *a little comfort* (some). The implied-but-omitted article is the "zero article," idiomatic with time, illness, transport, routines, meals {by sunset} {has cancer} {travel by train} {go to bed} {make breakfast} — note *in the morning* vs. *at night*, *make the bed* vs. *go to bed*.
- **[5.82] Article as pronoun substitute** — a definite article can replace a possessive: *a patient who develops the described rash on ___ hands should inform ___ doctor* → either *their … their* (now usually preferable to "his or her"; see 5.266) or *the … the*.
- **[5.83] Position of adjectives** — attributive (before the noun) is default {perfect storm} {a good bowl of soup}. Postpose only when (1) the adjective carries special emphasis {reasons innumerable} {captains courageous}, (2) standard usage requires it {court-martial} {notary public}, (3) it is a predicate adjective after a linking verb {I am ready}, (4) it is an appositive framed by commas or dashes {the man, tall and thin, stood in the corner}, or (5) it modifies a pronoun of the type that takes a following adjective {anything good} {nothing important} {something wicked}. Some adjectives are predicate-only {the city is asleep} {the door was ajar}; some are attributive-only {utter nonsense} {a mere child}.
- **[5.84] Adjective after a possessive** — the adjective follows the possessive noun or pronoun {children's athletic shoes} {the company's former president} {her red dress} — unless you mean athletic children or a former company.
- **[5.85] Adjective modifying a pronoun** — usually follows it {the searchers found him unconscious} {some like it hot}, sometimes as a predicate {it was insensitive}; occasionally precedes {flustered, he sat down} {uncertain, she demurred}.
- **[5.86] Predicate adjective** — follows a linking verb and modifies the *subject* {the child is afraid} {the night became colder} {this tastes delicious} {I feel bad}. If it modifies a noun in the predicate it is not a predicate adjective: *the train will be late* (modifies *train*) vs. *the train will be here at a late hour* (modifies *hour*).
- **[5.87] Dates as adjectives** — month–year and month–day need no hyphen or comma as modifiers {October 31 festivities} {December 2022 financial statement}; a full month–day–year date would need commas on both sides {the May 27, 2024, ceremonies} — **avoid that construction** {commencement ceremonies on May 27, 2024}. MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT.
- **[5.88] Three degrees** — positive {hard} {a big balloon}, comparative {harder}, superlative {hardest}.
- **[5.89] Comparatives** — one- or two-syllable adjectives take -er (synthetic) {light–lighter} {merry–merrier}; three or more syllables take *more/less* (periphrastic) {more intelligent} {less purposeful}. Most two-syllable adjectives take *more/less* {more hostile} {less fearful}, though those ending in -er, -le, -ow, -ure, -y often allow either {maturer / more mature} {mellower / more mellow}.
- **[5.90] Superlatives** — compare **at least three** things; -est (synthetic) for one or two syllables {light–lightest}, *most/least* for three or more {most quarrelsome} {least humorous}. Some two-syllable adjectives take -est {holiest} {noblest}; most take *most/least* {most fruitful} {least reckless}. JUDGMENT: a superlative for two things is a defect (*the taller of the two*, not *the tallest*).
- **[5.91] Forming comparatives/superlatives** — (1) double a final consonant after a single vowel in a monosyllable {red–redder–reddest}; (2) drop silent -e {polite–politer–politest}; (3) **participles used as adjectives take *more/most*, never a suffix** {this teleplay is more boring than the first one} {I am most tired on Fridays}; (4) *real, right, wrong* take only *more/most* (informal); (5) *eager, proper, somber* take only *more/most*; (6) *un-* prefixed two-syllable adjectives allow either {unhappiest} {most unhappy}; (7) irregulars {good–better–best} {less–lesser–least} — check a dictionary; (8) **never both a suffix and *more/most***: *more greener*, *least greenest* are double comparatives/superlatives and nonstandard. Items (3) and (8) are MECHANICAL.
- **[5.92] Equal and unequal comparison** — higher degree: -er/-est or *more/most*; lower: *less/least*; equivalence: *as … as* {this is as old as that}, or *so* usually in the negative {that test was not so hard as the last one}.
- **[5.93] Noncomparable adjectives** — absolute-state adjectives (*entire, impossible, pregnant, unique*) take no comparative suffix, no *more/most/less/least*, and no *very/largely/quite*. The licensed exception is deliberate rhetorical emphasis — the Constitution's "a more perfect Union." MECHANICAL (*very unique*, *more impossible*) with a JUDGMENT escape hatch.
- **[5.94] Participial adjectives** — present participle {the dining room} {a rising star}; past participle {an endangered species} {a completed assignment} {a proven need}; some exist only as adjectives {a shaven face} {a graven image}. Modify with *quite* {a quite fatigued traveler}, *barely* {a barely concealed wince}, *little* {a little-known fact}, or *very much* {a very much distrusted public official}. Use *very* alone when the participle is strongly adjectival {very tired} {very drunk}; *very much* when it still reads as a verb {very much appreciated} {very much delayed}; with mid-spectrum participles (*bored, interested, pleased, satisfied*) drop the *much*. JUDGMENT (see also 5.114).
- **[5.95] Coordinate adjectives** — adjectives in sequence modifying the same noun are separated by commas or *and* {skilled, experienced chess player} {nurturing and loving parent}. If one adjective modifies the noun and the other modifies the *combination*, they are not coordinate and take no comma: *a lethargic soccer player*, *white brick house*, *wrinkled canvas jacket* (a white house can be made of anything; a wrinkled jacket likewise).
  > "The most useful test is this: If and would fit between the two adjectives, a comma is necessary."
  - MECHANICAL (the *and* test) — a reliable auto-check.
- **[5.96] Phrasal adjectives (compound modifiers)** — (1) hyphenate before a noun to prevent misdirection {dog-eat-dog competition}; compare *small animal hospital* with *small-animal hospital*; (2) hyphenate an entire compound noun inside the phrasal adjective {time-clock-punching employees}; (3) hyphenate multiple phrasal adjectives before one noun {nineteenth-century song-and-dance numbers} {state-inspected assisted-living facility}; (4) shared endings take a suspended hyphen {middle- and upper-class operagoers}; (5) drop the plural in amounts and durations {a 3,000-pound truck} {a 24-hour-a-day schedule}, but keep it for fractions {a two-thirds majority}; (6) if the phrasal adjective gets awkward, recast.
  - ex: *The news about the lower-than-expected third-quarter earnings disappointed investors* → *The news about the third-quarter earnings, which were lower than expected, disappointed investors* (or *Investors were disappointed by the third-quarter earnings, which were lower than expected*).
  - MECHANICAL for hyphenation; JUDGMENT for recasting. (See also 7.87–96.)
- **[5.97] Exceptions to phrasal-adjective hyphenation** — (1) after a linking verb, usually unhyphenated: *a well-trained athlete* vs. *an athlete who is well trained*; (2) no hyphen when a proper name begins the phrase {the Monty Python school of comedy}; (3) no hyphen after an -ly adverb {a sharply worded reprimand} — but *a not-so-sharply-worded reprimand*. MECHANICAL.
- **[5.98] Adjectives as nouns** — established conversions are fine (*a postmortem*, *collectibles*, *the French*); anything not established needs a second look, and an available noun is almost always better (*collaboration*, not *collaborative* as a noun). (See also 5.27–29.)
  > "If there's an alternative, it will almost certainly be better."
- **[5.99] Adjectives as verbs** — rare, usually jargon or slang {the cargo tanks were inerted by introducing carbon dioxide into them} {it would be silly to low-key the credit for this achievement}; they "generally don't fit comfortably into formal prose." JUDGMENT — flag, don't auto-fix.
- **[5.100] Other parts of speech as adjectives** — nouns (5.27), pronouns (5.53), verbs (5.111).

## Verbs (5.101–5.160)

- **[5.101] Verbs generally** — the main word of the predicate, asserting something about the subject; a verb (usually imperative) can constitute a whole sentence with the subject understood {Run!} {Enjoy!} {Think!}. One-word sentences like *Why?* or *Yes* are elliptical, not verbless statements.
- **[5.102] Transitive vs. intransitive** — transitive requires an object and says what the subject does to it {the cyclist hit a curb}; a few take cognate objects {drink a drink} {build a building} {see the sights}. Intransitive needs none {the rescuer jumped}, though it may take an adverbial phrase {jumped to the ground}. Many verbs do both with a meaning shift (transitive *succeed him* = "follow and replace"; intransitive *succeed in identifying* = "accomplish"), and some with no shift at all (*ride*). Transitivity is tested by trying objects.
- **[5.103] Ergative (ambitransitive) verbs** — the transitive object becomes the intransitive subject {the impact shattered the windshield} / {the windshield shattered}; {the torpedo sank the boat} / {the boat sank}. Commercial jargon extends the shift (*the books shipped on January 16*; *how to grow your business*).
  > "Careful writers and editors employ such usages cautiously if at all, preferring well-established idioms."
- **[5.104] Regular vs. irregular** — regular (weak) verbs add -ed {draft–drafted–drafted} (or -d after -e {charge–charged}); nearly two hundred common verbs are irregular (strong) {begin–began–begun} {bet–bet–bet} {bind–bound–bound} {bite–bit–bitten}, varying by vowel change (ablaut), zero change, shared form, or ending change; *be* has eight forms (be, am, is, are, was, were, been, being). Meaning can change the form (*bid/bade/bidden*), as can figurative use (*wove a rug* / *weaved in traffic*). Where -ed and -t variants both exist, American English prefers -ed (*dreamed, learned, spelled*), British often -t (*dreamt, learnt, spelt*). MECHANICAL in part (a dictionary check); the -ed/-t choice is house style.
- **[5.105] Linking verbs** — link subject to a subjective complement, take no object {Mr. Block is the chief executive officer} {that snake is venomous}. Two kinds: be-verbs, and weakened intransitives (*appear, become, feel, look, seem, smell, taste*), often figurative {he fell heir to a large fortune} {the river ran dry}. Occasional linkers: *act, get, go, grow, lie, prove, remain, sit, stay, turn, wax*. Some passive constructions contain linkers {this band was judged best in the contest} {she was made sales-force manager}. No complement → not a linker {Kansas City, Kansas, is across the river} {there is an unfilled receptionist position}; nor when followed by an adverbial or a direct object {he appeared in court} {the dog smelled the scent}. JUDGMENT: this diagnosis drives the adverb/adjective choice (5.175).
- **[5.106] Phrasal verbs** — verb + preposition/particle functioning as adverb {settle down} {act up} {phase out}; **not hyphenated** as verbs, though the noun/adjective equivalents are: *to flare up* / *a flare-up*; *to step up the pace* / *a stepped-up pace*. (1) Keep the whole phrase when the sense is noncompositional (*hold up* = rob or delay; *get rid of*, *do away with*); (2) avoid the phrasal verb when the verb alone means the same (*rest up* → *rest*); (3) don't compress it into one word if a one-word noun exists (*burn out* / *burnout*); (4) keep the *to* separate after *in/on* {papers should be turned in to the teacher} {we held on to the railing}. MECHANICAL (*turn into* vs. *turn in to*).
- **[5.107] Principal vs. auxiliary** — principal verbs stand alone {he jogs}; auxiliaries combine with them to mark mood, tense, or voice {you must study} {I will go} {the show was interrupted}; core auxiliaries: *be, can, do, have, may, must, ought, shall, will*.
- **[5.108] Verb phrases** — auxiliary + principal {could happen} {must go} {will be leaving}; an adverb modifying the phrase normally sits **between the auxiliary and the principal verb** {could certainly happen} {must always go} {will soon be leaving}.
  > "The idea that verb phrases should not be 'split' in this way is quite mistaken (see 5.176)."
  - Negation: *not* after the first auxiliary {we have not called him}; interrogative: auxiliary first {must I repeat that?} {do you want more?}; negated interrogative {do you not want more?} or, more naturally, contracted {don't you want more?}. Contracted negatives are standard (*don't, won't, hasn't, doesn't*); *I am not* → *I'm not*, never *I amn't*; interrogative *aren't I?*. Emphasis variants: *he is not / he isn't / he's not*; *they have not / they haven't / they've not*. MECHANICAL (*I amn't*, *amn't I*).
- **[5.109] Contractions** — "Most types of writing benefit from the use of contractions. If used thoughtfully, contractions in prose sound natural and relaxed and make reading more enjoyable." Be-verbs and most auxiliaries contract with *not* (*aren't, wasn't, can't, couldn't, don't*); *oughtn't* is awkward — avoid. Rarer contractions (*I'd've, she'd've, it'd, should've, there're, who're, would've*) are mostly for dialogue. Some are ambiguous: *there's* = *there is* or *there has*; *I'd* = *I had* or *I would* — check context. JUDGMENT.
- **[5.110] Infinitives defined** — the uninflected base form, optionally preceded by *to* {to dance} {to dive}; dictionary headword form. *To* is dropped after auxiliaries {you must flee} and may be dropped after *bid, dare, feel, hear, help, let, make, need, see* {you dare say that to me?}; **retain it in the passive** {he cannot be heard to deny it} {they can be made to listen} and after *ought/ought not* (5.154). MECHANICAL for the passive case.
- **[5.111] Uses of the infinitive** — as verb (taking subject {we wanted the lesson to end}, object {try to throw the javelin higher}, complement {want to race home?}, or adverbial modifier {you need to think quickly in chess}) and as noun (subject {to fly is a lofty goal}, object {I want to hire a new assistant}); governed by verbs, nouns, adjectives, participles, or pronouns {cease to do evil} {talents to be improved} {eager to learn} {preparing to go} {let him do it}.
- **[5.112] Split infinitive** — permitted, and sometimes necessary.
  > "Although from about 1850 to 1925 many grammarians stated otherwise, it is now widely acknowledged that adverbs sometimes justifiably separate the to from the principal verb {they expect to more than double their income next year}. See also 5.176."
  - **Do not de-split mechanically.** Recasting can change the nuance (*it's best to always get up early* [*always* modifies *get up*] vs. *it's always best to get up early* [*always* modifies *best*]) or make the phrasing unnatural (*it's best to get up early always*).
- **[5.113] Dangling infinitive** — an infinitive phrase modifying a verb needs a grammatical subject (or an implied imperative subject) that could perform it.
  - ex: *To repair your car properly, it must be sent to a mechanic.* (no logical subject for *repair*) → *To repair your car properly, you must take it to a mechanic.*
  - JUDGMENT; among the highest-value edits.
- **[5.114] Participles** — nonfinite verbs with tense but no person/number/mood: present participle always -ing, denoting action in progress at the time of the main verb {watching intently for a mouse, the cat settled in to wait} {hearing his name, Jon turned to answer}; past participle usually -ed, denoting completed action {planted in the spring} {written last year}.
- **[5.115] Participial phrases** — participle plus modifiers/complements, used (1) as an adjective {nailed to the roof, the slate stopped the leaks} {she pointed to the clerk drooping behind the counter} or (2) as an absolute phrase {generally speaking, I prefer spicy dishes} {they having arrived, we went out on the lawn for our picnic}. (For participial adjectives see 5.94; for danglers see 5.119.)
- **[5.116] Gerunds** — present participle used as a noun: subject {complaining about it won't help}, object of a verb {I don't like your cooking}, predicate nominative {his favorite pastime is sleeping}, object of a preposition {reduce erosion by terracing the fields}. Sometimes interchangeable with an infinitive (*to lie is wrong* / *lying is wrong*).
- **[5.117] Participle vs. gerund** — decide by function: participle = modifier {the running water} or verb-phrase member {the meter is running}, modified only by an adverb {the swiftly running water}; gerund = noun {running is great exercise}, modified by an adjective {sporadic running and walking makes for a great workout}.
- **[5.118] Fused participles** — a gerund is modified by an adjective or possessive {double-parking is prohibited} {Critt's parking can be hazardous to pedestrians}; using a plain noun/pronoun with a gerund is traditionally a fault:
  - ex: INFORMAL *Me painting your fence depends on you paying me first.* → FORMAL *My painting your fence depends on your paying me first.*
  - Legitimate exceptions where the possessive is unidiomatic: nonpersonal noun {we're not responsible for the jewelry having been mislaid}, nonpersonal pronoun {we all insisted on something being done}, group of pronouns {the settlement depends on some of them agreeing to compromise}.
  - JUDGMENT (formal prose: prefer the possessive; informal: leave it).
- **[5.119] Dangling participles** — a participle with no syntactic relationship to the nearest subject becomes, in effect, a preposition, and the sentence turns illogical, ambiguous, or incoherent.
  > "A participle that has no syntactic relationship with the nearest subject is called a dangling participle or just a dangler. ... Recasting the sentence so that the misplaced modifier is associated with the correct noun is the only effective cure."
  - ex: *Frequently used in early America, experts suggest that shaming is an effective punishment.* (*used* modifies *shaming*, not *experts*) → *Experts suggest that shaming, often used in early America, is an effective punishment.* (Or reword to drop the participle.)
  - ex: *Being a thoughtful mother, I believe Meg gives her children good advice.* (reads as self-praise) → *I believe that because Meg is a thoughtful mother, she gives her children good advice.*
  - ex: passive main clause creates one: *Finding that the questions were not ambiguous, the exam grades were not changed.* → *Finding that the questions were not ambiguous, the teacher did not change the exam grades.*
  - ex: expletive *it/there* subject: *Reviewing the suggestions, it is clear that no consensus exists.* → *Our review of the suggestions shows that no consensus exists.*
  - **Agent check:** for every sentence-initial participial phrase, find the subject of the main clause; if it can't perform the participle's action, rewrite. Also flag *it/there* main clauses after participles. JUDGMENT, high frequency, high value.
- **[5.120] Dangling gerunds** — a gerund as object of a preposition whose performer is absent or wrong.
  - ex: *After finishing the research, the screenplay was easy to write.* → *After Gero finished the research, the screenplay was easy to write* (or *After finishing the research, Gero found the screenplay easy to write*).
  - ex: *While driving to San Antonio, my phone ran out of power.* → *While I was driving to San Antonio, my phone ran out of power.*
- **[5.121] Five properties of verbs** — voice, mood, tense, person, number; verbs are conjugated to show them.
- **[5.122] Active vs. passive voice** — active: subject acts {the judge levied a $50 fine}; passive: subject is acted on {the tree's branch was broken by the storm}, formed with a be-verb (or colloquial *get*) + past participle {the cart is pulled by the ox}. Only transitive verbs have voice.
  > "As a matter of style, passive voice {the matter will be given careful consideration} is often inferior to active voice {we will consider the matter carefully}."
  - Voice choice can encode point of view: *the mouse was caught by the cat* (the mouse's experience) vs. *the cat caught the mouse* (the cat's). **Diagnostic:** a be-verb alone is not passive — *he is thinking about his finances* is a be-verb + present participle (progressive active).
- **[5.123] Progressive conjugation** — be + present participle = progressive, still active if the verb is transitive {the ox is pulling the cart}; be + being + past participle = passive progressive {the cart is being pulled}.
- **[5.124] Mood** — indicative, imperative, subjunctive.
- **[5.125] Indicative** — facts, opinions, questions {amethysts cost very little} {does that bush produce yellow roses?}.
- **[5.126] Imperative** — verb stem for commands {go away!}, requests {bring the tray in here}, exclamations {help!}, permission {come in!}; the subject *you* is understood, and a name is direct address, not subject {Cindy, take good care of yourself}. Soften with *please* or recast in the indicative {will you stop at the store, please?} — "In some contexts it could be too blunt or unintentionally rude."
- **[5.127] Subjunctive** — for states "not as a reality but as a mental conception": doubtful, imagined, desired, conditional, hypothetical, contrary to fact. Persists in fixed expressions {perish the thought} {heaven help us} {be that as it may}.
- **[5.128] Subjunctive vs. indicative, and three traps** — subjunctive marks contrary-to-fact condition {if I were you}, wishes {if I were a rich man}, conjecture {oh, were it so}, demands {the landlord insists that the dog go}, suggestions {I recommend that she take a vacation}.
  - ex: INFORMAL *If it wasn't for your help, I wouldn't have a chance of finding the place.* → FORMAL *If it weren't for your help …*
  - ex: NONSTANDARD *I called to see whether she were in.* → STANDARD *… whether she was in.* (not contrary to fact)
  - ex: *If I would have gone, I would …* → *If I had gone, I would …* (double conditional is the error)
  - Not every *if* takes the subjunctive: when the condition may be true and the writer doesn't know, use the indicative {if I am right about this, please call} {if Napoleon was in fact poisoned with arsenic, historians will need to reevaluate his associates}.
- **[5.129] Present subjunctive** — base form {he recommended that we be ready at a moment's notice} {we insist that he retain control of the accounting department}; also *be* + simple past or past-form auxiliary + infinitive {the chair proposed that the company be acquired by the employees} {might he take down the decorations this afternoon?}.
- **[5.130] Past subjunctive** — *were* for all subjects, referring to present or future contrary to fact {if only I had a chance} {I wish I were safe at home} {if she were any different}. Compare *If I am threatened, I will quit* (indicative) with *If I were threatened, I would quit* (subjunctive); *If the canary sings, I smile* with *If the canary sang (or should sing, or were to sing), I would smile*.
- **[5.131] Past-perfect subjunctive** — *had* + past participle for past counterfactuals {if he had been there} {if I had gone}. Compare *If it arrived, it was not properly filed* (indicative) with *If it had arrived, it could have changed the course of history* (subjunctive).
- **[5.132] Tense** — present, past, future, each with perfect (completed) and progressive (continuing) forms.
- **[5.133] Present tense** — present acts/states {the dog howls} {the air is cold}; habitual action or general truth {cats prowl nightly} {polluted water is a health threat}; timeless facts and enduring works {Julius Caesar describes his strategies in The Gallic War} {the Pompeiian mosaics are exquisite}; narrative present for plot {the scene takes place aboard the Titanic}. The last two are the **historical present** — "Characters in books, plays, and films do things—not did them." To distinguish present from past action within a work, use the present perfect {Hamlet, who has spoken with his father's ghost, reveals what he has learned to no one but Horatio}. JUDGMENT (writers about literature get this wrong constantly).
- **[5.134] Past indicative** — a past act at an explicit or implicit point {the auction ended yesterday}; -ed (with consonant doubling, -y → -i, silent -e → -d); *travel–traveled* but *prefer–preferred* (doubling follows stress in American English; British doubles all). Irregulars vary {give–gave–given} {stride–strode–stridden} {read–read–read}.
- **[5.135] Future** — *will* + stem {will walk} {will drink}. *Shall* survives mainly in first-person questions {shall we go?} and legal requirements {the debtor shall pay within 30 days}; elsewhere prefer *will*, or *must* for legal requirements.
- **[5.136] Present perfect** — *have/has* + past participle {I have put away the clothes} {it has been a long day}; a completed act or one continuing to the present. Distinct from the past tense: present perfect = indefinite past {I have played golf there before} or past action touching the present {I have played cards for the last eighteen hours}; past tense = specific or remote time. JUDGMENT — a common analytical error is past for present perfect (*I saw him lately*).
- **[5.137] Past perfect (pluperfect)** — *had* + past participle, for an act completed before another past time {the engineer had driven the train to the roundhouse before we arrived} {by the time we stopped to check the map, the rain had begun falling}. JUDGMENT: use only for genuine anteriority, not as a general "more past."
- **[5.138] Future perfect** — *will have* + past participle, completed before a future time {the entomologist will have collected sixty more specimens before the semester ends} {the court will have adjourned by five o'clock}.
- **[5.139] Progressive tenses** — be + present participle across all six tenses (*is playing, has been playing, was playing, had been playing, will be playing, will have been playing*); passive progressives: *I am being dealt the cards*, *I was being dealt the cards*.
- **[5.140] Mixing tenses** — "A single passage may refer to related events occurring at different times. Choose the tense that best shows when for each one."
  - ex: *Because the students passed their final exams, they are gathering for the graduation ceremonies and will soon file into the auditorium.*
- **[5.141] Verb person** — first (speaking), second (spoken to), third (spoken of).
- **[5.142] Verb number** — only third-person present-indicative singular changes form {I sketch} {you sketch} {she sketches} {they sketch}; *you* and singular *they* always take plural verbs. MECHANICAL.
- **[5.143] Agreement in person and number** — singular subject → singular verb; plural → plural; two subjects joined by *and* → plural {Socrates and Plato were wise}; joined by *or/nor* → agree with the last-named {Bob or his friends have your key} {neither the twins nor Jon is prepared to leave}; collective noun as a unit → singular {the nation is powerful}; collective as plurality → plural {the faculty were divided in their sentiments}. MECHANICAL, high frequency.
- **[5.144] Indefinite-pronoun subjects** — singular {everyone receives credits for this course} {somebody knows where the car is}. MECHANICAL.
- **[5.145] Relative pronoun as subject** — number from the antecedent {a woman who likes skydiving} {people who collect books}; the *one of those who* trap:
  - ex: NONSTANDARD *She is one of those employees who works tirelessly.* → STANDARD *… who work tirelessly.* (Reorder: *Of those employees who work tirelessly, she is one.*)
  - MECHANICAL.
- **[5.146] False attraction to a predicate noun** — the subject governs the verb, not the predicate noun {mediocrity and complacency are the source of his ire} {the source of his ire is mediocrity and complacency}. When correcting the verb makes the sentence awkward, rewrite:
  - ex: NONSTANDARD *My downfall are sweets.* → STANDARD *My downfall is sweets.* → BETTER *Sweets are my downfall.*
- **[5.147] Misleading connectives** — *along with, as well as, in addition to, together with* do not pluralize a singular subject; if the singular verb sounds awkward, switch to *and*:
  - ex: NONSTANDARD *The bride as well as her bridesmaids were dressed in mauve.* → STANDARD *The bride as well as her bridesmaids was dressed in mauve.* → BETTER *The bride and her bridesmaids were dressed in mauve.*
  - MECHANICAL (verb) + JUDGMENT (whether to recast).
- **[5.148] Agreement in first and second person** — *I am, you are, he is, we are, they are*; with *or/either–or/neither–nor*, the verb traditionally agrees with the nearer subject {either he or I am in for a surprise} {either you or he is right} {neither you nor I am a plumber}. These are so awkward that the better move is to recast {one of us is in for a surprise} {one of you is right} {neither of us is a plumber}.
- **[5.149] Auxiliary verbs** — irregular helping verbs preceding the principal verb to mark voice, tense, mood (see 5.107).
- **[5.150] Modal auxiliaries** — express ability, necessity, possibility, willingness, obligation {they might be there} {she could be leaving at this very moment}. All are "defective" (missing forms).
- **[5.151] *Can* and *could*** — *can* for ability, capacity, permission {you can carry this trunk}; permission-sense *can* is colloquial for *may* {can I go to the movies?}; *can* also marks actual possibility or common experience {storms can be severe in spring} {days can pass before a decision is announced}. *Could* = past ability {she could hum a tune at six months of age} or a softer *can* about the future {we could travel to Cancun if you wanted to} ("would be able to").
- **[5.152] *May* and *might*** — *may* = permission {you may go to the movies} or possibility {I may go to the movies}; negated permission often becomes *must not* {You must not climb that tree}. *May* connotes likelihood {we may get there on time}; *might* connotes stronger doubt {we might get there on time—if the traffic clears} and contrary-to-fact hypotheticals {we might have been able to make it if the traffic had been better}. JUDGMENT.
- **[5.153] *Must*** — necessity from will, circumstance, or rule {we must obey the rules} {all applications must be received by May 31 to be valid}, or a logical conclusion {that must be the right answer} {it must have been Donna who phoned}. Invariable form; no infinitive or participle; takes a bare infinitive.
- **[5.154] *Ought*** — reasonable expectation or duty {they ought to fix the fence}, or probable conclusion {they left at dawn, so they ought to be here soon}; more emphatic than *should*, less than *must*. Always with *to*, even negated {the driver ought not to have ignored the signal}; *to* may be dropped after *not* {you ought not worry} but "the better usage is to include it" {you ought not to worry}. MECHANICAL (missing *to*).
- **[5.155] *Should*** — past of *shall*, used for all persons; duty, compulsion, expectation {you should clean the garage today} {it should be ready by now}; inference {the package should have been delivered today}; attitude {how should I know?}. Slightly less emphatic than *ought* but far more frequent.
- **[5.156] *Will* and *would*** — *will* for future {she will be at her desk tomorrow}, certainty {I'm sure you will understand}, volition {I really will work out more}, requests/orders/offers {will you stop that!} {will you take $5 for it?}, typical behavior {she will read for hours on end}; *would* for past and conditionals {we would go fishing on Saturdays} {I would recognize the house if I saw it again}.
- **[5.157] *Do*** — emphatic auxiliary; present *does* (third-person singular, except singular *they*) / *do*; past *did*; participle *done*. Used only in present {we do plan some charity work} and past {did you speak?}; required with *not* in imperatives {do not touch!} {don't be late!}. Also a principal verb {he does well in school} and a pro-verb to avoid repetition {Marion dances well, and so do you} {he caught fewer mistakes than you did}.
- **[5.158] *Have*** — present *has* (third-person singular, except singular *they*) / *have*; past and participle *had*. As auxiliary it forms the perfect tenses {I have looked everywhere} {he had looked for a better rate}; *have to* = obligation {I have to finish this paper tonight!}; *had to* = past of *must* {I had to leave yesterday afternoon}. As principal: possession, action, experience {she has a car and a beat} {we'll have a party next week}; with *do* {do you have the time?} {Vicky did not have her coat}.
- **[5.159] Be-verbs: forms and uses** — eight forms; principal "exist" {I think, therefore I am}; auxiliary with present participle for progressive {the train is coming}, with past participle for passive {a signal was given} — "Often this type of construction can be advantageously changed to active voice {he gave the signal} {she dropped her earring}"; linking verb {truth is beauty} {we are the champions}; parts of compounds {a rock star wannabe} {a would-be hero} {a has-been}.
- **[5.160] Conjugation of *be*** — present: *am* (1st sg.), *is* (3rd sg., except singular *they*), *are* (all others); participle *being* {I am being stalked} {it is being reviewed}; past: *was* (1st/3rd sg.) / *were* (others) {she was} {we were}; past participle *been*; imperative *be* {be yourself!}. MECHANICAL (agreement).

## Adverbs (5.161–5.177)

- **[5.161] Adverbs generally** — modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs {she studied constantly} {really unusual} {very swiftly}, and also prepositions, conjunctions, and whole clauses {right over the lake} {exactly where I found it} {apparently you forgot to check your references}. Some adverbs modify only adjectives/adverbs (intensifiers: *quite, rather, really, somewhat, too*) — you can't say *He spoke very*. *Adverb* is also the catchall class (*not, please*, infinitival *to*, particles).
- **[5.162] Sentence adverbs** — modify a whole sentence, often marking doubt or certainty: *clearly, maybe, possibly, undoubtedly* {fortunately, we've had rain this week} {undoubtedly he drove his car to the depot}.
- **[5.163] -ly formation** — -ly, or -ally after most -ic words {slow–slowly} {public–publicly} {pedantic–pedantically}; -y → -i {happy–happily} but *shy–shyly*; silent -e drops {true–truly} {whole–wholly}; -le → -ly {terrible–terribly}; double -l takes -y {dull–dully}. Clumsy forms (*juvenilely, sillily, ghastlily, uglily*) should be rephrased {in a ghastly manner} {in an ugly way}. Other suffixes: -style {cowboy-style}, -ways {sideways}, -ward {skyward}, -wise {clockwise}, and phrases {matter-of-factly}. Not every -ly word is an adverb {lovely} {curly}. MECHANICAL.
- **[5.164] Adverbs without suffixes** — *almost, never, here, now, just, seldom, late, near, too*.
- **[5.165] Flat (bare) adverbs** — an adverb with an -ly form whose adjective form works as well or better, especially with imperatives in informal contexts {drive slow} {hold on tight} {tell me quick}; some exist only flat {work fast}; some change meaning: *I am working hard* vs. *I am hardly working*. JUDGMENT/register.
- **[5.166] Phrasal and compound adverbs** — phrasal: two or more words as a unit {in the meantime} {for a while} {here and there}; compound: one word from several {notwithstanding} {heretofore} {thereupon}.
  > "Compound adverbs should be used cautiously and sparingly because they tend to make the tone stuffy."
- **[5.167] Positive adverbs** — quality with no comparison {the nurse spoke softly} {the choir sang merrily}.
- **[5.168] Comparative adverbs** — compares two actions {Bitey worked longer than Arachne} {Rachel studied more industriously than Edith}; one-syllable non--ly adverbs take -er {sooner} {harder}; multisyllables take *more/less* {fenced more ineptly than the hero} {walking less haltingly today}; *early–earlier* (the -ly isn't a suffix).
- **[5.169] Superlative adverbs** — three or more {Sullie bowled fastest of all the cricketers} {Dunya defended her dissertation the most adamantly}; loosely, for emphasis {the pianist played most skillfully}; -est for one-syllable non--ly {soonest} {hardest}; *most/least* for multisyllables {I could see most acutely} {he chose least wisely}; *early–earliest*.
- **[5.170] Irregular adverbs** — {badly–worse–worst} {little–less–least}; check a dictionary.
- **[5.171] Noncomparable adverbs** — absolute by definition {eternally} {never} {singly} {uniquely} {universally}, and those of time {now} {then}, position {on}, number {first} {finally}, place {here}. MECHANICAL (*more uniquely*).
- **[5.172] Placement** — put the adverb as near as possible to what it modifies.
  > "To avoid miscues, an adverb should generally be placed as near as possible to the word it is intended to modify."
  - ex: *the marathoners submitted their applications to compete immediately* (does *immediately* modify *compete* or *submitted*?) → *the marathoners immediately submitted their applications to compete*
  - ex: *we nearly lost all our camping equipment* (saved) vs. *we lost nearly all our camping equipment* (almost everything lost)
  - Adverbs mark time {we'll meet again}, place {put the flowers here}, manner {speak softly}, degree {sales are very good}, reason {why Pat couldn't find the right answer}, consequence {we should therefore hasten to support her candidacy}, number {first, we need to get our facts straight}, and comment {Vic was undoubtedly late} {Imani clearly recalled everything}. JUDGMENT, very high value.
- **[5.173] Adverbs modifying non-verbs** — an adverb qualifying an adjective, adverb, preposition, or conjunction goes immediately before it {our vacation was very short} {the flight took too long} {partly over the property line} {leave only when the bell rings}; adverb + adjective grouped together = an adjective cluster {a classically trained pianist}.
- **[5.174] Adverbs with intransitive verbs** — generally follow the verb {the students sighed gloomily when homework was assigned} {the owl perched precariously on a thin branch}; exceptions *always, generally, never, often, rarely, seldom* may precede {mountaineers seldom succeed in climbing K2}.
- **[5.175] Adverbs with linking verbs** — do **not** use an adverb after a linking verb when the modifier describes the subject; use the predicate adjective:
  - ex: *the sculptor feels badly* (literally = impaired touch) → *the sculptor feels bad* (unwell or guilty)
  - Adverbs that normally precede intransitives may still modify linkers {she quickly became uncomfortable} {he sometimes seems dishonest}. MECHANICAL (smells/tastes/looks + -ly).
- **[5.176] Adverb inside a verb phrase or infinitive** — the normal place for a verb-phrase modifier is between auxiliary and principal verb {the administration has consistently repudiated this view} {the reports will soon generate controversy} {public opinion is sharply divided}.
  > "There has never been a rule against such placement, and in fact it's often preferable. (See 5.108.)"
  - Split infinitives are also fine: "If the adverb bears the emphasis in a phrase {to boldly go} {to strongly favor}, the split infinitive is justified and often even necessary." De-splitting can alter nuance or read unnaturally.
- **[5.177] *Only*** — functions as adjective, adverb, and conjunction; "It is probably poorly placed in sentences more often than any other word."
  > "Only emphasizes the word or phrase that immediately follows it. ... since in writing there is no guidance from intonation, rigorous placement of only aids reader comprehension."
  - ex: *I bought only tomatoes at the market* (nothing else) vs. *I bought tomatoes only at the market* (nothing other than tomatoes, or nowhere else) vs. *I bought tomatoes at the market only* (nowhere else)
  - Idiomatic speech puts *only* before the verb regardless (*I only bought tomatoes at the market*), which is acceptable orally because intonation disambiguates — not in writing. MECHANICAL (flag *only* not adjacent to its focus).

## Prepositions (5.178–5.201)

- **[5.178] Prepositions generally** — uninflected function words linking a noun element (the object) to the rest of the sentence: position (*about, above, below, on, under*), direction (*in, into, to, toward*), time (*after, before, during, until*), source (*from, of, out of*). The object is a noun or an objective-case pronoun {between me and them}. Usually the preposition precedes its object, but with relative *that* it follows {this is the moment (that) I've been waiting for}; it also often follows *which* {which alternative is your decision based on?} and *whom* {there is a banker (whom) I must speak with} (see 5.186).
- **[5.179] Simple vs. compound** — simple = single monosyllable {as} {at} {by}; compound = polysyllabic from two or more words {into} {outside} {upon}.
- **[5.180] Phrasal (complex) prepositions** — multiword units: *according to, because of, by means of, by reason of, by way of, contrary to, for the sake of, in accordance with, in addition to, in apposition with, in case of, in consideration of, in front of, in regard to, in respect to, in spite of, instead of, on account of, out of, with reference to, with regard to, with respect to*.
  > "Many of these phrasal prepositions are symptoms of officialese, bureaucratese, or other types of verbose style. If a single-word preposition will do in context, use it."
  - ex: *with regard to* / *in connection with* → *about*. MECHANICAL when a one-word equivalent exists.
- **[5.181] Participial prepositions** — participial forms functioning as prepositions (or subordinating conjunctions): *assuming, barring, concerning, considering, during, notwithstanding, owing to, provided, regarding, respecting, speaking of*. Unlike other participles they do **not** dangle {considering the road conditions, the trip went quickly} {regarding Watergate, he had nothing to say} (see 5.119). — Important exception to the dangling-modifier rule; don't "fix" these.
- **[5.182] Prepositional phrases** — preposition + object + modifiers; used as noun {for James to change his mind would be a miracle}, adverb {we strolled through the glade}, or adjective {we'd love to see the cathedrals of Paris}.
- **[5.183] Prepositional function** — space {to} {from} {out of} {into}, time {at} {for} {throughout} {until}, cause {because of} {on account of}, means {by} {with} {like}, possession {of} {without}, exception {but for} {besides} {except}, support {with} {for}, opposition {against}, concession {despite} {for all} {notwithstanding}.
- **[5.184] Placement of prepositional phrases** — put the phrase as close as possible to what it modifies.
  > "A prepositional phrase with an adverbial or adjectival function should be as close as possible to the word it modifies to avoid awkwardness, ambiguity, or unintended meanings."
  - ex: *Is there a person with a small dog named Sandy here?* (person or dog named Sandy?) → *Is there a person named Sandy here with a small dog?*
  - ex: *The woman with the Popular Front circulates petitions* vs. *The woman circulates petitions with the Popular Front.*
  - JUDGMENT, high value.
- **[5.185] Refinements** — a phrase modifying all elements of a compound follows the last element {the date, the place, and the budget for the wedding have been decided}; a singular subject plus a plural prepositional phrase keeps a singular predicate: *the man and his two daughters have arrived* vs. *the man with two daughters has arrived* / *the man has arrived with his two daughters*. MECHANICAL for the agreement.
- **[5.186] Ending with a preposition** — permitted; the prohibition is a superstition.
  > "The traditional caveat of yesteryear against ending sentences or clauses with prepositions is an unnecessary and pedantic restriction. And it is wrong."
  - Churchill (as reported): "That is the type of arrant pedantry up with which I shall not put."
  - ex: *This is the case I told you about* (more natural) vs. *This is the case about which I told you.* — "The 'rule' was an ill-founded superstition based on a false analogy to Latin grammar." **Never rewrite to avoid a terminal preposition.**
- **[5.187] Clashing prepositions** — a phrasal verb ending in a preposition followed by a phrase starting with the same one {he gives in in every argument}; recast {rather than continue arguing, he always gives in} or {in every argument, he gives in} (see also 6.59, and 5.106 on phrasal verbs). MECHANICAL (double *in in*, *on on*, *to to*).
- **[5.188] Elliptical prepositional phrases** — independent, detachable openers: *for example, for instance, in any event, in a word, in the last analysis, in the long run* {in any event, call me when you arrive}.
- **[5.189] Pronoun case in prepositional phrases** — objective {with me} {alongside her} {between them} (see also 5.20, 5.38). *Than*/*as* may be conjunction {he's taller than I [am]} or preposition {he's taller than me}; in formal and literary prose *than* has long been treated as a conjunction, and *taller than I* has predominated in edited American English from the beginning (and in British English until the 1990s), so *than me* reads as less polished; spoken English treats them as prepositions {you're better than me} {you're as well known as me}. A possessive may precede the object {to my house}.
- **[5.190] Functional variation** — a preposition always has an object; the same word may be adverb (no object) or preposition (object): *let's slide down the hill* vs. *we sat down*. *Than* and *but* can be prepositions: prepositional *but* = "except" {everyone but Fuzzy traveled abroad last summer} vs. conjunctive *but* {I like the cut but not the color}.
- **[5.191] *Like*** — traditionally an adjective {teens often see themselves as star-crossed lovers like Romeo and Juliet}; conjunctive *like* has been nonstandard since the seventeenth century though common colloquially {he ran like he was really scared} → *as* / *as if*: *it happened just like I said it would* → *as*; *you're looking around like you've misplaced something* → *as if*. Because *as/as if* are conjunctions they take nominative pronouns {do you work too hard, as I do?}; prepositional *like* takes objective {the person in that old portrait looks like me}. MECHANICAL (flag *like* + clause).
- **[5.192] *Of*-phrase and verb agreement** — with an indefinite-quantity noun + *of* + plural, the verb agrees with the noun before *of* {a group of cyclists is waiting to start} {the amount of herbs depends on how much flavor you want}. Exceptions: *percentage* is singular {a small percentage is acceptable} but plural when followed by *of* + plural {a small percentage of seeds are unlikely to germinate}; *majority* singular as collective {the majority is wheat} but plural with a spelled-out plural phrase {the majority of wild animals never attack people}; *number of*: *the number of options keeps changing* vs. *a number of people have changed their minds*; for *one of*, see 5.66. MECHANICAL.
- **[5.193] Avoiding overuse of prepositions** —
  > "Prepositions can easily be overused. Stylistically, a good ratio to strive for is one preposition for every ten to fifteen words."
  - Five methods follow (5.194–5.198). MECHANICAL: count prepositions per 100 words.
- **[5.194] Cut the prepositional phrase** — if context supplies it: *the most important ingredient in this recipe* → *the most important ingredient*.
- **[5.195] Cut unnecessary prepositions (kill nominalizations)** — nouns in -ance, -ence, -ity, -ment, -sion, -tion are often deverbal and drag prepositions in: *during her performance of the concerto* ≈ *while she performed the concerto*.
  > "These nouns are sometimes called nominalizations or zombie nouns."
  - ex: *our efforts toward maximization of profits failed* → *our efforts to maximize profits failed*. MECHANICAL (suffix + *of* pattern).
- **[5.196] Replace the phrase with an adverb** — *the president spoke with force* → *the president spoke forcefully*.
- **[5.197] Replace the phrase with a genitive** — *I was dismayed by the complexity of the street map* ≈ *The street map's complexity dismayed me* (see 5.22).
- **[5.198] Use active voice to drop *by*** — *the ship was sailed by an experienced crew* = *an experienced crew sailed the ship*.
- **[5.199] Prepositional idioms** — "Among the most persistent word-choice issues are those concerning prepositions." Learn the pairs: *fill A with B* / *instill B into A*; *replace A with B* / *substitute B for A*; *prefix A to B* / *preface B with A*; *force A into B* / *enforce B on A*; *A implies B*, so *you infer B from A*. MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT.
- **[5.200] Shifting idioms** — usage moves (traditional *oblivious of* vs. contemporary *oblivious to*); choose for euphony (*ruminate on/about/over*) and be alert to connotation (*smitten with* vs. *smitten by*). JUDGMENT.

## Conjunctions (5.202–5.212)

- **[5.202] Conjunctions defined** — function words connecting sentences, clauses, or words within a clause {my daughter graduated from college in December, and my son will graduate from high school in May} {I said hello, but no one answered} {we're making progress slowly but surely}. **In Standard English, conjunctions connect pronouns in the same case** {he and she are colleagues} {the teacher encouraged her and me}. A pronoun after *than*/*as* is normally nominative even with the clause understood {you are wiser than I [am]} {you seem as pleased as she [does]}, except informally {you are wiser than me} — where *than* reads as a preposition (see 5.189). MECHANICAL (case after *and/or/than/as*).
- **[5.203] Simple, compound, phrasal** — simple {and, but, if, or, though}; compound single words {although, because, nevertheless, notwithstanding, unless}; phrasal {as though, inasmuch as, in case, provided that, so that, supposing that}. Two main classes: coordinating and subordinating.
- **[5.204] Coordinating** — join words or groups of equal grammatical rank {are you speaking to him or to me?} {the results are disappointing but not discouraging}; may be single or correlative.
- **[5.205] Correlative** — pairs: *as–as, if–then, either–or, neither–nor, both–and, where–there, so–as, not only–but also*. They **must frame structurally identical parts** {she wanted both to win the gold medal and to set a new record} {they not only read the book but also saw the movie} {if the first claim is true, then the second claim must be false}. MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT — faulty parallelism after *not only … but also* and *both … and* is a standard defect.
- **[5.206] Subordinating** — connect clauses of unequal rank, introducing the dependent clause {follow this road until you reach the highway} {that squirrel is friendly because people feed it} {Marcus promised that he would help}.
- **[5.207] Semantic classes of subordinators** — (1) comparison/degree: *than, as, else, otherwise, rather, as much as, as far as, as well as* {is a raven less clever than a magpie?} {these amateur musicians play as well as professionals} {it's not true as far as I can discover}; (2) time: *since, until, as long as, as soon as, before, after, when, as, while* {while we waited, it began to snow} {the tire went flat as we were turning the corner} {we'll start the game as soon as everyone understands the rules} {the audience returned to the auditorium after the concert's resumption was announced}; (3) condition/assumption: *if, though, unless, except, without, once* {once you sign the agreement, we can begin remodeling the house} {your thesis must be presented next week unless you have a good reason to postpone it} {I'll go on this business trip if I can fly first class}; (4) reason/concession: *as, inasmuch as, why, because, for, since, though, although, albeit* {since you won't share the information, I can't help you} {Sir John decided to purchase the painting although it was very expensive} {she deserves credit because it was her idea}; (5) purpose/result: *that, so that, in order that, such that* {we dug up the yard so that a new water garden could be laid out} {he sang so loudly that he became hoarse}; (6) place: *where* {I found a great restaurant where I didn't expect one to be}; (7) manner: *as if, as though* {he swaggers around the office as if he were an executive}; (8) apposition: *and, or, what, that* {the buffalo, or American bison, was once nearly extinct}; (9) indirect questions: *whether, why, when* {he could not say whether we were going the right way}.
- **[5.208] Adverbial conjunctions** — connect two clauses and qualify a verb {the valet has forgotten where Alvaro's car is parked} (*where* qualifies *is parked*); relative type has an antecedent {do you recall that café where we first met?}; interrogative type states a question indirectly {Barbara asked when we are supposed to leave}. Common relative conjunctive adverbs: *after, as, before, now, since, so, until, when, where*; interrogative: *why, how, when, where, what* {I don't see how you reached that conclusion}.
- **[5.209] Beginning a sentence with a conjunction** — legitimate.
  > "There is a widespread belief—one with no historical or grammatical foundation—that it is an error to begin a sentence with a conjunction such as and, but, or so. In fact, a substantial percentage (often as many as 10 percent) of the sentences in first-rate writing begin with conjunctions. It has been so for centuries, and even the most conservative grammarians have followed this practice."
  - Lloyd (1938), quoted at length: no textbook supports the "groundless notion," and about half of English teachers inculcate it anyway.
  - The one genuine caveat: adversative *but* can be unclear. Test: if *and* would work, *but* is wrong.
  - ex: *He went to school this morning. But he left his lunch box on the kitchen table.* (no contradiction → *and* fits, so *but* is wrong; make the contrast explicit: *He went to school, intending to have lunch there, but he left his lunch behind.*)
  - *But* opening a paragraph that develops a contrasting idea is often effective. JUDGMENT; **never delete sentence-initial *And*/*But* as a rule.**
- **[5.210] Beginning a sentence with *however*** — grammatical but ponderous: "however is more ponderous and has less impact than the simple but." Prefer *but* for contrast, and place *however* inside the sentence to emphasize what precedes it {The job seemed exciting at first. Soon, however, it turned out to be exceedingly dull.} Fine sentence-initially in the sense "in whatever way," with no comma {however that may be, we've now made our decision}. JUDGMENT.
- **[5.211] Conjunctions and verb number** — *and*/*through* group elements → plural {the best vacation and the worst vacation of my life were on cruises} {the first through seventh innings were scoreless}; *or*, *either–or* separate them → singular if the elements are singular {a squirrel or a chipmunk raids the bird feeder every day} {either William or Henry dances with Lady Hill}; other conjunctions don't affect number (copulative *and* can leave a singular: *Andrés's bicycle was new, and so was his helmet*). MECHANICAL (see also 7.8).
- **[5.212] Omitting *that*** — "When that is a conjunction, omitting it may result in a miscue or ambiguity, however slight."
  - ex: *the plaintiff charged the expenses were recorded as $5,000 instead of $500* (the expenses weren't charged) → insert *that* before *the expenses*
  - Safe to omit after *say* and *think* {we said the budget needs to be accurate} {Hank thought the tire was flat}; **retain it after other verbs and their near-synonyms** {we declared that the budget needs to be accurate} {Hank believed that the brochure was outdated} to mark the clause boundary; when the subordinate clause opens with a pronoun, omission is usually safe {Jane assured us she could do it} {Hank believed his tire was flat}.
  - MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT; a real readability lever in analytical prose.

## Interjections (5.213–5.216)

- **[5.213] Interjections defined** — exclamatory words/phrases with no literal meaning and no grammatical connection {never again!} {you don't say!}; used "absolutely" {really, I can't understand why you put up with the situation} {oh no, how am I going to fix the damage?} {hey, it's my turn next!}; may stand alone, often verbless {Oh! I've lost my wallet!} {Ouch! I think my ankle is sprained!} {Get out!} {Whoa!}. *Well* and *why* act as interjections when extraneous {well, I tried my best} {why, I would never do that}. Punctuation marks the difference: *Well, I didn't know him* vs. *I didn't know him well*; *Why, here you are!* vs. *I have no idea why you are here* vs. *Why? I have no idea.* MECHANICAL (comma after initial interjection).
- **[5.214] Use** — natural in speech {your order should be shipped, oh, in eight to ten days} and dialogue; a mid-sentence interrupter signals informality or attitude {because our business proposal was, ahem, poorly presented, our budget will not be increased this year}. JUDGMENT (register).
- **[5.215] Functional variation** — any part of speech can be used as an interjection, and is then named for its class: *good!* (adjective), *idiot!* (noun), *help!* (verb), *indeed!* (adverb), *me!* (pronoun), *and!* (conjunction), *quickly!* (adverb).
- **[5.216] Exclusive interjections** — *ouch, whew, ugh, psst, oops*.

## Syntax (5.217–5.231)

- **[5.217] Syntax defined** — the rules for arranging words into sentences; in an analytic language like English, word order carries the meaning.
- **[5.218] Statements** — declarative: a clause with a subject preceding the verb; in speech/informal writing the subject may be implied {[he] missed the ball} {[I] think I'll go to the store}; a few negative idioms invert {scarcely had we arrived when we had to return}.
- **[5.219] Questions** — interrogative, usually opening with a question word; three types: yes-no {will we be gone long?}; wh- {which apples do you want?}; alternative {would you rather play golf or tennis?}.
- **[5.220] Four exceptional question types** — declarative with rising pitch {he's going to Corpus Christi?} (vexation may pair ?! {she's going to Padre Island?!}); tag {he has arrived, hasn't he?} {it's good, isn't it?} {it's raining, right?} {you want to go, yes?}; exclamatory {isn't it nice out here!} {how great is this!}; rhetorical {why should I care?} {who knows how long it might take?}, which becomes more insistent with the question mark dropped {you've lost the car keys; that's a problem, isn't it}. (See also 6.72.)
- **[5.221] Directives** — imperatives; eight subtypes: command {come here now!}, prohibition {don't do that!}, invitation {join us for dinner!}, warning {watch out for rattlesnakes!}, plea {stay here} {help!}, request {put your book away}, well-wishing {play well} {have a good time!}, advice {put on some insect repellent}.
- **[5.222] Exceptional directives** — expressed subject {sit you down} {you stay there}; *let* {let's have a picnic} {let us wait}; *do* {do help yourself}.
- **[5.223] Exclamations** — express the extent to which a speaker is moved; simple interjection {oh my!} {pishposh!}, or *what/how* + subject + verb {what an extraordinary novel this is!} {how well she writes!}, or elliptical {what finery!} {how pretty!} {how ugly!}; in formal literary English, inverted order {little did I expect such unfair treatment}. In casual writing an exclamation point may signal enthusiasm {thank you!} {that's a great idea!} but can read as sarcastic: "As a rule, exclamation points should be used with restraint." (See also 6.75.) MECHANICAL — budget the marks.
- **[5.224] Simple sentence** — one independent clause, no dependent clause {no man is an island}; internal compounds don't change the type {time and tide wait for no man}.
- **[5.225] Compound sentence** — two independent (coordinate) clauses, no dependent {the rain was heavy, and my umbrella was not much help}. Contested case: *She arrived early and stayed late* — traditional grammarians call it simple with a compound predicate; transformationalists call it compound with an elided subject.
- **[5.226] Complex sentence** — one independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses {I'll be home after I finish work} {she won because she practiced so hard}; dependents can stack {the books that were nominated argued that most behavioral differences among people aren't genetic in origin}.
- **[5.227] Compound-complex** — multiple independent clauses plus at least one dependent {it was a beautiful evening, so after we left work we went for a walk}.
- **[5.228] Word order** — English is analytic: since Old English lost most inflection (except pronoun forms), position governs meaning. In *Chris sees Pat* the SVO order alone tells us who notices whom; reversing it reverses the meaning. Departures from SVO signal unusual emphasis or a question.
- **[5.229] The SVO pattern** — the pattern repeats constantly yet sustains variety through what fills each slot: *Mary likes pomegranates* / *The umpire we were talking about rejected our arguments* / *The woman down the street is selling loaves of bread* / *The obstacles that we face create opportunities* / *How you think of yourself affects both the way you approach the problems of everyday life and the degree to which you're perceived as being well adjusted*.
- **[5.230] All seven clause patterns** — S+V {Sandy smiled}; S+V+O {Sandy hit the ball}; S+V+C {Sandy is eager}; S+V+A {Sandy plays well}; S+V+IO+O {Sandy gave Jerry the ball}; S+V+O+C {Sandy got her bag wet}; S+V+O+A {Sandy wrote her score on the card}.
- **[5.231] Variations on syntactic order** — inversions mark either a question {is Sandy all right? [V-S-C]} or special emphasis {Yoda my name is!}. JUDGMENT (use sparingly; don't let a writing agent invert word order for "style").

## Syntax — Variations on syntactic order (5.231, tail)

- **[5.231] Variations on syntactic order** — When clause elements appear out of the normal subject–verb–object order, the inversion signals either a question or a special emphasis; such inversions "achieve a special emphasis precisely because they depart from the normal sequence of sentence elements." **JUDGMENT**
  - ex: "'Finished,' **John** mumbled" (O-S-V); "'Help!' cried **the child**" (O-V-S); "Yoda my name is!" (C-S-V).
  - Agent note: fronting an object, complement, or adverbial is a legitimate emphasis device, but it reads as oral or literary; do not use it in neutral explanatory prose without a reason.

## Syntax — Clauses (5.232–5.235)

- **[5.232] Clauses** — Distinguish an *independent* clause (can stand alone: "José saw a squirrel") from a *dependent* clause (cannot, because a word links it to an independent clause: "because he was hungry, he sat down for a meal"). A dependent clause is introduced by a relative pronoun (→ relative clause) or a subordinating conjunction, which "establishes the semantic relationship between the independent clause and the dependent one." Linking dependent clauses to an independent clause is *subordination*; the result is a complex sentence. **JUDGMENT**
  - A dependent clause does real work: direct object ("everyone believed **that the note was genuine**"), adjectival modifier ("**he who hesitates** is lost"), adverbial modifier ("everyone listens **when she speaks**").
  - Practical test for an agent: if a clause opens with *because, although, when, if, which, that, who*, it must be attached to a main clause, never left standing as a sentence.
- **[5.233] Relative clauses** — A relative clause modifies the noun element (or sentence or clause) it follows: "the car that you own," "they were ten minutes late to the opera, **which** meant they couldn't enter." In a *contact clause* the relative pronoun is merely implied ("all the people [whom] you mention") — a whiz-deletion. **JUDGMENT**
  - Rule: omit the relative pronoun only if it is **not** preceded by a preposition and is **not** the subject of the clause.
    - ok: "the professor I most admire is Dr. Cawthon" (whom/that understood)
    - counter-example (constructed): "the professor I gave the book" is ungrammatical — a pronoun after a preposition cannot be dropped ("the professor **to whom** I gave the book").
  - Caution (→ 5.212): omitting *that* when it functions as a **conjunction** right after a verb "may result in a miscue or ambiguity" — keep *that* wherever the clause boundary would otherwise be unclear to a reader. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.234] Appositive clauses** — An appositive clause is self-contained: strip its introductory *that/which/who* and what remains is a grammatical sentence. Use this as the test that separates it from a relative clause. **JUDGMENT**
  - ex: "we all heard the report **that the beloved broadcaster had died**" → without *that*: "the beloved broadcaster had died" (complete sentence = appositive clause).
  - Contrast: "the report **that** she filed was late" → "she filed was late" is not a sentence (relative clause).
- **[5.235] Conditional clauses** — *If/unless* clauses (protasis) set the condition for the main clause (apodosis). Keep open (real) conditions distinct from hypothetical (closed/unreal) ones, and match tense and mood accordingly. **JUDGMENT**
  - open: "if you don't finish the work on time, we'll have to reevaluate our arrangement"
  - hypothetical: "if he had only remembered to wear a raincoat, he wouldn't have ruined his new suit"; "if I had a hammer, I could fix this creaky stair"
  - other shapes are legitimate: indirect ("if I recall correctly, his assistant's name is Miljana"), alternative ("whether or not it rains"), open range ("whatever you're doing, it's working").

## Syntax — Ellipsis (5.236)

- **[5.236] Ellipsis generally** — Omit words only when the reader can *recover* them from idiom, context, or the "principle of recoverability": "he preferred chocolate, she vanilla" (preferred understood). Elliptical sentences suit commands, exclamations, and answers to questions whose full form would repeat the question. **JUDGMENT**
  - ex (good): "Thank you." / "One lump or two?" / "Glad you like it!" / "The Tucker family." (in answer to "Can you tell me who built this house?")
  - Do not confuse grammatical ellipsis with the three-dot ellipsis of quotation (12.59–69) — different device, different rules.

## Syntax — Negation (5.237–5.245)

- **[5.237] Negation generally** — Four mechanisms: (1) *not* / *no*; (2) negating pronouns and adverbs (*nobody, none, no one, nothing, nowhere, never, neither*); (3) *neither … nor*; (4) words negative in meaning or function — *hardly, scarcely, barely* (= almost not), *few, little* (= not many/much), *rarely, seldom* (= almost never) — plus negative affixes (*a-, dis-, in-/il-/im-/ir-, non-, un-, -less, -free*). **JUDGMENT**
  - Agent caution: a sentence can be negative without any visible *not* ("I hardly think so"); do not add a second negative to it.
- **[5.238] The word "not"** — *Not* negates whichever element it precedes; placement changes meaning, so place it immediately before the thing negated. For an ordinary verb in present/past indicative, use *do/did* + *not* + bare infinitive. **JUDGMENT** (placement is a read-check)
  - do-support: "They sell newspapers in the hotel" → "They **do not** sell newspapers in the hotel."
  - *not* follows the principal verb or the **first** auxiliary: "I **should not** leave for work"; "I am **not** happy."
  - with participles, *not* precedes: "**not** given any warning, Josué nonchalantly opened the door."
  - scope contrast (the key example): "He definitely did not accept the job offer" (= he rejected it) vs. "He did not definitely accept the job offer" (= he may still reject it); "We have not eaten yet" (= but we expect to).
  - contrastive negation may be limited to one element: "I discovered **not** a scientific breakthrough **but** a monstrous development."
  - contractions: *aren't, wouldn't, hasn't* are fine; exceptions are *am not* (use "I'm not"), *cannot/can't*, *will not/won't*, *shall not/shan't* — and "shan't isn't used in American English except in jest."
- **[5.239] The word "no"** — *No* negates only adjectives and nouns, and can be ambiguous when it modifies an adjective phrase. Rewrite rather than leave the ambiguity. **JUDGMENT**
  - ex: "we found no eggs" is clear; "we found **no fresh eggs for sale**" could mean no eggs at all, only nonfresh eggs, or fresh eggs not for sale.
- **[5.240] Using pronouns and adverbs for negation** — Prefer a negative pronoun or adverb to *not* + *any*-word: it saves words and improves flow. **JUDGMENT**
  - "We did not see anyone in the audience" → "We saw **no one** in the audience."
  - "The children do not have anything to do" → "The children have **nothing** to do."
  - "You do not ever listen!" → "You **never** listen!"
  - "I cannot put the groceries anywhere" → "There's **nowhere** to put the groceries."
- **[5.241] Using "neither" and "nor"** — *Neither … nor* negates alternatives simultaneously and frames parallel elements. Use a simple *neither–nor* for exactly two items; with three or more, "a simple neither-nor construction isn't recommended" — write *neither … nor … nor*. **JUDGMENT**
  - "The dog and the cat are not friendly" → "Neither pet is friendly" / "Neither the dog nor the cat is friendly."
  - "The radiator does not leak, and the water pump also does not leak" → "Neither the radiator nor the water pump leaks."
  - three items: "Neither John **nor** Sally **nor** Brenda can attend the meeting."
- **[5.242] Negative interrogative and imperative statements** — In a negative question, contract the first auxiliary with *not* ("Aren't you doing your homework tonight?") or let *not/no* precede the negated element ("are you not doing your homework tonight?"; "is there no satisfying you?"). In a negative imperative the negating word follows the imperative verb, contracts with it, or elliptically replaces it. **MECHANICAL** (contraction spacing) + **JUDGMENT**
  - ex: "come no closer!" / "don't talk back!" / "no closer!"
- **[5.243] Double negatives** — In Standard English two negatives "are usually thought to cancel each other out to make a mild positive" ("he didn't not say anything" = he did say something; "this isn't an uncommon problem"). Dialect uses multiple negation for emphasis; formal prose should not. Multiple negatives breed ambiguity, so rewrite. **JUDGMENT**
  - ambiguous: "I wouldn't be surprised if Dan doesn't find the hammer" — does the speaker expect Dan to find it or not?
- **[5.244] Other forms of negation** — Negation can be carried by *but* (= if not) and *except* (= but not). **JUDGMENT**
  - ex: "what is a pampered dog **but** a child in a fur suit?"; "you may borrow the car **except** when it is raining."
- **[5.245] "Any" and "some" in negative statements** — With the negating particle *not*, use *any*-words (*any, anyone, anybody, anything, anywhere*), not *some*-words — unless you mean one particular unspecified person or thing. **MECHANICAL** (high-confidence pattern)
  - wrong → right: "I don't want to see **somebody**" → "I don't want to see **anybody**" (keep *somebody* only for "one particular person I'm not naming").
  - wrong → right: "There aren't **some** seats left" → "There aren't **any** seats left."

## Syntax — Expletives (5.246–5.248)

- **[5.246] Expletives generally** — In grammar an *expletive* is a word with no lexical meaning that fills a structural slot: chiefly *it* and *there*. An expletive in subject position shifts emphasis onto the predicate, where the real subject sits, and implicitly answers a "who/what" question. **JUDGMENT**
  - ex: "**It** is a rule that children must raise their hands" = "The rule is that children must raise their hands." "**It** is better to stay here than to go there" = "To stay here is better than to go there."
  - "It is foolish to ignore facts" tells the reader *what* is foolish to ignore and emphasizes *facts*.
  - An expletive *it* may also occupy the direct-object slot: "some people don't like **it** that stores are open on Thanksgiving."
  - Agent note: expletives are legitimate emphasis tools but weaken sentences when they merely postpone a short subject ("It is important to note that…"). Prefer the plain subject when it is short.
- **[5.247] Expletive "it"** — Distinguish the pronoun *it* (has an antecedent, or is the formal subject of a be-verb) from the expletive *it* (adds no meaning; takes the subject's or object's place). **JUDGMENT**
  - the failure mode to fix: expletive and pronoun *it* colliding in one passage — "The much-anticipated feast was a disappointment; **it** was poorly cooked and presented. **It** is hard to believe that such a famous chef thought **it** would be edible…" CMOS: "Avoid having several its in a passage clash in this way."
  - other names for it, useful when explaining the fix: ambient, anticipatory, empty, introductory, nonreferential, prop *it*.
- **[5.248] Expletive "there"** — *There* is expletive with *be* or an intransitive (especially linking) verb followed by the subject: "**there** are many different viewpoints presented in the students' essays." Do not confuse it with adverbial *there* of place. **JUDGMENT**
  - contrast: "**There** seemed to be someone" vs. "Someone seemed to be **there**."

## Syntax — Parallel Structure (5.249–5.252)

- **[5.249] Parallel structure generally** — Parallelism is the single most testable sentence-level rule in this chapter.
  > "Every element of a parallel series must be a functional match (word, phrase, clause, sentence) and serve the same grammatical function in the sentence (e.g., noun, verb, adjective, adverb). This syntactic linking of matching elements is called coordination. When linked items do not match, the syntax of the uncoordinated sentence breaks down." (5.249)
  - Parallel series may link words ("win, lose, or draw"), phrases ("government of the people, by the people, for the people"), dependent clauses, or sentences ("I came; I saw; I conquered"). **JUDGMENT**
  - wrong → right: "She did volunteer work in the community kitchen, the homeless shelter, and **taught free ESL classes** offered by her church" → "…the community kitchen, the homeless shelter, and **her church, where she taught free ESL classes**."
  - wrong → right: "The candidate is a former county judge, state senator, and **served two terms as attorney general**" → "…and **two-term attorney general**." (The nonparallel version yields the nonsense "The candidate is a former served two terms…")
  - Diagnostic an agent can apply: read the shared element with each item in turn. If one combination is ungrammatical or absurd, the series is not parallel.
- **[5.250] Prepositions and parallel structure** — In a series of prepositional phrases, repeat the preposition with every element **unless they all take the same preposition**; the error is to let two phrases share one preposition while a third brings its own. **JUDGMENT**
  - wrong → right: "I looked for my lost keys **in** the sock drawer, the laundry hamper, the bathroom, and **under** the bed" → "…**in** the sock drawer, **in** the laundry hamper, **in** the bathroom, and **under** the bed."
  - ok without repetition: "I looked for my lost keys in the sock drawer, the laundry hamper, and the bathroom."
- **[5.251] Paired joining terms and parallel structure** — Correlatives (*either–or, neither–nor, both–and, not only–but also*) and adverb pairs (*where–there, as–so, if–then*) "must join grammatically parallel sentence elements." **JUDGMENT**
  - wrong → right: "I'd like **to either go** into business for myself **or else to write** freelance travel articles" → "I'd like **either to go** into business for myself **or else to write** freelance travel articles."
  - wrong → right: "Our guests **not only ate** all the turkey and dressing **but** both pumpkin pies as well" → "Our guests **ate not only** all the turkey and dressing **but** both pumpkin pies as well." (Placed after the first correlative, *ate* attaches to the turkey but not to the pies.)
  - Rule of thumb: put the correlative immediately before the element it governs, and make both governed elements the same part of speech.
- **[5.252] Auxiliary verbs and parallel structure** — An auxiliary placed before a series of verb phrases must apply to **all** of them; a phrase needing a different auxiliary breaks the series. **JUDGMENT**
  - wrong → right: "The proposal **would** streamline the application process, speed up admission decisions, and **has proved** to save money…" → "…and—**as other schools have learned**—save money," or split it: "The proposal would streamline the application process and speed up admission decisions. **It has proved** to save money…" (The split also avoids a future-to-past tense shift mid-sentence.)

## Word Usage — Grammar versus usage (5.253)

- **[5.253] Grammar versus usage** — Most of what writers and editors argue about is not grammar but usage.
  > "The great mass of linguistic issues that writers and editors wrestle with don't really concern grammar at all—they concern usage: the collective habits of a language's native speakers." (5.253)
  - Two similar-sounding words drifting into each other is *catachresis* (CMOS's example: *corollary* vs. *correlation*). Some confusions are new; others (*lay/lie*, *infer/imply*) are centuries old. **JUDGMENT**

## Word Usage — Good Usage Versus Common Usage (5.254)

### Glossary of problematic words and phrases (5.254) — what the glossary is for

- **[5.254] Use a style guide, not a dictionary, as your authority** —
  > "The mere presence of a word in the dictionary's pages does not mean that the word is in all respects fit for print as Standard Written English. The dictionary merely describes how speakers of English have used the language…" (5.254)
  - So *infer* is recorded as meaning *imply*, *irregardless* as *regardless*, *restauranteur* as *restaurateur* — and none of that makes them fit for edited prose. For usage questions "it is generally necessary to consult a style or usage guide, such as Fowler's *Modern English Usage* or Garner's *Modern English Usage*."
  - Agent consequence: do not defend a misuse by pointing at a dictionary entry; CMOS's judgments below are the operative standard, and they are conservative but evidence-based (it frequently concedes a disputed form has won).

### De facto concision rules scattered through the glossary (5.254)

CMOS 18 has no numbered *jargon*, *legalese*, *clichés*, or *euphemism* rule anywhere in chapter 5 (nothing of the sort appears in the chapter's table of contents); the anti-inflation doctrine is delivered entirely through individual glossary entries, with the inclusive-language section handling euphemism (5.262). Collected, those entries amount to a wordiness checklist:

- Cut empty filler nouns: **area** ("often a nearly meaningless filler word… Try deleting *the area of*"), **basis** ("Much overworked… substitute adverbs: *personally*, not *on a personal basis*; *daily*, not *on a daily basis*"), **case** ("often a sign of verbal inflation": *in case* = if, *in most cases* = usually, *in every case* = always), **eventuality** (displaces *event, result, possibility*), **space** ("voguish equivalent of *area*… avoid it"), **parameters** ("usually just pretentious"; use *boundaries, limits, guidelines, grounds*), **individual** (use *person, adult, child*).
- Replace inflated verbs and coinages: **utilize** → *use* ("most often an overblown alternative"), **impact** (vb.) → *affect, influence*; **impactful** → *influential, powerful* ("jargon"), **finalize** → *finish*, **enthused** → *enthusiastic*, **precondition** → *condition, prerequisite*, **orientate** → *orient*, **cohabitate** → *cohabit*, **preventative** → *preventive*.
- Replace turgid prepositional phrases: **at the present time / at this time / at present** → *now, today, currently*; **due to the fact that** → *because*; **in order to / in order for** → *to, for*; **in excess of** → *more than, over*; **prior to / previous to / preliminary to** → *before*; **in the process of** → delete; **as to** → *about*; **as per** → *as you requested* ("long been considered nonstandard"; *as per usual* is "an illiteracy"); **by means of** → *by, with*; **by reason of** → *because (of)*; **in connection with** → *of, about, for*; **inasmuch as** → *because, since*; **at the time that/when** → *when*; **in the near future** → *soon*; **as far as** (without *is concerned*) → *as for*; **of** — "try removing every of-phrase that you reasonably can" (also: *June 2022*, not *June of 2022*; no *of* after *off*, *outside*, *inside*).
- Delete redundancies: **in actual fact / actual fact, in** (use *actually*, *in fact*, or nothing); **close proximity** (write *close* or *in proximity*); **as yet / as of yet** (use *yet, still, so far*); **after having** + past participle (use *after* + present participle); **equally as** (delete *as*); **and etc.** (*et* already means "and"); *etc.* after *such as* / *e.g.* / *for example* (those already signal a partial list); **free rein** (not "free reign"); **bated breath** (not "baited"); **home in** (not "hone in"); **just deserts** (not "deserts" of dessert); **hark back** (not "hearken back"); **harebrained** (not "hairbrained").
- Prefer the plain conjunction: **and** and **but** may begin sentences, and "typically outperform" *moreover, additionally, in addition, further, furthermore* — but "do not put a comma after" *but*. Avoid **and/or** ("this Janus-faced term"). **such** used for *this/that* "is symptomatic of legalese."
- Kill hype and vagueness: **literally** (= actually; never figurative), **unique** (not *very unique*, *more unique*), **decimate** (not "completely decimated"), **fulsome** (= excessive, not "full"), **plethora** (= excess, not "abundance"), **nonplussed** (= bewildered, not "unfazed"), **peruse** (= read carefully, not "scan"), **noisome** (= foul-smelling, not "noisy"), **center around** (use *center on* / *revolve around*), **presently** (ambiguous: write *now* or *soon*), **hopefully** as a sentence adverb (established, but "many careful writers still avoid" it).
- Prefer plain syntax over inflated phrasing: **there is/are** expletives (5.246–5.248) and *of*-chains are the two structural tics this range flags, and the glossary's repeated fix is to drop the phrase and keep the verb (*in the process of* → delete; *in order to* → *to*).
- Legalistic tics to strip: **shall** in ordinary prose and in contracts ("perhaps the most widely litigated word in the law"; use *must, will, may, is*), **such** as a demonstrative ("symptomatic of legalese"), **prejudice** as a verb (*harm, hurt*), **therefor**, **in the affirmative / in the negative** ("slightly pompous ways of saying *yes* and *no*"). **JUDGMENT**

### Contested usages where CMOS 18 states a verdict

- **Split infinitives** are ruled acceptable at 5.112 (outside this range); in this range CMOS explicitly licenses *and*/*but* as sentence-openers and *more importantly* as a sentence adverb (5.254).
- Verdicts, quick list: **data** plural in formal prose and always in science (5.254); **media** plural in mass communications; **none** singular or plural by the following noun; **neither/either** singular as subject; **different from** (5.254); **compare with** for difference-weighing, **compare to** for likening; **like** as a conjunction "will still provoke frowns" — use *as* / *as if*; **who/whom** — resist the hypercorrect *whom*; **whom** after a preposition is still right (*to whom*); **that/which** — observe the restrictive/nonrestrictive split (British usage does not); **hopefully** — acceptable but avoid in careful prose; **singular *they*** — accepted, including in formal prose (5.51, 5.52, 5.265–5.266); **irregardless, alright, anyways, anyplace, alot, preventative, supercede, miniscule, sacrilegious-as-"sacreligious"** — errors; **shall** — avoid, especially in legal drafting; **is comprised of** — nonstandard → *is composed of* / *consists of*.

| contagious; infectious | contagious = spreads by direct contact; infectious = spreads by germs, objects, water (tetanus is infectious but not contagious). |
| contemporary; contemporaneous | contemporary = of the same time (but also "present-day," so ambiguous across eras); contemporaneous = of things or actions at the same time. |
| contemptuous; contemptible | contemptuous = showing contempt; contemptible = deserving it. |
| content; contents | content = ideas or substance of a work; contents = what is inside a container, or many items. |
| continual; continuous | continual = repeated with interruptions; continuous = unbroken, no gaps. |
| corps; core | corps = organized body (press corps); core = center. |
| councilor; counselor | councillor = council member; counselor = adviser. |
| credible; creditable; credulous | credible = believable; creditable = praiseworthy; credulous = gullible. *Strains credibility*, not "strains credulity." |
| criteria; phenomena; strata | Plurals: one criterion, one phenomenon, one stratum. "Criterias," "phenomenas," "stratas" are solecisms. |
| data | Commonly a mass noun with a singular verb; in formal prose and always in the sciences use it as a plural — and be consistent throughout. |
| decimate | = destroy a large part; never write "completely decimated." |
| defamation; libel; slander | defamation in writing or recording = libel; spoken = slander. |
| definite; definitive | definite = clear, exact; definitive = authoritative, not to be revised. |
| denounce; renounce | denounce = condemn publicly or inform against; renounce = give up, disown. |
| depend on | Keep the *on*: "That depends on how we approach the problem." |
| deprecate | = disapprove; *self-deprecating* is now standard for belittling oneself. |
| derisive; derisory | derisive = mocking; derisory = laughably inadequate (*derisory paychecks*). |
| deserts; desserts | *just deserts* = what is deserved; desserts = sweets. |
| despite; in spite of | Prefer *despite*. |
| determine whether; determine if | Prefer *whether*; *if* is casual. |
| differ from; differ with | differ from = be unlike; differ with = disagree. |
| different from | Standard everywhere; *different than* is defensible only in adverbial phrases (*differently than*). |
| disburse; disperse | disburse = pay out money; disperse = scatter. |
| discreet; discrete | discreet = tactful, unobtrusive; discrete = separate, distinct. |
| disinterested; uninterested | disinterested = impartial, no stake; uninterested = bored, unconcerned. |
| disk; disc | disk usual; disc in *compact disc*, *disc brakes*. |
| disorganized; unorganized | disorganized = thrown into confusion, or habitually bad at planning. |
| distinctive; distinguished; distinguishable | distinctive = easy to recognize; distinguished = eminent; distinguishable = able to be told apart. |
| dive | dive-dived-dived traditional; *dove* is now the predominant American past tense. |
| doctrinal; doctrinaire | doctrinal = of doctrine (neutral); doctrinaire = dogmatic. |
| doubt that; doubt whether; doubt if | doubt that = strong skepticism; doubt whether = milder; doubt if = casual. |
| dual; duel | dual = twofold; duel = armed combat. |

| dying; dyeing | dying ↔ die; dyeing ↔ dye. |
| each | Singular verb (*each of them was present*); plural when an emphatic appositive follows a plural subject (*they each have their virtues*). |
| each other; one another | each other for two; one another for more than two. |
| economic; economical | economic = of the economy; economical = thrifty. |
| edible; eatable | edible = fit for human consumption; eatable = at least minimally palatable. |
| effrontery; affront | effrontery = shameless impudence; affront = deliberate insult. |
| e.g.; i.e. | e.g. = for example; i.e. = that is. Comma after either; English equivalents preferred in formal prose. |
| either; neither | Singular verb as subject (*is either of the spouses present?*). |
| elemental; elementary | elemental = essential constituent or force of nature; elementary = basic, introductory. |
| elicit; illicit | elicit = draw out (a response); illicit = unlawful, forbidden. |
| eminent; imminent | eminent = famous, respected; imminent = about to happen, usually bad. |
| emoji; emoticon | emoji = pictorial character; emoticon = keyboard-character face ( ;-) ). |
| emotive; emotional | emotive = arousing intense feeling; emotional = showing or involving feeling. |
| empathy; sympathy | empathy = understanding others' feelings; sympathy = compassion for another's misfortune. |
| endemic; epidemic; pandemic | endemic = always present in a region or population; epidemic = outbreak in a limited area; pandemic = widespread (nation, continent, world). |
| enervate; innervate | enervate = weaken, drain; innervate = supply with energy or nerves. |
| enormity; enormousness | enormity = monstrousness, moral outrage; enormousness = hugeness. |
| enthused | Use *enthusiastic*. |
| enumerable; innumerable | enumerable = countable; innumerable = too many to count. |
| equally as | Faulty; delete *as*. |
| et al.; etc. | et al. = and other *people*; etc. = and other *things*. No "and etc."; no *etc.* after *such as* or *e.g.* |

| every one; everyone | *every one of them* (each) vs. *everyone* (everybody). |
| evoke; invoke | evoke = call up (memories, laughter); invoke = cite as authority, appeal to, conjure. |
| exceptional; exceptionable | exceptional = extraordinary; exceptionable = objectionable. |
| executor; administrator | executor named in a will; administrator appointed by the court. Avoid *executrix*, *administratrix*. |
| explicit; implicit | explicit = clearly spelled out; implicit = suggested, not stated. |
| farther; further | farther = physical distance; further = figurative distance. |
| faze; phase | faze = disconcert (*unfazed*); phase = carry out in stages. |
| feel bad | Never "feel badly." |
| fewer; less | fewer with plural count nouns; less with mass nouns and amounts. |
| fictional; fictitious; fictive | fictional = of fiction; fictitious = false, imaginary; fictive = imaginatively creative (or *fictive kin*). |
| finalize | Inflated jargon; prefer *finish*. |
| first; second; last | Not *firstly*, *secondly*, *lastly*. |
| flammable; inflammable | Same meaning; *flammable* is standard; antonym is *nonflammable*. |
| flare; flair | flare = sudden light or outburst (flare-up); flair = talent or stylishness. |
| flaunt; flout | flaunt = show off ostentatiously; flout = openly disobey. |
| flounder; founder | flounder = struggle awkwardly; founder = sink or run aground. |

| forbear; forebear | forbear = refrain (forbore, forborne); forebear = ancestor. |
| forego; forgo | forego = go before (*foregone conclusion*); forgo = do without, renounce. |
| foreword; preface | foreword by someone other than the author; preface by the author. |
| former; latter | Only for pairs. |
| fortuitous; fortunate | fortuitous = happening by chance; fortunate = lucky. |
| free rein | So written — not "free reign." |
| fulsome | = excessive to the point of disgust, not "very full"; *fulsome praise* often says the opposite of what is meant. |
| gauntlet | *run the gauntlet*, *throw down the gauntlet*; the gantlet distinction is abandoned. |
| gibe; jibe; jive | gibe = taunt; jibe = agree, accord (*didn't jibe with*); jive = swing music or deceptive talk. |
| gourmet; gourmand | gourmet = discerning appreciator; gourmand = gluttonous. |
| graduate | graduate *from* a school; a school graduates a student. |
| grateful; gratified | grateful = thankful; gratified = pleased, indulged. |
| grisly; grizzly | grisly = gruesome; grizzly = grayish (the bear). |
| hail; hale | hail = greet, acclaim, ice pellets; hale = haul (*haled into court*) or healthy. |
| hanged; hung | hanged = execution by the neck; hung = everything else (*hung up his clothes*). |
| harass; embarrass | One *r* in harass, two in embarrass. |
| harebrained; hark back; home in | harebrained (not hair-); hark back (not hearken); home in (not hone in). |
| healthy; healthful | healthy = having health; healthful = promoting health. |
| historic; historical | historic = momentous; historical = pertaining to history. |
| hoard; horde | hoard = hidden supply (vb: to amass); horde = crowd. |
| hoi polloi | = the common people (plural); it does not mean elites. |
| holocaust | Capitalized = the Nazi genocide; avoid light or hyperbolic use. |
| hopefully | Sentence-adverb sense ("it is to be hoped") is established but still avoided by many careful writers. |
| humanitarian | Do not use for plain *human* (*a humanitarian disaster* → *a disaster*). |
| I; me | Use the first person when needed, correctly: *Sally and I are planning to go*; *give John or me a call*. |
| idyllic | = charming, picturesque; not a synonym of *ideal*. |
| if; whether | whether for alternatives and indirect questions (*call to let me know whether you can come*); *if* is conditional. |
| illegible; unreadable | illegible = cannot be read (handwriting); unreadable = badly composed or intolerably dull. |
| immigrate; emigrate | immigrate = into a country; emigrate = out of one. |
| impact | Resist as a verb (use *affect*, *influence*); avoid *impactful* (jargon for *influential*, *powerful*). |
| imply; infer | The speaker or writer **implies**; the reader or listener **infers**. |
| important; importantly | *More importantly* is now established as a sentence adverb and is acceptable. |
| impractical; impracticable | impractical = unrealistic; impracticable = impossible to carry out. |
| include; comprise | include = non-exhaustive; comprise = exhaustive (except in patent law). |
| incredible; incredulous | incredible = unbelievable; incredulous = disbelieving. |
| inculcate; indoctrinate | inculcate *values into* someone; indoctrinate *someone with* values. |
| individual | Prefer person, adult, child, man, woman. |
| inflict; afflict | inflict a thing *on* someone; someone is afflicted *with* or *by* it. |
| ingenious; ingenuous | ingenious = clever, original; ingenuous = candid, naive. |
| innate; inherent | innate = present from birth (living things); inherent = part of a thing's nature (a rock's hardness). |
| innocent; not guilty | innocent = without blame; not guilty = exonerated by a jury. |
| insidious; invidious | insidious = creeping, unnoticed harm; invidious = offensive, discriminatory. |
| intense; intensive | intense generally; intensive retained in *labor-intensive*, *intensive care*. |
| inter; intern | inter = bury (interment); intern = trainee, or to confine (internment). |
| inveigh; inveigle | inveigh against = protest; inveigle = cajole, ensnare. |
| irregardless | An error; use *regardless*. |
| it is I; it is me | Both acceptable; in the third person prefer *this is he*, *it isn't she*. |
| its; it's | its = possessive; it's = it is. |
| jealousy; envy | jealousy = resentment of a rival (often intimate); envy = coveting another's advantages. |
| kudos | Singular ("praise and admiration"); there is no *kudo*. |
| laudable; laudatory | laudable = praiseworthy; laudatory = expressing praise. |
| lay; lie | lay is transitive (lay-laid-laid, takes an object); lie is intransitive (lie-lay-lain): *lay your pencils down* / *lie down and rest*. |
| leach; leech | leach = percolate, wash out in solution; leech = bloodsucker (literal or figurative). |
| lease; let | Owner or tenant may lease; only the owner lets. |
| led | Past tense and participle of *lead* — not "lead." |
| lend; loan | lend is the verb preferred in edited English; loan is the noun (and a standard verb for money). |
| lest | One of the few words that invariably take the subjunctive (*lest he take a wrong turn*). |
| life-and-death | Standard idiom (*a life-and-death decision*). |
| light; lighted; lit | Both inflections correct; *lighted* before nouns (*a lighted building*), *lit* after an -ly adverb (*brightly lit*). |
| like; as | like is a preposition; for conjunctive senses in Standard Written English use *as*, *as if*. |
| literally | = actually, without exaggeration; do not use figuratively. |
| loathe; loath | loathe = detest (verb); loath = reluctant (adjective). |
| lose; loose; loosen | lose = be deprived of; loose = release from restraint; loosen = make less tight. |
| luxuriant; luxurious | luxuriant = lush, abundant growth; luxurious = lavish, comfortable. |
| mad; angry | mad traditionally = insane; use *angry* in formal prose. |
| majority | Use of countable things, and prefer *most*; plural verb after *a majority of* + plural. |
| mantle; mantel | mantle = cloak (usually metaphorical); mantel = fireplace shelf. |
| masterful; masterly | masterful = domineering; masterly = virtuoso, authoritative. |
| may; might | may = possible or factual; might = uncertain, hypothetical, contrary to fact. |
| media; mediums | media is plural in mass communications and science (*the media are reporting*); mediums = spiritualists. |
| memoranda | Plural; singular *memorandum*; do not use *memoranda* as a singular. |
| mete out | Not "meet out." |
| minuscule | Not "miniscule." |
| mistreatment; maltreatment | maltreatment = harsh, abusive mistreatment. |
| mitigate; militate | mitigate = lessen; militate *against* = weigh against. Never "mitigate against." |
| moot; mute | moot = debatable (or, by extension, practically irrelevant); mute = silent (avoid as a noun; *deaf-mute* is to be avoided). |
| much; very | much with past-participial adjectives and comparatives (*much obliged*, *much worse*); very with adverbs and most adjectives (*very bored*). |
| murder; manslaughter; homicide | murder = intentional and malicious unlawful killing; manslaughter = unlawful but not malicious; homicide = any killing, including lawful. |
| myself | Reflexive or emphatic only; not a substitute for *I* or *me*. |
| nauseous; nauseated | nauseous = causing nausea; nauseated = feeling sick. |
| necessary; necessitous | necessitous = impoverished. |
| neither … nor | Frame parallel elements; keep to two elements (nors may multiply only for emphasis: *neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night*); singular verb as subject. |
| noisome | = noxious, foul-smelling; it shares a root with *annoy*, not *noise*. |
| none | Singular or plural verb according to the following noun. |
| nonplussed | = surprised and confused; not "unfazed." |
| notable; noticeable; noteworthy | notable = readily noticed, remarkable; noticeable = detectable by the senses; noteworthy = deserving attention. |
| notwithstanding | One word; may precede or follow its noun. |
| observance; observation | observance = keeping a rule or custom; observation = watching, or a remark. |
| obtuse; abstruse | obtuse = a person who cannot understand; abstruse = an idea that is hard to understand. |
| odious; odorous; malodorous | odious = hateful; odorous = detectable by smell (either way); malodorous = foul-smelling. |
| of | Delete needless *of* after all, off, inside, outside; write *June 2022*, not *June of 2022*; "try removing every of-phrase that you reasonably can." |
| officious | = aggressively meddlesome; not *official*. |
| on; upon | Prefer *on*; *upon* for events and conditions (*upon the job's completion*). |
| onto; on to | onto = movement to a position (*jumped onto the bars*); *on to* = verb + preposition (*held on to the bars*). |
| oppress; repress | oppress = persecute, tyrannize; repress = restrain, subordinate. |
| ordinance; ordnance | ordinance = municipal regulation; ordnance = military armament. |
| orient; orientate | Prefer *orient*. |
| ought | Stronger than *should* and requires its *to* even in the negative (*ought not to see*). |
| over | Fine as an equivalent of *more than*. |
| overly | Now unobjectionable (*overly tired*). |
| pair | Plural *pairs*; verb may be singular or plural. |
| palette; palate; pallet | palette = artist's board or a work's colors; palate = taste or the roof of the mouth; pallet = wooden platform or crude bed. |
| parameters | Usually pretentious outside technical use; use boundaries, limits, guidelines, grounds. |
| partake in; partake of | partake in = participate; partake of = take a share of, or smack of. |
| partly; partially | partly = in part (preferred); partially = incompletely, or with bias. |
| past; passed | past = adjective, noun, preposition, adverb; passed = past tense and participle of *pass*. |
| pastime | pass + time; single *s*, single *t*. |
| peaceable; peaceful | peaceable = inclined to avoid strife; peaceful = serene, tranquil. |
| peak; peek; pique | peak = apex; peek = quick glance; pique = resentment, or to arouse (*piques one's interest*). |
| pedal; peddle | pedal = foot-operated device; peddle = sell goods, often door to door. |
| pendant; pendent | pendant = dangling jewelry (noun); pendent = hanging (adjective). |
| penultimate | = next to last, not "ultimate"; *antepenultimate* = third from last. |
| people; persons | persons traditionally for small numbers; *people* now usual even for small groups. |
| peroration; oration | peroration = the conclusion of a speech, not a rousing speech. |
| perpetuate; perpetrate | perpetuate = prolong, sustain; perpetrate = commit (a crime). |
| personally | Redundant when the verb already implies presence; *I personally think* weakens the statement. |
| persuade; convince | persuade to act; convince of a belief. |
| pertain; appertain | pertain to = relate to; appertain to = belong as a matter of form or function. |
| peruse | = read with great care; not a fancy word for *read* or *scan*. |
| pitiable; pitiful | pitiable = deserving pity; pitiful = wretched or contemptible. |
| pleaded; pled | pleaded is traditional; *pled* is increasingly common in American legal usage. |
| plethora | = excess, surfeit; not merely "abundance." |
| populace; populous | populace = the people (noun); populous = densely populated. |
| pore; pour | pore over = read intently. |
| practicable; possible; practical | practicable = feasible; possible = maybe; practical = fit for actual use. |
| precede; proceed | precede = come before; proceed = go on (often misspelled "preceed"). |
| precipitate; precipitous | precipitate = rash, sudden; precipitous = dangerously steep. |
| precondition | Prefer *condition* or *prerequisite*. |
| predominant; predominate | predominate is a verb only (*a view that predominates*); the adjective is *predominant*. |
| prejudice, vb. | Legalese for *harm*; use harm or hurt (though *prejudiced* is fine as an adjective). |
| preliminary to; previous to; prior to | Use *before* (or *until*). |
| presently | Ambiguous; write *now* or *soon*. |
| presumption; assumption | presumption rests on evidence or experience; assumption is accepted without proof. |
| preventive | Correct form; avoid "preventative." |
| principle; principal | principle = rule; principal = head of a school, a sum of money, or "most important." |
| process of, in the | Almost always deletable. |
| propaganda | Singular noun. |
| prophesy; prophecy | prophesy = verb; prophecy = noun. |
| proscribe; prescribe | proscribe = prohibit; prescribe = mandate or order (a remedy). |
| protuberance | So spelled — no extra *r*. |
| proved; proven | proved is the preferred past participle; *proven* only as an adjective (*a proven success*). |
| purposely; purposefully | purposely = on purpose; purposefully = with a clear aim. |
| question whether | Prefer over *question of whether* / *question as to whether*. |
| quick; quickly | quickly is the general adverb; *quick* survives in idioms (*get rich quick*, *come quick*). |
| quote; quotation | quote is the verb; quotation the noun (the noun *quote* is informal). |
| rack; wrack | rack in all familiar senses (*racked with guilt*, *nerve-racking*, *rack and ruin*); wrack only for seaweed. |
| raise; raze | raze = demolish, level to the ground. |
| reason why | Acceptable despite the supposed redundancy; but *reason … is because* → *reason … is that*. |
| recur; reoccur | recur = happen again and again; reoccur = happen again once. |
| reference; referral | reference = source or mention; referral = directing someone to a specialist. |
| refrain; restrain | refrain = keep oneself from; restrain = hold back (often others, by force). |
| refute | = prove wrong; not merely deny or rebut. |
| regrettable; regretful | regrettable = unfortunate; regretful = feeling regret; *regrettably* = unfortunately. |
| rein; reign | rein = horse control (*free rein*, *rein in*); reign = rule (*reign supreme*, *reign of terror*). |
| relegate; delegate | relegate = assign a lesser position; delegate = authorize someone to act. |
| reluctant; reticent | reticent = unwilling to *talk*; reluctant = unwilling to *act*. |
| repellent; repulsive | repulsive = so disgusting as to make one feel sick. |
| repetitive; repetitious | repetitious = tediously repeated. |
| restive; restful | restive = impatient, fidgety; restful = calming. |
| rob; steal | You rob a person or place; you steal a thing. |
| role; roll | role = part or function; roll = roster, cylinder, to rotate. |
| sacrilegious | So spelled (from *sacrilege*, not *religion*). |
| seasonal; seasonable | seasonal = of the season; seasonable = timely, fitting the time of year. |
| sensual; sensuous | sensual = indulgence of the physical senses, especially sexual; sensuous = aesthetic enjoyment. |
| sewer; sewage; sewerage | sewer = the pipe; sewage = the waste; sewerage = the system. |
| shall | Little used in ordinary prose; in legal drafting replace with must, will, may, is. |
| shear; sheer | shear = cutting or a force that makes plates slip; sheer = pure, steep, semitransparent. |
| shine | shone (intransitive: the stars shone); shined (transitive: shined the silver). |
| simplistic | Derogatory ("oversimplified"); not a fancy synonym of *simple*. |
| since | Fine for causation, but use *because* if the temporal sense could intrude. |
| sink; sank; sunk | *sank* is the past tense; avoid "the ship sunk." |

| systematic; systemic | systematic = methodical; systemic = affecting the whole system. |
| tantalizing; titillating | tantalizing = tormentingly out of reach; titillating = pleasantly (often sexually) exciting. |

| timbre; timber | timbre = tone quality; timber = wood. |
| tolerance; toleration | tolerance = the habitual quality; toleration = a particular instance. |
| tortious; tortuous; torturous | tortious = of a tort; tortuous = winding; torturous = agonizing. |

### Other glossary entries worth knowing (not in the table above)

- **[5.254] Numerals/large numbers** — *billion* and *trillion* differ historically between American and British usage; the American sense now predominates. See also 9.9.
- **[5.254] Pronunciation-only entries** — *alleged* has two syllables (/ə-LEJD/), *allegedly* four; *harass* stresses the second syllable in American English, the first in British; *kudos* is /K[Y]OO-dos/, not /-dohz/.
- **[5.254] Punctuation-adjacent entries** — *i.e.* and *e.g.* always take a following comma; *et al.* takes a period after *al.* but not after *et*; *who's/whose*, *its/it's*, *your/you're* are contractions vs. possessives (all **MECHANICAL**).
- **[5.254] Plural and agreement entries** — *criteria, phenomena, strata, memoranda, media* are plurals; *data* is plural in formal prose; *none, neither, either, each, pair, couple, handful, half (of), majority, number* each have stated agreement rules (**MECHANICAL** where a program can parse the noun phrase).
- **[5.254] Entries that are about register, not error** — *get* is often more natural than *obtain*; *quote* for *quotation* is informal; *transpire* ("happen") and *access* (vb.) are common but carry a whiff of jargon; *decimate*, *fulsome*, *nonplussed*, *literally*, *peruse* are the classic semantic-drift traps.

## Word Usage — Inclusive Language and Minimizing Bias (5.255–5.266)

- **[5.255] Making conscious choices** — Choose words with awareness that they may carry bias based on race, ethnicity, sex, gender, disability, and other characteristics; bias is often unintentional, "rooted in unknown or unconsidered etymologies of words {master; lame} or in unexamined assumptions about what is 'normal' (male; cisgender)."
  > "Eliminating all bias from a piece of writing is an unrealistic goal, but making conscious choices to avoid bias and be more inclusive where possible offers important benefits to both writer and readers." (5.255)
  - Terminology: CMOS draws on *conscious language* (Karen Yin, *Conscious Style Guide*). **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.256] Maintaining credibility** — Biased language "that is not central to the meaning of a work can distract or offend readers and therefore make the work less credible"; readers may stop engaging with the argument and focus on its political or social subtext. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.257] Evolving standards** — Preferred terms change quickly, and CMOS does **not** police vocabulary:
  > "Although some publishers recommend avoiding certain terms or specific usages in all cases, Chicago's editorial staff does not maintain a list of words or usages considered unacceptable." (5.257)
  - So: reason case by case from the principles, do not apply a banned-word list. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.258] Common areas of biased language** — Consider language choices when referring to people by race, ethnicity, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, disability, age, weight, appearance, nationality, religion, social standing, or similar characteristics; **avoid irrelevant references to these characteristics**. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.259] Labels** — Use the label preferred by the individual or group written about or for; research what is current, since labels are "sometimes rapidly adopted or discarded." More than one may be acceptable {Black; African American}; apparently interchangeable labels may name different people {First Nations; Inuit; Métis} or be preferred in different regions or cohorts {Hispanic; Latino, Latina, Latinx, Latine; Chicano, Chicana}. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.260] Person, not characteristic** — "In general, emphasize the person, not a characteristic." When the characteristic matters, prefer an adjective to a noun for groups: "the **Vietnamese students** in the library," not "the Vietnamese in the library." **JUDGMENT**
  - CMOS's own contrast: "…is one of the most successful **Black female** graduates of Harvard Law School" invites inferences (successful "for a female," notable only among Black graduates, abnormality) that "…is the **first Black woman** to sit on the US Supreme Court" does not.
- **[5.261] Person-first versus identity-first** — Person-first ("an editor who is deaf") emphasizes the person; identity-first ("an autistic illustrator") treats the condition as integral to identity and as a political/social fact. Choose by audience and, when known, the person's own preference. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.262] Ableism** — Avoid disability euphemisms (*differently abled*, *special needs*): they "can have the unfortunate result of both obscuring meaning and suggesting shame through avoidance." Emphasize agency, not limitation: *wheelchair user* (not "confined to a wheelchair"), *speech impediment* (not "speech defect"). Avoid figurative uses of disability — {deaf as a post} {a lame excuse} {willfully blind} → {can't hear a thing} {a bogus excuse} {refuses to see}. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.263] Gender-neutral nouns** — Drop gender-specific suffixes: *author*, *testator* (not *authoress*, *testatrix*); *actor* or *female actor* increasingly replaces *actress*. The *-person* suffix "rarely functions well as a substitute for *-man*" — *chairperson* and *anchorperson* "sound more pompous and wooden than the simpler (and effective) *chair* and *anchor*." Substitutes: *business owner/entrepreneur*, *crew/staff/workers* (for *manpower*), *humans/human beings/people* (for *mankind*), *supervisor/chief/leader* or *presiding juror* (for *foreman*), *ancestors* (for *forefathers*); even *founding fathers*, though technically accurate, "may reflect a limited perspective on whose contributions deserve credit." **MECHANICAL** (suffix scan) + **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.264] Gender-specific labels as adjectives** — When gender is relevant, *female*/*male* work as modifiers ("the patient requested a female doctor"); *nonbinary* covers those who don't identify with a sex-specific modifier. Do not use *woman* as a modifier ("a woman astronaut," "the woman plumber" — the latter sounds dismissive) and avoid *lady* as noun or adjective. **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.265] Options for gender neutrality in pronoun use** — Nine methods, in CMOS's order: (1) omit the pronoun ("the programmer should update the records when data is transferred by the head office"); (2) repeat the noun, sparingly ("the writer's credibility"); (3) use a plural antecedent ("contestants must conduct themselves with dignity"); (4) use an article ("waive **the** right to have a guidance counselor present"); (5) use singular *one*; (6) use the relative pronoun *who* ("an applicant who can't write well won't be a good employee"); (7) use the imperative ("keep a close watch over children while monitoring the pool"); (8) use singular *they*; (9) rewrite so no pronoun is needed ("misbehavior will be punished by the loss of privileges"). **JUDGMENT**
- **[5.266] Uses of singular "they"** — Three legitimate uses: (1) with indefinite pronouns ("no one in the group could remember **their** great-grandfather's middle name"); use *themselves* when the indefinite has or could have a plural sense, *themself* when clearly singular ("each should serve themself"); (2) when the antecedent's gender is unknown, irrelevant, or confidential ("will the driver of the pink sedan please move **their** car"); (3) for a specific person who uses *they* or another nonbinary pronoun — "this usage, when known to apply to a specific individual, should be respected." If an easy unambiguous revision reads as well, prefer it. **JUDGMENT**

## A Summing Up (5.267)

- **[5.267] Stability and renewal** — Language changes slowly; English usage "is remarkably stable, even if it varies somewhat from generation to generation." Match the register to the purpose (dialogue dialect vs. editorial dialect vs. scholarly Standard English), and "people who use dictionaries and handbooks should use current ones, and use them intelligently."
  > "Effective uses of language show a satisfying balance between constraint and freedom, tradition and novelty, obedience and nonconformity." (5.267)
  > "Good usage in a particular context will always require wide knowledge, discerning judgment, and some degree of literary taste." (5.267)
