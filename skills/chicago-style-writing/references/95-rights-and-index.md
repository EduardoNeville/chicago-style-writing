# Permissions and copyright, indexes, and a glossary

Source: CMOS ch 4, ch 15, and the back-matter Glossary. Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch04, ch15, glossary. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch04

1. **Quote only what you need to make your point** — a few contiguous paragraphs of prose or lines of poetry, never a whole essay, article, or poem (4.90).
2. **Judge the quote by proportion, not word count** — 500 words lifted from a short essay is riskier than 500 from a long book, and a small excerpt can still be "the heart of the work" (4.90).
3. **Make the borrowing transformative** — comment, critique, or reframe; do not quote another author to restate that author's own argument or to decorate your prose (4.90).
4. **Never let quotation overshadow your own text** (4.90).
5. **Transcribe quotations exactly and never let a quoted author appear to say the opposite of what was meant** (4.88).
6. **Attribute everything, permitted or not** — a footnote or legend costs nothing and forecloses plagiarism charges (4.79, 4.96).
7. **Write "Source:" for reformatted data, "Reprinted from:" for verbatim reproduction** (4.96).
8. **Permission is required for others' copyrighted material unless the use is fair**; no permission is needed for US government works or expired copyright (4.79).
9. **Do not ask reflexively** — permission-seeking is not an admission, and a denial or absurd fee is the moment to argue fair use, not to cut the quotation (4.97).
10. **Paraphrase is not a workaround** — extensive paraphrase is "merely disguised copying" (4.93).
11. **Public domain covers the old text, not the modern edition's translation, introduction, or notes** (4.79, 4.29).
12. **Dates decide public-domain status** — life + 70 for post-1977 works; 95 years from publication for pre-1978 US works (renewal required for 1923–1963, automatic from 1964); works published before 1924 entered the public domain at the end of 2018, with one more year's cohort freed each January (4.24–4.30, table 4.1).
13. **A revised edition worth reprinting probably has its own copyright** — check the revision, not just the original (4.29).
14. **Books written on staff belong to the employer; a commissioned monograph never does** (4.10–4.11).
15. **Include the three notice elements** — © (or *Copyright*/ *Copr.*), year of first publication, owner's name — even though notice is optional (4.42).
16. **"All rights reserved" adds nothing legally, and it should be dropped from CC-licensed works** (4.42, 4.73).
17. **Never quote song lyrics, epigraphs, or poetry at length without clearance**; short resonant epigraphs are the fair-use case, decorative ones are not (4.91).
18. **Keep quotation of images minimal in size and resolution**, never full-page color or cover use without permission (4.94).
19. **Reusing your own previously published work may need the old publisher's permission** (4.82).
20. **Credit prior publication and follow any credit line the grantor dictates, word for word** (4.82, 4.106).

### From ch15

1. An index is an intellectual product, not a keyword dump: gather substantive terms, sort them, cross-reference, and attach locators (15.1, 15.5).
2. Choose terms from the author's own vocabulary; index *essence* where the text says *essence* (15.30).
3. Do not index passing mentions or scene-setting names; index what carries the argument (15.31).
4. Keep headings to nouns or noun phrases, pluralize count nouns, never let a bare adjective head an entry, and invert only to bring the reader's keyword first ("imperialism, American") (15.9, 15.64).
5. Lowercase headings that are lowercase in text; reserve initial capitals for proper nouns and for indexes so dense that capitals aid navigation (15.11).
6. Break any entry with more than five or six locators into subentries (15.10, 15.127).
7. Phrase subheadings with a leading keyword, concisely, and drop *a*/*an*/*the* (15.128).
8. Never write *ff* or *et seq.* in an index, and prefer individual locators to *passim* (15.12).
9. Never let a *see* reference carry a locator or point to another *see*; double-post two short entries instead (15.16).
10. Cross-reference to the heading's exact wording, capitalization, and inversion, and italicize *see* / *see also* unless the target term is itself italic (15.21–15.22).
11. Follow the text's style and spelling throughout; do not modernize or correct names (15.29).
12. Sort word by word unless a letter-by-letter index is already under way and sound (15.66).
13. Ignore articles, prepositions, and conjunctions when sorting subentries, and avoid opening a subheading with them (15.76).
14. Alphabetize numerals as spelled out, accents as unaccented, and acronyms as written (15.72–15.75).
15. Punctuate mechanically: comma before the first locator, semicolon between run-in subentries, colon before run-in subheadings, no period at the end of an entry (15.102–15.105).
16. Use the en dash for every inclusive range, abbreviated per Chicago's table, and cite notes as `334n14` unspaced (15.14, 15.34, 15.108).
17. Never begin indexing before pagination is final, never let software stand in for reading the whole work, and err toward inclusiveness (15.109, 15.114, 15.117).
18. Proofread the finished index for alphabetical order, cross-reference accuracy, and rising locators — the three failure modes reviewers catch first (15.129, 15.132).

## Overview / Copyright Law and the Licensing of Rights  (4.1–4.6)
- **[4.1] Scope** — Chapter covers acquisition, ownership, protected subject matter, the bundle of rights, duration, transfer, contracts, open access, warranties, permissions/fair use, and rights licensing. Assume a basic knowledge of it is "essential for both authors and editors."
- **[4.2] Relevant law** — Apply the Copyright Act of 1976 (effective Jan. 1, 1978) plus amendments; pre-1978 works are still governed by the old dual regime, so know both. Law discussed is US law; Berne Convention harmonizes but foreign law still differs.
- **[4.3] Copyright comes into being automatically** — Copyright attaches the moment a work is "fixed" in a "tangible" form and embodies a human author's original expression; "fixed" means not purely transitory (stored email, filmed ad-lib, recorded webinar).
- **[4.4] Registration and notice not required** — Neither is a prerequisite to protection. Register anyway (see 4.52) and use notice anyway (see 4.42–48).
- **[4.5] Original expression, not facts** — Copyright protects expression (words, sounds, images), not "the underlying facts, ideas, or theories, no matter how novel." Selection and arrangement count as expression. The threshold is a "modicum of creativity"; *labor* in gathering data is unprotected, modest selection/arrangement is protected unless "glaringly obvious."
  - ex: simple graphs cannot be copyrighted (that would protect the data); recipes cannot be copyrighted (that would protect a way of cooking), but collections of recipes can.
  - ex: copyright in a new annotated edition of an eighteenth-century book covers only the annotations.
  - AI (18th ed.): prompting an AI is not enough; the human must contribute expression that itself supports copyright, and protection is limited to that contribution.
- **[4.6] Author is the original owner** — The author (not synonymous with "creator") controls copyright at the outset and holds the rights automatically.

## Varieties of Authorship  (4.7–4.8)
- **[4.7] Individual and joint authors** — A joint work is "a work prepared by two or more authors with the intention that their contributions be merged into inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole."
- **[4.8] Collective works** — Independent contributions merely combined = collective, not joint. Copyright in the collection (selection and arrangement) belongs to the compiler or editor and is separate from copyright in each component. Ex: newspapers, anthologies, journal issues, edited volumes.

## Works Made for Hire  (4.9–4.12)
- **[4.9] Employer as author** — For a work made for hire the law treats the employer or other controlling party as the "author" and initial copyright owner. Some works are WMFH by definition; some only by agreement.
- **[4.10] Three categories** — (1) prepared within the scope of employment (staff writer's story, staff photographer's photo, staff-written marketing copy); (2) a non-payroll creator acting as an "agent" (test: control over hours, assignments, tools); (3) a specially ordered or commissioned work that both parties **agree in writing** to treat as WMFH — available only for: contributions to collective works, contributions to motion pictures/audiovisual works, translations, instructional texts, tests and test answer material, atlases, compilations of data or existing materials, and "supplementary works" (forewords, afterwords, illustrations, photographs, maps, charts, tables, figures, bibliographies, indexes, appendixes).
  - The writing must expressly say WMFH and should be signed *before* creation (some courts have saved later agreements that reflected the original intention).
- **[4.11] Ineligible works** — Monographs and novels can never be WMFH, "no matter what agreement may be made"; copyright stays with the writer unless expressly assigned. An overreaching WMFH clause is usually read as an assignment — same practical effect but different rules on duration (4.25) and termination (4.39).
- **[4.12] Joint authorship with a hireling** — If any coauthor is writing for hire, the whole joint work takes the WMFH term.

## Rights of the Copyright Owner  (4.13–4.19)
- **[4.13] Reproduction, distribution, display** — The right to make copies and to distribute them (together = to publish); online, the two blend into transmitting on demand. Public display applies to online exploitation (viewable = displayed; download or print also = distribution).
- **[4.14] Derivative works and performance** — A derivative work is one "based on or derived from the original work, such as translations, abridgments, dramatizations, or other adaptations." A revised edition is generally different enough to carry its own copyright. Public-performance rights matter little for literary prose (a poet reading a poem) but a lot for plays, music, film.
- **[4.15] Moral rights; CMI** — Federal moral rights against mutilation and misattribution run only to certain works of fine art, with broader state statutes; the chapter states flatly that moral rights have "no relevance to the publishing industry in the United States," and foreign moral-rights notices have no legal effect here. Separately, federal law protects "copyright management information" (title, author, owner, terms of use, identifying numbers/links) against intentional removal or falsification done to enable or conceal infringement. State common law may still bar attributing to an author something that author did not write.
- **[4.16] Titles and trademarks** — Copying only a title is unlikely to be copyright infringement, but a title can acquire trademark protection. Book titles are harder to protect than journal titles; *The Chicago Manual of Style* qualified because it names a series, and *The Hobbit* had strong trademark status before franchising. Trademark in the book itself dies with copyright; active franchising keeps it alive elsewhere, as for character names and imaginary locales (Middle-earth).
- **[4.17] Basic vs. subsidiary rights** — Basic = reproduce and distribute (publish). Subsidiary = exploiting markets the publisher is not in (translations, audio, film), usually licensed to third parties; "permissions" (course-pack copying, reusing an illustration) is a form of licensing handled here too.
- **[4.18] Author retention** — Agented authors usually keep some subsidiary rights and license them directly; permissions usually stay with the publisher.
- **[4.19] Non-US licensors** — Foreign licensors commonly retain derivative-work rights (film); a US licensee should insist on control of subsidiary rights tied to publishing the text in specialized markets, plus audio.

## Copyright and the Public Domain  (4.20–4.23)
- **[4.20] Before 1978** — Dual system: common-law (state) copyright from creation until publication, however long; statutory (federal) copyright from publication for 28 years if proper notice appeared, renewable for 28 more (if timely registered and renewed) — normally 56 years, then public domain.
- **[4.21] After 1978** — One unitary federal copyright of far greater length; formalities largely eliminated, so public-domain entry is "almost theoretical" for new works, though restoration gave older foreign works "an unexpected reprieve" (4.30).
- **[4.22] US government works** — Works by federal employees in the course of official duties are public domain; work by private parties under government contract is copyrightable; state and foreign government works are presumptively copyrightable.
- **[4.23] Using public-domain works** — PD work "is free for all to use," directly (Twain reprints) or as raw material for adaptations; the new work's copyright covers only its new material. See table 4.1 (duration chart) and 4.27–31, 4.40–48.

## Duration  (4.24–4.31)
- **[4.24] Life plus seventy** — Author's life + 70 years; joint works: life of the last author to die + 70. All terms run through Dec. 31 of the expiry year.
- **[4.25] Works made for hire** — 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter.
- **[4.26] Anonymous and pseudonymous** — Same fixed term as WMFH; if the author's identity is later revealed and recorded with the Copyright Office, life + 70 takes over (unless WMFH).
- **[4.27] Pre-1978 unpublished** — Same terms as post-1977 works, plus: protected at least through Dec. 31, 2002, and if published before Dec. 31, 2002, at least through Dec. 31, 2047.
- **[4.28] Pre-1978 published in the US** — 1923–1963 works got 95 years from first publication *if renewed in the 28th year*; the term has been expiring year by year (pre-1924 works at end of 2018, pre-1925 at end of 2019, and so on). 1964–1977 works renew automatically and are protected 95 years without fail. Timely renewal before 1964 "cannot be overstated" — the extension did not revive lapsed copyrights.
- **[4.29] New editions** — Each material revision creates a new copyright in the new material: a 1920 treatise may be PD while its 1934 revision is not, and the right to reprint the 1920 text "may be an empty privilege."
- **[4.30] Pre-1978 published abroad** — Foreign works got the pre-1978 US term without the notice and renewal conditions (restoration effective Jan. 1, 1996), and US and foreign terms do not necessarily sync: a 1922 French work may be PD in the US but protected in France through 2040, so foreign markets may be closed to a US reprint. Ninth Circuit anomaly: foreign works may escape the 95-year rule if no US compliance was attempted.
- **[4.31] Restoration eligibility** — First published abroad, at least one author not a US citizen or domiciliary; exception: works published in the US within 30 days of publication abroad (the old "back door" to Berne).

## Renewing Copyright in Pre-1978 Works  (4.32–4.34)
- **[4.32] Benefits of renewal** — Active filing fixed second-term ownership on the filing date and is prima facie evidence of copyright and its ownership; automatic renewal vests in whoever could have renewed. **[4.33] Author renews** if living. **[4.34] If deceased**, heirs (spouse, children, others) renew, but a publisher holding renewal rights may file in their names. [Collapsed: not writer-facing beyond "who owns the renewal term."]

## Assigning or Licensing Copyright  (4.35–4.39)
- **[4.35] Subdividing a copyright** — Each right can be licensed separately and carved by geography, time, or medium (North American vs. UK rights; a ten-year French translation license; hardcover vs. paperback).
- **[4.36] Exclusive vs. nonexclusive** — Exclusive licensees are treated like owners and may sue infringers; nonexclusive licensees hold "something more like a personal privilege" and cannot sue or transfer without permission. Drafters of exclusive licenses should expressly permit assignment and sublicensing.
- **[4.37] Goals of the parties** — Licensors draft narrow to preserve future flexibility, licensees draft broad; both need clarity and should plan for evolving technologies.
- **[4.38] Payment** — Payment and license are reciprocal, not mutually dependent: nonpayment gives a contract claim, not an infringement claim, unless the contract makes timely payment a condition.
- **[4.39] Termination and reversion** — "Out of print" reversion clauses fit badly with ebooks and print-on-demand, so contracts now use sales benchmarks. Statute allows authors to terminate post-1977 grants after 35 years (and comparable pre-1978 grants); no termination right for WMFH.

## Copyright Notice  (4.40–4.48)
- **[4.40] History** — Notice rules caused "most forfeitures of copyright" and were "largely a trap for the unwary"; softened in 1978, removed almost entirely in 1989.
- **[4.41] Three regimes** — No notice required for works first published on or after Mar. 1, 1989; required (with curing options, 4.48) for US works published Jan. 1, 1978–Feb. 28, 1989; near-certain forfeiture for pre-1978 US works lacking notice on all copies. **Use notice anyway** to deprive infringers of any defense of ignorance.
- **[4.42] Three elements** — (1) © (preferred, also satisfies the Universal Copyright Convention) or the word *Copyright* or *Copr.*; (2) year of first publication; (3) name of the copyright owner. **MECHANICAL**
  - "All rights reserved" is harmless but its advantages "have all but vanished"; "No part of this work may be reproduced…" notices are No Trespassing signs with no legal effect.
  - Renewal year is customary but not required; year not needed on greeting cards and similar.
- **[4.43] Unpublished works** — A truncated notice ("Copyright [no date] J. Smith") is harmless on circulating manuscripts, but publishers may find it "unprofessional or even offensive, as indicating a lack of trust." **JUDGMENT**
- **[4.44] Whose name** — Use the author's name unless all rights are assigned; a publisher's name is common even without full ownership. An author's insistence on being named "has absolutely no effect on the allocation of rights" (some publishers leave one right, e.g., performance rights, so naming the author is accurate).
- **[4.45] Placement** — Any placement giving "reasonable notice of the claim of copyright"; in practice books use the title page or its verso, journals the title page, first page of text, or cover. **MECHANICAL**
- **[4.46] US government material** — If a work is "preponderantly" federal material, say so in the notice, positively ("Copyright is claimed only in the introduction, notes, appendixes, and index…") or negatively ("Copyright is not claimed in 'Forest Management,' a publication of the United States government…").
- **[4.47] Derivative works** — A derivative work may carry a new notice and new year, and need not delineate what is and is not covered — which unfortunately makes users think embedded public-domain material is protected. Best practice: list the publication years of all editions (see 1.25).
- **[4.48] Correcting mistakes** — Pre-1978: no cure. 1978–1989: a wrong owner name or a date off by no more than a year was largely excused; omissions from a "relatively small" number of copies were excused; otherwise register within five years and make a "reasonable effort" to add notice.

## Deposit and Registration  (4.49–4.54)
- **[4.49] Deposit** — Mandatory Library of Congress deposit of physical copies was held unconstitutional by the DC Circuit in 2023 (18th ed.); deposit remains mandatory to register.
- **[4.50] Forms and fees** — Register online at Copyright.gov/registration; paper forms only for some group registrations. Group registration covers up to 750 photographs, up to ten unpublished works, short online-only literary works, periodical contributions by one author, and some serials, for a single fee.
- **[4.51] Accuracy and candor** — Answer every question correctly; courts have sanctioned owners and dismissed cases for misleading the Office (e.g., hiding reliance on preexisting material). No duty to volunteer adverse claims, but AI use must be disclosed at least in general terms — which expression the AI made and which the human made (18th ed.).
- **[4.52] Why register** — Registration is a prerequisite to suit (except non-US authors first published abroad); registering within three months of publication or before infringement begins unlocks statutory damages and attorney's fees. "Preregistration" covers prepublication piracy fears.
- **[4.53] Successive editions** — Register again if substantially revised — unless the new edition cannot be infringed without infringing the original registration. Change of ownership is recorded, not re-registered.
- **[4.54] Fixing a registration** — File a Supplementary Registration online, repeating accurate information and adding the new (a missing coauthor, for instance).

## The Publishing Agreement, Open Access, and Warranties  (4.55–4.78) — compressed
- **[4.55] Basic rights**, **[4.56] basic book-contract provisions**, **[4.57] option clauses** — Publishers need (and insist on) exclusive rights to copy and distribute; contracts cover royalties and escalators, advances, manuscript acceptance standards, index, author say over title/cover/design/marketing copy, publication deadlines, subsidiary-rights splits, indemnification and media-perils insurance, division of infringement recoveries, noncompete scope, revision duties, reversion, and audit rights. Options on "the author's next book," first refusal, and matching rights still appear, especially in series.
- **[4.58] Other contracts**, **[4.59] journal transfers**, **[4.60] less than full rights**, **[4.61] journal editors**, **[4.62] edited books**, **[4.63] symposium proceedings** — Without a written transfer a journal publisher gets only the privilege of distributing the piece in that journal — and authors rarely realize this. Limited or customized licenses must still capture needed subsidiary rights (especially foreign-language and database) and exclusivity where required. A journal editor's selection and arrangement is authorship of a compilation (the editor's, unless an employee); edited books and symposium proceedings follow the same logic, with the volume editor contracted as an author.
- **[4.64] Theses and dissertations** — Authors normally keep copyright and may register, but repository deposit and embargo terms can limit later reuse in books or articles; third-party material beyond fair use needs written permission and documentation.
- **[4.65] Open-access models** — Open access changes the copyright clauses, not the existence, of a publishing agreement. **[4.66] Green** = deposit a version in a repository (preprints or the peer-reviewed manuscript, not the typeset version of record). **[4.67] Gold** = CC license plus an institutional subsidy or an article processing charge. **[4.68] Diamond** = fully subsidized. **[4.69] University licenses** give the institution a presumptive nonexclusive license, often via an author addendum (Chicago criticizes typical addenda as vague about *which* version and about "noncommercial"). **[4.70] Public-access policies** (NIH/PubMed Central and a dozen other agencies) mandate deposit of the accepted manuscript with no opt-out. **[4.71] Authors' own works** — posting on campus or personal sites and reuse in the author's own books is customary; publishers prefer the author to avoid a CC license or to use an NC one.
- **[4.72–4.74] Creative Commons** — A CC license is irrevocable and waives at least reproduction and distribution, often derivative control; absent an NC term anyone may use the work commercially. The six licenses run CC BY, BY-SA, BY-ND, BY-NC, BY-NC-SA, BY-NC-ND. **[4.73]** Apply it where the copyright notice goes and **omit "All rights reserved."** **MECHANICAL** **[4.74]** Publishers should push for BY-NC on individual articles and add ND for the issue as a whole.
- **[4.75] Self-publishing** — Platform contracts are typically nonexclusive, the author keeps copyright and sets price within limits; self-publishing does not preclude later traditional publication once the agreement is terminated.
- **[4.76] Author's copyright warranties** — The author guarantees the work is original, owned by the author, not previously published, not under another agreement, and that all third-party copyrighted material is used with permission or by fair use. The permission burden is the author's unless the contract says otherwise. (18th ed.: publishers should also require a warranty that AI was not used except as disclosed.)
- **[4.77] Other warranties** — No libel, no invasion of privacy, and sometimes accuracy of fact and safety of instructions. Rule of thumb given: defamation = assertions of fact (not opinion) about identifiable living people that would hold them up to "scorn or ridicule"; invasion of privacy = public disclosure of private facts "highly offensive to a reasonable person." Public figures face a much higher bar; corporations have no privacy right but can sue for defamation. Query the author rather than relying on the warranty.
- **[4.78] Counsel** — Have house counsel review doubtful passages; counsel advises the publisher, not the author, and counsel's suggested changes "should be followed absent compelling considerations to the contrary."

## Obtaining Permissions  (4.79–4.87)
- **[4.79] General principle** — **Get permission for any copyrighted material created by others unless the use is fair use.** Get it in writing (oral permission is "most unwise" to rely on). No permission is needed for US government works or works whose copyright has expired. Public-domain *text* does not free up a modern edition's translation, abridgment, introduction, notes, or apparatus.
  > But whether permission is needed or not, the author should always, as a matter of good practice (and to avoid any possible charge of plagiarism), credit any sources used. (4.79)
- **[4.80] Start early** — The contract usually puts permissions on the author; keep a record of every rights holder from the start of writing; expect weeks or months. World rights may be split (US/Canada with one publisher, Europe/Commonwealth/ROW with others), requiring separate letters. Begin as soon as the manuscript is accepted; production often waits until permissions are in hand.
- **[4.81] Releases** — Get the interviewee's permission in writing or on the recording for any extract beyond fair use; for photographs, the photographer owns copyright, but the subject's release is required for **cover or marketing use** — not for interior illustration.
- **[4.82] Your own earlier work** — Reusing or even revising your own published chapter needs the prior publisher's permission unless the earlier contract reserved reuse or the material was CC-licensed. Credit prior publication (copyright page, first-page footnote, or acknowledgments) as courtesy, contract, or a condition of permission.
- **[4.83] Fees and record keeping** — Fees are normally the author's; send all permissions to the publisher, which files them with the contract; the copyeditor checks that every required credit appears.
- **[4.84] Scope of the grant** — Many grants withhold sublicensing, or limit to one edition, a print run, or five to seven years; then new permission (and a new fee) is needed for reprints, paperbacks, serialization, or later editions. Ask up front for all editions and media.
- **[4.85] Unpublished material** — Deal with the author or heirs, who may be hard to identify or find.
- **[4.86] Missing owners / orphan works** — Conduct and document a "reasonable" search (Copyright Office records, internet, relevant databases); if the owner surfaces, "it is unlikely that any court would do more than require the payment of a reasonable permissions fee" — so be ready to offer and pay one.
- **[4.87] Archive restrictions** — Copyright in a manuscript ≠ ownership of the paper: two permissions may be needed, one for access and one to copy. Ask the librarian or archivist what else restricts use.

## Fair Use: Quoting Without Permission  (4.88–4.98)
- **[4.88] The four factors** — Fair use is an equitable limit on absolute copyright, and the statute does not fix its boundaries. Weigh, at minimum:
  1. **The purpose and character of the use**, including whether it is commercial or nonprofit educational
  2. **The nature of the copyrighted work**
  3. **The amount and substantiality of the portion used** in relation to the work as a whole
  4. **The effect of the use on the potential market for, or value of, the work**
  > Authors invoking fair use should transcribe accurately and give credit to their sources. They should not quote in such a way as to make the author of the quoted passage seem to be saying something opposite to, or different from, what was intended. (4.88)
- **[4.89] Rules of thumb have no legal force** — "Courts, not publishers, adjudicate fair use." Publisher yardsticks exist to give permissions staff a workable default. **JUDGMENT**
- **[4.90] How much may be quoted without permission** — Transformative uses (different purpose, changed context) are judged "more leniently" than uses that "merely parallel or parrot the original."
  > Fair use is use that is fair—simply that. (4.90)
  - Whole-work use of a poem, essay, or article is "hardly ever acceptable."
  - **Never quote more than a few contiguous paragraphs of prose or lines of poetry at a time**, and never let scattered quotations "begin to overshadow the quoter's own material."
  - Substantial quotation is fine inside a critique; the same words used to restate the first author's argument or "embellish one's own prose" are not.
  - **Proportion beats absolute length**: 500 words from a 5,000-word essay is riskier than 500 from 50,000; but a tiny percentage can still infringe if it is "the heart of the work."
- **[4.91] Epigraphs and lyrics** — An epigraph is likely fair use if its resonance with the text makes it transformative; if it is "largely decorative," the argument is "notably weaker." Same for short lyric and poetry quotation inside narrative. This never licenses a collection of epigraphs or lyrics.
- **[4.92] Unpublished works** — Same factors apply, with added weight on a living author's (or spouse's or children's) interest in controlling first disclosure.
- **[4.93] Paraphrasing** — Paraphrase is no escape: extensive paraphrase is "merely disguised copying" and gets the same analysis; very small paraphrase may not be copying at all.
- **[4.94] Images** — Details of paintings in a critical monograph are fair; a full black-and-white reproduction may be; thumbnail sketches are fair "since they are so small and of such poor resolution that they cannot reduce the commercial value"; a full-page vivid color plate, or cover use, is commercial and needs a much stronger case. The Grateful Dead case allowed full-color concert posters as "historical artifacts" at "the minimal image size and quality necessary to ensure the reader's recognition." **JUDGMENT**
- **[4.95] Charts, tables, graphs** — Reproducing a single graph, table, or chart "that simply presents data in a straightforward relationship" should ordinarily be fair use; embellished charts are riskier; some fact-only graphs may not be copyrightable at all. **JUDGMENT**
- **[4.96] Attribution** — Always name the source; a standard footnote or a legend suffices. **MECHANICAL**
  - ex: "Source: [author, title, and date of earlier work]"
  - ex: for verbatim reproduction, prefer "Reprinted from [author, title, and date]" over "Source."
  - If the new work will carry a CC license, reproduce the original's copyright notice so no one thinks the borrowed material is CC-licensed.
- **[4.97] Don't over-ask** — Reflexive permission-seeking is "unfortunate" — fair use "should not be allowed to decay because scholars fail to employ it boldly," and permissions processing slows publication. The Supreme Court holds that requesting permission is not an admission that permission was needed; if permission is refused or priced unreasonably, make the fair use case.
- **[4.98] Chicago's own guidelines** — University of Chicago Press posts its fair-use guidelines for quoting from its publications, meant to be "generous and fair to all parties."

## Requesting Permission  (4.99–4.105)
- **[4.99] The process** — Use publishers' online permissions forms, the Copyright Clearance Center (US), or PLSclear (UK); many have standard fee schedules. Song lyrics are controlled by music publishers (findable via the Copyright Office, ASCAP, or BMI) and the process is slow and unautomated.
- **[4.100] What to include in a request** — (1) title of the original work plus exact identification with page numbers (figure number and page; poem title, page, and lines; prose opening and closing phrases with pages), citing the **original** source, not a later reuse; (2) details of the new publication — title, length, formats, publisher, date, print run, list price; (3) the rights sought.
  - Minimum acceptable grant: "nonexclusive world rights in the English language, for one edition."
  - Ask for: all editions in all media, the right to include the material in translation licenses, the right to grant customary permissions (where the material sits inside your own work), and the right to assign the license — none of which is implied by a nonexclusive license (4.36).
- **[4.101] Who owns what** — Charts and graphs follow the text's copyright; pictorial and sculptural works usually belong to the artist or the estate (license through the Artists Rights Society or its counterparts). Pre-1978, copyright in an artwork often traveled with the physical object; post-1977 it stays with the artist absent a written assignment. Museums may charge for photography or high-resolution files even of public-domain works; the law denies copyright to photographs that merely reproduce another two-dimensional image.
- **[4.102] Stock agencies** — Photographs are the easiest clearance, via stock agencies and image archives with published fee schedules.
- **[4.103] Illustration requests** — Specify the work, the publication, the reproduction size (quarter page, half page), and the scope of rights; say explicitly if the image will appear on the cover or in promotion.
- **[4.104] Alt text** — Illustrated works need alternative text describing each image (see 3.28); museums and licensing societies often supply descriptions if asked (18th ed., accessibility).
- **[4.105] Fees** — Image fees usually cover one-time use only; cover and advertising use cost more; paperbacks and new editions incur new fees. Try to secure all editions and media.

## Acknowledging Sources and Fees  (4.106–4.109)
- **[4.106] Credit lines** — Give the exact source whether or not permission was needed; follow the grantor's stipulated wording. **MECHANICAL**
  - ex: "Reprinted by permission of the publisher."
  - ex: "Courtesy of the Newberry Library, Chicago, Illinois."
- **[4.107] Acknowledgments sections** — Group many credits at the front or back, but still cite the source on the page where the material appears.
- **[4.108] Who pays** — The author, under most agreements; the publisher may pay and deduct from royalties, split the cost, or (textbooks) fund illustrations itself.
- **[4.109] Anthologies** — Begin clearing permissions as soon as the contract or letter of intent is in hand; a selection can be refused or priced out, so the table of contents is not final until all permissions and fees are settled.

## Subsidiary Rights and Permissions  (4.110–4.115) — compressed
- **[4.110] Handling requests in house**, **[4.111] the rights database** — Rights-and-permissions staff handle course-pack and e-reserve requests and whole-work licensing; a complete, accurate rights database (including third-party material used by permission and any author-approval condition) is prerequisite to licensing safely.
- **[4.112] Categories of subsidiary rights**, **[4.113] electronic-rights licensing** — Territory rights (UK, Commonwealth, Europe/Middle East, "ROW"), translation, serial (first/second), paperback, book club, reprint, abridgment, accessible-format, dramatic (stage/film), audio (dramatic vs. nondramatic), graphic-novel, electronic, and enhanced electronic rights. Electronic licenses should generally be nonexclusive, short (a few years), and conditioned on the licensee's download safeguards; keep territory metadata accurate.
- **[4.114] Economic considerations**, **[4.115] permissions** — Proceeds are split per contract — the author typically gets at least 50%, often more; sweetheart affiliate licenses are "suspect and of doubtful legality." "Permissions" is the catchall: nonexclusive, nonrecurring, modest fees, for quoting beyond fair use or reproducing a chapter in a course pack.

## Overview: what an index is for (15.1–15.8)
- **[15.1–15.2] Purpose** — An index gathers the substantive terms and subjects of a work, sorts them alphabetically, adds cross-references, and attaches locators; it is written for readers, not generated from the text, and in a searchable age it still "provides insurance against fruitless queries and unintended results."
  > "a good index can do what a plain search cannot: It gathers all the substantive terms and subjects of the work, sorts them alphabetically, provides cross-references to and from related terms, and includes specific page numbers or other locators" … "In a word, a good index makes the text more accessible."
  - ex: text says "countries outside the sphere of US and Soviet influence played those two nations against each other" — a search for *Cold War* will not find it, so an index entry for "Cold War" is required.
- **[15.3] Who should index** — The indexer must see the work whole and guess what readers will look for; authorial knowledge alone is insufficient. JUDGMENT.
- **[15.4–15.5] Deadlines and software** — Indexing happens after page proofs, usually within two to six weeks; a concordance (mechanical list of terms) is *not* an index, and word processors give only rudimentary sorting and cross-reference help. JUDGMENT.
- **[15.6] One index or several** — Use a single comprehensive index for most works; add an author index, index of first lines, or taxonomic index only when the genre demands it, and make each visually distinct with its own running head (Index of Names, Index of Subjects).
- **[15.7] Embedded indexes** — Selection principles for key-term coding in electronic works match those for back-of-the-book indexes.
- [15.8] resources for indexers (Mulvany, *Indexing Books*; Wellisch; Fetters) — bibliographic only; skipped.

## Components: headings, subentries, locators (15.9–15.14)
- **[15.9] Main headings** — A main heading is normally a noun or noun phrase; make count nouns and count-noun phrases plural; never let an adjective stand alone as a heading; invert the phrase when that puts the keyword first.
  - ex: "agricultural collectivization"; "imperialism, American"; "police, Soviet secret"; "Brest-Litovsk, Treaty of"; "Cold War"
- **[15.10] Subentries** — Any entry needing more than five or six locators is broken into subentries; subheadings either form a phrase with the heading ("capitalism: and American prohibition…") or mark divisions within it ("Native American peoples: Chinantec…; Huastec…"). JUDGMENT.
- **[15.11] Capitalization of headings** — Capitalize the first word of a main heading only if it is capitalized in text (proper noun, genus name, title); subheadings are always lowercase unless the keyword itself is capitalized in text. Science indexes should generally avoid initial capitals.
  - MECHANICAL: the all-caps heading style is recommended "only where the subentries are so numerous that capitalized main headings make for easier navigation."
- **[15.12] Locators** — Use page, paragraph, or section numbers; give inclusive numbers for a discussion that runs on (`34–36`, `10.36–41`); give separate locators for scattered references (`34, 35, 36`).
  - ex: *ff* and *et seq.* "should never be used in an index."
  - ex: *passim* "has often been used" for scattered references (`78–88 passim`), but "individual locators are preferred."
- **[15.13] Electronic indexes** — Link entries directly to the passage, not to a nominal page: in reflowable text a page-based link lands screens away from the target.
- **[15.14] Inclusive numbers** — Use the text's own system; Chicago's traditional abbreviation (preferred over giving full numbers everywhere) has four cases, plus Roman numerals in full.
  - MECHANICAL: <100 → all digits (`3–10`, `96–117`); 100 and multiples of 100 → all digits (`100–104`, `1100–1113`); 101–109, 201–209 etc. → changed part only (`101–8`, `808–33`, `1103–4`); 110–199, 210–299 etc. → two digits unless more are needed (`321–28`, `498–532`, `1496–500`, `11564–615`); `xxv–xxviii`, `cvi–cix`.

## Cross-references (15.15–15.23)
- **[15.15] Two kinds, used sparingly** — *See* redirects; *see also* points to related material. Both are italicized, and an overabundance "may signal the need for consolidation of entries." JUDGMENT.
- **[15.16] *See* references** — Send readers from an informal term, pseudonym, variant spelling, alias, or abbreviation to the full entry; **never include locators**; never let a *see* lead to another *see*. If both entries would be about equally long, **double-post** instead.
  - ex: "FBI (Federal Bureau of Investigation), 145–48 / Federal Bureau of Investigation, 145–48" — rather than "Federal Bureau of Investigation. See FBI"
- **[15.17] *See* after a main heading** — Precede with a period, capitalize *See*; two or more refs go in alphabetical order, separated by semicolons, mirroring the heading's capitalization and word order.
  - ex: "Clemens, Samuel. See Twain, Mark"; "The Hague. See Hague, The"; "Mormons. See Latter-day Saints, Church of Jesus Christ of"
- **[15.18] *See* after a subheading** — Put it in parentheses and lowercase *see*.
  - ex: "statistical material, 16, 17, 89; as online supplement (see supplements, online); proofreading, 183"
- **[15.19] *See* to a subheading** — Use *see under* ("lace making. See under Bruges"); if that could misdirect, drop *under* and add the subheading's wording after a colon — a colon is preferred to a comma, and the wording must match the subheading exactly.
  - ex: "Pride and Prejudice. See Austen, Jane: Pride and Prejudice"
- **[15.20] *See also*** — Place at the end of an entry to point to additional, genuinely different material; do not use it for synonyms (combine those) and never let it lead to an entry with no new locators. In a run-in index it follows a period; in an indented index it sits on its own line; *See also* is capitalized, both words italic; multiple refs alphabetical, semicolon-separated; *see also under* for a subentry, or a colon.
- **[15.21] Correspondence** — Cite the cross-referenced heading in full, with its capitalization, inversion, and punctuation exactly as printed; shorten a long heading only if consistent and unambiguous.
- **[15.22] Italics** — *see*, *see under*, *see also* are italic; set them in roman when what follows is itself italic, so the words stay distinguishable.
  - ex: "Austen, Jane. See Pride and Prejudice" but "Pride and Prejudice. See Austen, Jane"
- **[15.23] Generic cross-references** — A ref may point to a *type* of heading ("See names of individual buildings"); italicize the whole ref, and place it last even out of alphabetical order, preceded by a semicolon and joined with *and*.
  - ex: "dogs, 35–42. See also American Kennel Club; shelters; and individual breed names"

## Run-in versus indented format (15.24–15.28)
- **[15.24] Flush-and-hang** — Set every entry flush left on the first line with runovers indented; multiple columns in print, never columns in a manuscript.
- **[15.25] Run-in** — Run subentries on after the main heading, separated by semicolons; colon after the heading when subentries follow immediately; a comma before locators that follow the heading directly. Chicago and many publishers prefer run-in for its economy; it works best with one level of subentry.
- **[15.26] Indented (stacked)** — Start each subentry on a new line indented one em; no colon after the heading, no semicolons between subentries; indent runovers two ems; put entry-level cross-refs after the last subentry and subentry-level refs in parentheses. Preferred in scientific and reference works and wherever sub-subentries are needed.
- **[15.27] Sub-subentries in a run-in index** — More than a handful means switch to indented; a very few can be avoided by repeating the keyword, or handled by starting those subentries on a new line after an em dash flush with the margin, with sub-subentries run in. Never use em dashes where only one level of subentry exists.
- **[15.28] Sub-subentries in an indented index** — Best run in; if that fails, indent them deeper than the subentries, which forces runovers to three ems and may yield very short lines. Sub-sub-*sub*entries "should be avoided if at all possible."

## General principles of indexing (15.29–15.31)
- **[15.29] The index mirrors the text, always** — Follow the work's own style: British spelling if the text is British, *Shakspere* if the text says so, no modernization of place-names (Constantinople, Siam) and no accent-fixing (*Schonberg*, not Schoenberg); italicize or quote titles exactly as in text; use the text's inclusive-number style. Add a cross-reference only where readers might otherwise miss the entry. MECHANICAL + JUDGMENT.
- **[15.30] Choose terms from the author's usage** — Headings must be concise and logical; index a philosopher's *essence* under *essence* (with a cross-ref from *being*); if two terms are used interchangeably, either pick one (cross-ref imperative) or double-post. JUDGMENT.
- **[15.31] What not to index** — Skip passing mentions, scene-setting, and off-theme names: in a history of the US automobile, index the MG but not Northampton or Ann Arbor. Vanity entries are "not forbidden" if readers might look for their own names. JUDGMENT.

## What to index (15.32–15.39)
- **[15.32] Parts of the work** — Index the entire text; skip title page, dedication, epigraphs, lists of illustrations and tables, acknowledgments; index a preface or foreword by another if it concerns the subject; always index substantive introductions; index appendixes that supplement the text but not ones that merely reproduce documents (a treaty text, a questionnaire); glossaries and bibliographies are usually not indexed.
- **[15.33] Notes** — Index substantive notes only, not reference notes that merely cite sources; a mixed note is indexed for its substantive content.
- **[15.34] Endnote locators** — Page + *n* + note number, **no internal space** (`334n14`); consecutive notes take two *n*'s and an en dash (`334nn14–16`); nonconsecutive notes are cited separately (`334n14, 334n16, 334n19`); add the chapter in parentheses when notes from different chapters share a page (`334n2 (chap. 8)`). MECHANICAL.
- **[15.35] Footnote locators** — As for endnotes; a sole note on a page may drop the number (`156n`), never when several notes share the page; use the symbol if symbols are used (`156n*`); if text and related note are both indexable give the page once, if unrelated cite both (`156, 156n, 278, 278n30`).
- **[15.36] Notes running over a page** — Give the first page only; use the second if the reference is to that part; a run of notes may need inclusive pages (`234–35nn19–23`).
- **[15.37] Parenthetical citations** — Author-date citations in text are not normally indexed unless they document an otherwise unattributed statement; any author *discussed* is indexed; legal works usually index case citations.
- **[15.38] Author indexes** — Make a separate entry for each author named in text and do not index those hidden in *et al.*
  - ex: "(Jones, Smith, and Black 1999)" → Black, Jones, Smith; "(Sanchez et al. 2001)" → Sanchez only
- **[15.39] Illustrations and tables** — Index them when important and not listed in the table of contents; mark references with italic or bold plus a headnote, or with roman `t`/`f`/`pl` and no space (`208t`, `210f`), or with *table*/*fig.* and no comma (`88 table 5`).

## Indexing names: variant forms (15.40–15.54)
- **[15.41] Use the familiar full form** — Index the form of a name by which the person is widely known, respecting the text's spelling; give the fuller form only in a work devoted to that person.
  - ex: "Cervantes, Miguel de [not Cervantes Saavedra, Miguel de]"; "Fisher, M. F. K."; "Poe, Edgar Allan [not Poe, E. A.]"
- **[15.42] Pseudonyms** — List under the real name when both appear; if the pseudonym is a household word, use it as the main entry with the real name in parentheses and usually no cross-ref; omit alternative forms absent from the text, especially a deadname.
  - ex: "Twain, Mark (Samuel Langhorne Clemens)"; "Ai. See Russell, George William"; but "Rihanna (Robyn Rihanna Fenty)"
- **[15.43–15.44] Ambiguous and changed names** — Disambiguate same-name persons with a middle initial or a parenthetical tag ("Field, David Dudley (lawyer)" / "(clergyman)"; "Pitt, William (the elder)"); index a woman by her birth name unless the married name is more familiar, and by the married name when she uses both together (unless hyphenated), with cross-refs as needed.
- **[15.45–15.49] Titles** — Index monarchs and popes under the official name with Roman numeral ("Charles III (king)", "Benedict XVI (pope)"); princes and princesses under given names, dukes and earls under the title, with a cross-ref where it differs from the family name; omit *Lord*/*Lady* and usually *Sir*/*Dame* ("Thatcher, Margaret"), add *Rev.*/*Msgr.* only to identify, never keep *Prof.*, *Dr.*, PhD, or MD; retain *Jr.* and *III* after the given name with a comma ("King, Martin Luther, Jr.").
- **[15.50–15.53] Saints, unknowns, epithets, look-alikes** — Index saints under the given name unless better known otherwise; identify incomplete names parenthetically ("John (Smith's shipmate on Stella)"); always expand epithets to the full form ("the Great Emancipator" → "Lincoln, Abraham"); tag confusable names ("New York (city)" / "New York (state)").
- **[15.54] Abbreviations** — Index and alphabetize organizations under their widely known abbreviation, with a parenthetical gloss or cross-ref; lesser-known bodies go under the full name with a cross-ref from the abbreviation.
  - ex: "EEC (European Economic Community)"; "NATO"; "MLA. See Modern Language Association"

## Indexing titles of works (15.55–15.63)
- **[15.55–15.57] Typography and periodicals** — Treat titles typographically as in text; index English-language newspapers and journals as generally known, italicized, initial *The* omitted, city in parentheses if needed ("Plain Dealer (Cleveland)", "Times (London)"); for non-English periodicals the article follows the title after a comma ("Monde, Le (Paris)", "Spiegel, Der").
- **[15.58] Authored works** — A work meriting its own main entry is also indexed under its creator, often as a subentry; the creator's name follows the title in parentheses unless every title shares one creator. ex: "Look Homeward, Angel (Wolfe), 34–37".
- **[15.59] English titles with an initial article** — In a main heading, move *A*, *An*, *The* to the end after a comma ("Professor and the Madman, The"); as a subheading it stays in normal position in a run-in index but is inverted in an indented index.
- **[15.60] Non-English titles** — Retain the initial article: for a general audience, list titles as they appear in text, uninverted, alphabetized by the article ("La bohème"); in a specialized work, invert as in English but ignore the article in alphabetizing ("bohème, La"). Beware *un*/*une*/*ein*/*eine* meaning "one" ("Un deux trois" sorts under U).
- **[15.61–15.63] Prepositions, end punctuation, subtitles** — An initial preposition is never dropped or ignored in alphabetizing ("For Whom the Bell Tolls"); a title ending in ? or ! takes a comma wherever the heading's syntax requires one ("'Are You a Doctor?,' 25"); omit subtitles from headings and subheadings unless needed to identify the work.

## Alphabetizing (15.64–15.101)
### The two systems (15.64–15.69)
- **[15.64] Basic rule** — The first word of a main heading determines its place, which sometimes requires inversion; file where readers will look first ("Tale of Two Cities, A" under T).
- **[15.65] Software will not do it for you** — No standard word-processor sort conforms exactly to either system; the output must be edited. Word processors default to word-by-word.
- **[15.66] Chicago now prefers word-by-word** — Choose the system before starting; word-by-word is easier where many open compounds share a first word. "Chicago now prefers the word-by-word system (a departure from previous editions) but will not normally impose it on a well-prepared index arranged letter by letter." (18th-ed. change; dictionaries sort letter by letter, library catalogs word by word.)
- **[15.67] Letter-by-letter** — Alphabetize up to the first parenthesis or comma, then restart after it; ignore spaces and all other punctuation; precedence: one word → word + parenthesis → word + comma → word + number → word + letters.
- **[15.68] Word-by-word** — Alphabetize only to the end of the first word (an abbreviation or hyphenated compound counts as one word), using later words only to break ties; same parenthesis/comma interruption; precedence inserts word + space before numbers and letters.
- **[15.69] The difference, concretely** — Both systems put a parenthesis before a comma and ignore hyphens, slashes, quotation marks, and periods; the letter-by-letter index runs *new-12 compound, newborn, newcomer, New Deal, new economics*, while word-by-word runs *New Deal, new economics, New England, new math, … new-12 compound, newborn, newcomer*.

### General rules (15.70–15.75)
- **[15.70] Same name, different entities** — Arrange person, place, and thing in plain alphabetical order ("hoe, garden" / "Hoe, Robert"), adjusting neighbors as needed ("London, Amy" / "London (England)" / "London, Jack").
- **[15.71] Initials first** — Initials used for a given name precede spelled-out names on the same letter: Oppenheimer, J. Robert; Oppenheimer, K. T.; Oppenheimer, James N.; Oppenheimer, Katharine S. MECHANICAL.
- **[15.72] Abbreviations and acronyms** — Alphabetize acronyms, initialisms, and most abbreviations as they appear and intersperse them ("faculty clubs, NATO, FBI, North Pole, Feely, John, NOW"). Exceptions: *&* may be treated as spelled out; *@* may be ignored or treated as *a*.
- **[15.73] Numerals at the start** — Alphabetize as spelled out ("1984 (Orwell)" as *nineteen eighty-four*; "125th Street" as *one hundred twenty-fifth street*); if there are many, list them numerically at the front of the index before the As.
- **[15.74] Similar headings with numerals** — Order like headings numerically regardless of pronunciation: Henry III, Henry IV, Henry V; E7, L44, L50; section 9, section 44, section 77. MECHANICAL.
- **[15.75] Accents** — Alphabetize accented letters as unaccented: Ubeda / Über den Gipfel / Ubina; Schoenberg / Schomberg / Schonborn. MECHANICAL.

### Subentries (15.76–15.78)
- **[15.76] Alphabetical order** — Disregard introductory articles, prepositions, and conjunctions ("as anti-Fascist", "on Curzon line"); avoid substantive openers such as "relations with" or "views on," and in an indented index invert instead ("Curzon line, views on, 348, 379"). JUDGMENT.
- **[15.77] Numerical order** — Some subentries demand it inside an otherwise alphabetical entry ("Daley, Richard J. (mayor): third term, 205; fourth term, 206–7"; "flora, alpine: at 1,000-meter level…").
- **[15.78] Chronological order** — Permitted for a biography's subentries in a run-in index, to summarize a career and keep "death of" from leading; use only when the chronology is obvious. JUDGMENT.

### Personal names (15.79–15.95)
- **[15.79] Particles** — Alphabetize by the individual's known usage and national tradition, with cross-refs often advisable; Chicago follows the form readers will seek.
  - ex: Beauvoir, Simone de; Ben-Gurion, David; Costa, Uriel da; da Cunha, Euclides; D'Amato, Alfonse; de Gaulle, Charles; La Fontaine, Jean de; Leonardo da Vinci; Medici, Lorenzo de'; Van Rensselaer, Stephen
- **[15.80] Compound family names** — Alphabetize by the first element: Lloyd George, David; Mies van der Rohe, Ludwig; Sackville-West, Victoria; Teilhard de Chardin, Pierre.
- **[15.81–15.82] *Mac*, *Mc*, *O'*, *Saint*** — *Mac*/*Mc* file letter by letter as spelled (Macalister, MacAlister, Macauley, Macmillan; Madison, McAllister, McAuley, McMillan); *O'* files as if the apostrophe were missing (Onassis, O'Neill, Ongaro); names spelled *Saint*, *San*, or *St.* file as spelled, with a cross-ref if the forms are far apart.
- **[15.83–15.84] Arabic and Burmese** — File *al-*/*el* names under the element after the particle (Hakim, Tawfiq al-) but *Abu*, *Abd*, *Ibn* under those elements; file Burmese under the first element with terms of respect (*U*, *Daw*) omitted or trailing ("Thant, U").
- **[15.85–15.90] East Asian names** — Do **not** invert or comma Chinese, Japanese, or Korean names given in family-name-first order (Li Bai; Tajima Yumiko; Kim Dae-jung); invert when the text uses Western order (Kung, H. H.; Kurosawa, Noriaki; Lee, Chang-rae). Hungarian names are re-inverted with a comma (Bartók, Béla). Indian names file family name last (Gandhi, Mohandas Karamchand); Indonesian usage varies and must be checked against the text.
- **[15.91–15.94] Romance and Southeast Asian names** — Portuguese files under the last element, ignoring *Filho*, *Neto*, *Junior* (Martins, Luciana de Lima); Spanish double surnames file under the first element ("García Lorca, Federico"; "Ortega y Gasset, José"), with cross-refs from the second ("Lorca, Federico García. See García Lorca, Federico"); Thai practice varies (seek expert help); Vietnamese file under the last part of the given name ("Diem, Ngo Dinh", "Giap, Vo Nguyen").

### Organizations and places (15.96–15.101)
- **[15.96–15.97] Organizations** — Drop an initial *the* ("University of Chicago", "Beatles (band)"); do **not** invert a personal name used corporately ("A. G. Edwards & Sons, Inc." under A), but file under the family name when the organization is known that way (Shedd Aquarium); index a person and that person's firm separately.
- **[15.98] *Mount*, *Lake*, *Sea of*** — Invert generic first elements and file under the specific name (Geneva, Lake; Japan, Sea of; McKinley, Mount); leave foreign generics as they are (Loch Ness, Mauna Kea, Sierra Nevada); explain a systematic inversion in a headnote, double-post isolated cases, and cross-ref a renamed feature ("McKinley, Mount. See Denali").
- **[15.99–15.101] Articles and *Saint* in place-names** — Drop an informal initial *the* (Bronx, Ozarks, Netherlands) but keep it where it is part of the formal name (Hague, The); file non-English article names under the article (El Paso, La Crosse, Le Havre, Los Alamos, Den Haag); file place-names with *Saint*/*St.* as spelled in text, cross-referencing between forms ("Saint-Cloud", "St. Louis").

## Punctuating indexes: a summary (15.102–15.108)
- **[15.102] Comma** — Comma before the first locator, between locators, after an inverted heading, and after a qualifying phrase when there are no subentries. MECHANICAL.
  - ex: "lighthouses, early history of, 40–42"; "Sabba da Castiglione, Monsignor, 209, 337; on cosmetics, 190, 195, 198"
- **[15.103–15.104] Colon and semicolon** — A colon precedes the first subheading in a run-in index and precedes the subentry wording in a cross-reference ("See also Yucatan: Maya") but never follows a main heading in an indented index; semicolons separate run-in subentries, sub-subentries, and multiple cross-references. MECHANICAL.
- **[15.105] Period** — In a run-in index, only before *See*/*See under*/*See also*; in an indented index, only before *See*; never before a parenthetical *see*/*see also*; never at the end of an entry. MECHANICAL.
- **[15.106–15.108] Parentheses, dashes** — Parentheses enclose identifying or supplementary information ("Charles I (king of England)", "Of Human Bondage (Maugham)"); the em dash appears only for run-in sub-subentries; the en dash carries every inclusive locator ("dogs, 135–42"). MECHANICAL.

## Practical mechanics (15.109–15.139)
- **[15.109–15.111] Timing and inputs** — Do not begin until pagination is final (paragraph/section locators or e-links permit an earlier start); work from a clean, complete set of proofs, ideally a searchable PDF.
- **[15.110] Budget the time** — An index for a 300-page book "could take as much as three weeks' work or more."
- **[15.112–15.115] Confirm house rules first** — Settle alphabetizing system, run-in vs. indented, inclusive-number style, numeric headings, and length with the publisher; dedicated software automates collation and cross-references but cannot generate an index — "there is no substitute for rereading the whole work."
- **[15.116–15.118] Marking** — Read the table of contents and the whole work, then mark terms on the proofs, one chapter (and its notes) at a time, entering and alphabetizing before moving on; a colon on the proof separates a proposed heading from a proposed subheading, and first names go in the margin.
- **[15.117] How dense** — About five references per text page yields a modest index (roughly 1/50 the length of the text); fifteen or more yields a long one (1/20 or more). "It is always easier to drop entries than to add them; err on the side of inclusiveness."
- **[15.119–15.121] Provisional subentries** — Write a modifying phrase for every term as you mark it, or the finished heading will trail "a long string of numbers, which makes for an all but useless index entry"; record inclusive numbers as you go and check each typed locator against the proofs.
- **[15.123–15.125] Second pass and cleanup** — Reread the marked proofs for omissions that later chapters make significant; keep a list of the errors you notice and send it to the publisher; then settle synonyms (agriculture/farming/crop raising) and build the cross-refs that reflect those choices. JUDGMENT.
- **[15.126–15.128] Structure and wording** — Decide main entry vs. subentry by asking where readers look first (kindergarten, elementary school, middle school become subentries under *school* only when they are not the subject); never leave a main heading with more than five or six bare locators; phrase subheadings concisely, opening with the keyword a reader would seek, and omit *a*, *an*, *the*.
- **[15.129–15.130] Consistency and submission** — Verify that every cross-reference matches the edited heading and that double-posted entries carry identical locators; submit unjustified, single-spaced, column-free, with hard returns only at entry (and subentry) ends, hanging indents built with the software, no end-of-line hyphenation, and one file per index.
- **[15.131–15.132] Editing an index** — A badly made index cannot be remade: omit it or have it redone. The editing checklist covers alphabetical order, spelling/capitalization/font, punctuation consistency, cross-references that actually lead somewhere, false locators ("193–93", "12102"), ascending locators, consistent subentry order, overanalyzed entries, unanalyzed strings of locators, and a random accuracy check of five to ten entries.
- **[15.134–15.139] Typography (production, not authorial)** — Set indexes two sizes smaller than text, often in two columns, always ragged right; indent subentries one em, sub-subentries two ems, all runovers three ems; fix one-line orphans at column tops; repeat a heading with "(continued)" when an entry breaks from recto to verso; explain any bold/italic/caps distinction in a headnote.
- [15.140–15.144] examples of index formats (run-in with italic figure refs; indented with run-in and with indented sub-subentries; index of first lines; combined author/title/first-line index) — illustrative only; skipped per brief.

## Highest-leverage glossary terms for a writing agent

Ranked by how often they change a sentence or a page a writer hands in.

1. **serial comma** — insert before *and* in any series of three or more; Chicago says it "prevents ambiguity." (6.19)
2. **em dash** — no space on either side; never open a new em-dash pair inside or right after another one — use parentheses or commas. (6.91–6.96)
3. **en dash** — ranges and compound terms, not a hyphen. (6.82–6.87)
4. **ellipsis** — the three-dot method, three spaced dots, never three-or-four. (12.59–12.69)
5. **title case** — major words up, prepositions of five letters or more up. (8.160–8.162)
6. **sentence case** — override title case wherever Chicago asks for it (subheads may be either, but be consistent). (8.159)
7. **block quotation** — set off only what earns it; otherwise run the quotation in. (12.22)
8. **short title** — for every note after the first; never *op. cit.* or *loc. cit.* (13.36; 13.39)
9. **appositive** — commas unless restrictive; a restrictive appositive carries no commas. (5.26)
10. **caption** — edited as text, with locators and credits checked. (3.21–3.29)
11. **text reference** vs **callout** — the author writes "see table 11.1"; only the production file says "[insert table 11.1 here]."
12. **widow / orphan** — reword rather than fiddle with spacing; use the terms precisely (widow = short final line atop a page; orphan = first line of a paragraph stranded at the foot).
13. **style sheet** — start one on page one; it is what keeps a 300-page manuscript consistent.
14. **cross-reference** — verify every one before submitting; chapters get renumbered. (2.41)
