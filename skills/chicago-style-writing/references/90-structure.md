# Document structure, headings, illustrations, and tables

Source: CMOS ch 1 (rules 1.1-1.126), ch 3 (rules 3.1-3.89). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch01, ch03. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch01

Ranked for application to *any* prose drafted or edited.

1. **[1.3 / 1.4] Put everything in its proper division.** Front matter introduces and orients; the text argues; back matter documents. Acknowledgments, sources, and raw data do not belong in the body.
2. **[1.48 / 1.53] Preface ≠ foreword ≠ introduction ≠ acknowledgments.** Preface = author's own account of the work's origins/method; foreword = someone *else's* remarks, name at the end; introduction = integral to the argument, in the text, arabic pagination, no acknowledgments; acknowledgments = thanks, usually at the back.
3. **[1.45] Reserve "foreword" for non-author remarks** — using it for the author's own note is an error.
4. **[1.53] Never title chapter 1 "Introduction."** Give it "a more evocative title," and add a descriptive subtitle even to a short introduction.
5. **[1.55] Chapter titles: short and descriptive beats long and whimsical** — they are the reader's overview of contents.
6. **[1.61] Never let the first sentence under a heading point back at it** ("This vaulted structure…"). Repeat the key noun. *Highest-frequency mechanical failure in drafted prose.*
7. **[1.61] Make sibling subheads parallel in structure and tone** — same grammatical form across the level.
8. **[1.62] Use the fewest heading levels that work: one is often enough; almost nothing needs more than three.**
9. **[1.62] Never subdivide into a single subsection** — if you have one B-level head, you need two (exceptions: a unique case, or apparatus like endnotes).
10. **[1.63] Don't number headings unless something cross-references them** — "subheads are more useful to a reader than section numbers alone."
11. **[1.41] An epigraph is "pertinent but not integral"** — if the text depends on the quotation, it belongs in the argument, not above it.
12. **[1.41] Attribute an epigraph with author (last name if famous) and usually title, on the line below, sometimes after a dash — and stop there.** No notes, no page numbers.
13. **[1.40] Dedication: "To my parents," not "Dedicated to my parents."**
14. **[1.66] An appendix elaborates; it is "not a repository for odds and ends."** If it can't be justified as clarification or supporting document, cut it.
15. **[1.66] Number or letter multiple appendixes *and* give each a title.**
16. **[1.68] Glossary entries: alphabetical, one per line, term then definition** (period, colon, or em dash), before the notes and bibliography.
17. **[1.69 / 1.70] Order back matter: appendixes → notes → bibliography → contributors → credits → index.** Notes are headed "Notes"; a per-chapter note subhead identifies the chapter.
18. **[1.71] Contributor lists: alphabetical by surname, not inverted** ("Aiden A. Author").
19. **[1.42] A contents page lists what follows it, with starting page numbers; part titles get no page numbers; subheads appear only if they are genuinely useful signposts.**
20. **[1.13] A heading must make sense lifted out of context** — subheads become running heads and TOC entries.
21. **[1.64] Extra-textual breaks get a mark (asterisk, rule, ornament), not a heading**, and the paragraph after begins flush left.
22. **[1.102] An abstract is self-contained:** a few hundred words, no citations, no figure callouts, no "see below."
23. **[1.85] Keywords should anticipate the reader's search terms** — repeat key terms and add synonyms.
24. **[1.126] Consistency of style is invisible when it works and lets the substance show** — "the substantive contribution… stands out more sharply when typographical distractions are at a minimum."
25. **[1.75 / 1.107 / 1.108] Details must agree everywhere** — bios consistent across cover and back matter; use full names, not initials.

### From ch03

1. **[3.9]** Cite figures by number, never by position: "as figure 1 shows," not "the chart on this page" or "the photograph opposite."
2. **[3.9]** *figure* is lowercase roman in text and spelled out except in parentheses, where ***fig.*** is allowed; *plate* is never abbreviated.
3. **[3.51]** Cite tables by number, lowercase in text ("see table 5.3"); number each table separately (tables 14, 15, 16 — not 14A–C).
4. **[3.21]** Captions take **sentence case**; a caption that is only a phrase gets **no end punctuation**; once full sentences appear, **every sentence including the opening phrase takes terminal punctuation**.
5. **[3.21]** Keep the caption/legend/key/label distinction: caption outside, key and labels inside the illustration (3.7).
6. **[3.22]** Titles of works inside a caption follow chapter 8 (title case, italics/quotation marks); **generic titles stay lowercase**.
7. **[3.23]** Put a **period after the figure/table number**, then the caption ("Figure 3. Detailed stratigraphy . . .").
8. **[3.24]** Italicize locators (*top, bottom, left to right, inset*) inside captions; comma or colon after a leading locator, parentheses mid-sentence.
9. **[3.25]** Identify every symbol or fill pattern in the caption or a key; italicize part letters (A, B) in captions but not in text references (3.12), and don't italicize letters naming study groups.
10. **[3.28]** Write alt text for what the image **shows**, distinct from the caption's "what it is"; ≤250 characters, ordinary punctuation, no markup, none for decorative images.
11. **[3.29]** Never present math as an image without alt text restating it in words and numerals.
12. **[3.31–3.38]** Credit lines go at the **end of the caption**, often in parentheses, and must name author, title, publisher/collection, and page/figure; use "courtesy of" for free material, name the CC license for licensed material, note AI generation.
13. **[3.48]** Keep a table understandable on its own for a reader unfamiliar with the material — and **do not duplicate its details in the text**; summarize the message.
14. **[3.55]** Table titles: **above** the table, succinct, noun form, participles over relative clauses, **no interpretation** of the data; sentence case (or title case) consistently.
15. **[3.45]** A chart's title is part of the caption **below** the figure, in sentence case; internal labels are lowercase except proper nouns.
16. **[3.56]** Parenthetical statistics in a table title are **sentence case even in a title-case title**.
17. **[3.57]** Column heads: brief, sentence case, with **units/symbols in parentheses after the head** ("Growth (%)"); explain dual values in head or note ("27.3 (6/22)").
18. **[3.60]** Stub entries: sentence case, no end punctuation (except questions), and **parallel in syntax** ("Authors, Publishers, Printers").
19. **[3.63]** No ditto marks in tables; define every nonstandard abbreviation in a note.
20. **[3.68]** Mark missing data deliberately: blank = not applicable, em dash or ellipsis = no data, **zero = zero**.
21. **[3.77–3.80]** Table notes in fixed order — **Source/Sources** (italic + colon), **Note** (italic + colon), specific notes (superscript *a*, *1*, or \*, left-to-right/top-to-bottom), significance notes last — never a period after the marker.
22. **[3.83]** Use *percent* adverbially/adjectivally and *percentage* as the noun; **%** may replace either.
23. **[3.84]** Make number ranges exhaustive and non-overlapping, and use **> / < strictly** (>60 means 61 and up).
24. **[3.86]** *N* = total group, *n* = subgroup (italic).
25. **[3.50, 3.43, 3.82]** Enforce one style across all tables and charts in a document — titles, heads, abbreviations, symbols — and check each visual against the prose for consistency and redundancy.

## OVERVIEW (rules 1.1–1.2)

- **[1.1] Treat the book as the default architecture for any long document** — "Given their relative length and complexity, books can serve as a useful model for most other types of published documents" (1.1). Journal articles are "a lot like chapters in books" (1.1). Recommendations are pitched to academic *and* trade/fiction writers, and to self-publishers as well as traditional ones.
- **[1.2] Structure is format-independent** — print, ebook, and audiobook change what the *reader* can see, not how the *work* is divided. "The principles that apply regardless of medium or format remain the focus of this chapter" (1.2). Practical consequence: decide your divisions from the argument, then let the medium adapt.

## THE PARTS OF A BOOK — Introduction (rules 1.3–1.4)

- **[1.3] Divide any book into three divisions: front matter (prelims), text, back matter (end matter)** — and let each division do only its own job (1.3):
  - *Back matter* "acknowledges debts to the work of others and presents sources or source notes, appendixes, and other types of documentation supporting the text but outside its central focus or narrative" (1.3).
  - *Front matter* presents title/publisher/copyright, provides navigation, and "briefly introduces the book and sets its tone" (1.3).
  - Scholarly books carry more components than novels, "but the basic structure is the same across genres" (1.3).
- **[1.4] Follow the traditional order of parts; use lowercase roman numerals for front matter, arabic for everything else** (1.4). "**Few books contain all these elements**, and some books have components not listed here" (1.4) — the list is a menu and an order, not a checklist. Starting pages that cannot be numbered until page makeup begin on the first available recto. Ebooks "may omit some of these elements (e.g., the half title) and may relegate some of the front matter (e.g., the copyright page) to the back, but they typically retain the order for the main text" (1.4).

  **Front matter (roman)**
  - Praise page(s) — i (but see 1.17)
  - Book half title — i (if no praise pages)
  - Series title, other works, frontispiece, or blank — ii
  - Title page — iii
  - Copyright page — iv
  - Dedication — v
  - Epigraph — v or vi
  - (Table of) Contents — v or vii
  - (List of) Illustrations / (List of) Tables — recto or verso
  - Foreword — recto
  - Preface — recto
  - Acknowledgments (if not in preface or back matter) — recto
  - Introduction (if not part of text) — recto
  - Abbreviations (if not in back matter) — recto or verso
  - Chronology (if not in back matter) — recto

  **Text (arabic, from 1)**
  - First text page (introduction or chapter 1) — 1
  - *or* second half title / first part title — 1; blank — 2; first text page — 3
  - Subsequent chapters — recto or verso
  - Conclusion — recto or verso
  - Epilogue or afterword — recto or verso

  **Back matter (arabic continues)**
  - Acknowledgments — recto
  - Appendix (or first appendix) — recto; subsequent appendixes — recto or verso
  - Chronology (if not in front matter) — recto
  - (List of) Abbreviations (if not in front matter) — recto
  - Glossary — recto
  - Notes (if not footnotes or chapter endnotes) — recto
  - Bibliography or references — recto
  - (List of) Contributors — recto
  - Illustration credits (if not in captions or elsewhere) — recto
  - Index(es) — recto
  - Reading group guide — recto
  - About the author (if not on back cover or elsewhere) — recto
  - Colophon (production details) — recto or verso

## Pages and Page Numbers (rules 1.5–1.9)

- **[1.5] Know leaf / recto / verso if you refer to a page by number in your own prose** — a *leaf* is the physical sheet, a *page* is one side of it; the recto (front, right-hand when open) is always odd-numbered, the verso always even (1.5). Ebooks do not distinguish recto from verso (1.5).
- **[1.6] Expect pagination to count pages that do not display a number** — every page except endpapers (and trailing blanks) counts (1.6). The displayed number is the *folio*; at the foot of the page it is a *drop folio* (1.6). Ebooks with reflowable text have no fixed pages, only location data (1.6; 14.59).
- **[1.7] Why front matter takes roman numerals** — "This traditional practice prevents renumbering the remainder of a book when, for example, a dedication page or a foreword is added at the last moment" (1.7). No folio appears on blank or "display" pages (half title, title, copyright, dedication, epigraph); a drop folio or no folio is used on the opening page of each later front-matter section (1.7).
- **[1.8] Arabic page 1 is the first page of the text** — if a second half title or part title precedes it, that page is 1, its verso 2, and the first visible numeral is the drop folio 3 (1.8). Arabic numbering continues through the back matter (1.8).
- **[1.9] Multivolume works: choose consecutive or separate pagination by the index** — consecutive pagination saves index entries from carrying volume numbers; separate pagination suits very long works; front matter restarts at i in every volume (1.9). [Mostly production detail.]

## Running Heads (rules 1.10–1.16)

- **[1.10–1.11] Running heads are navigational signposts, and chapter/section openers never carry them** — running heads may be dropped entirely in novels and books of poems (1.10); "Running heads are never used on display pages (half title, title, copyright, dedication, epigraph) or on the first page of the table of contents, preface, and so forth" (1.11). A front-matter element running more than one page carries the same head on verso and recto (1.11).
- **[1.12] Let running heads encode your hierarchy** — acceptable scholarly arrangements, verso / recto (1.12): Part title / Chapter title; Chapter number / Chapter title; Chapter title / Chapter title; Chapter title / Chapter subtitle; Chapter title / Subhead; Subhead / Subhead; Chapter author / Chapter title. Principle: "the more important entity (starting with the name of the author) goes on the verso and an equal or lesser entity on the recto" (1.12). Creative genres commonly run book title / chapter title or book title / story or poem title (1.12).
- **[1.13] Choose subheads that survive being lifted out of context** — when subheads serve as running heads, "the last one on the page is used as the running head" on rectos and "the first subhead on the page" on versos (1.13). A subhead written as "This" or "Further Considerations" makes an unreadable signpost.
- **[1.14] Back-matter running heads must differentiate like items** — "Appendix" verso, appendix title recto; with more than one index, heads must distinguish them (e.g., "Index of Names," "Index of Subjects") (1.14).
- **[1.15] Endnote running heads must locate the reader in the text** — give inclusive page numbers ("Notes to Pages 2–10") or chapter ("Notes to Chapter One"); if chapter numbers are used, the text running heads must carry chapter numbers too (1.15; 13.50).
- **[1.16] Omit running heads on part titles, chapter openings, and illustration-only or table-only pages** — but keep them on pages that mix text with an illustration or table, and in long sequences of figures (1.16).

## Front Matter (rules 1.17–1.51)

### TITLE PAGES (rules 1.17–1.21)

- **[1.17] Praise/blurb pages, if used, precede the half title on page i (folio omitted)** — half title then begins on the next available recto (1.17). Praise may also live on the back cover (1.17; 1.79).
- **[1.18] Half title = main title only** — "normally consists only of the main title (less any subtitle)... All other information—including author name, publisher, and edition—is omitted" (1.18). No folio.
- **[1.19] Page ii carries series information or is blank** — series title and volume number, general editor, sometimes other titles in the series; a long list may continue at the end of the back matter (1.19).
- **[1.20] Frontispiece or two-page title, if any, uses page ii/iii** — the frontispiece traditionally faces the title page (1.20).
- **[1.21] Title page carries exactly five kinds of information** — full title; subtitle; author(s)/editor(s) ("Edited by")/translator(s) ("Translated by," or "Translated from the French by"); edition number for a new edition; publisher name and city (1.21). Further rules:
  - Titles are normally title case on the title page (8.160), but designers may set all caps or lowercase and may drop the colon between title and subtitle if differentiated by type (1.21). Metadata is entered in title case regardless (1.21; 1.84).
  - "The author's name should appear in the form preferred by the author or by which the author is generally known"; Chicago "usually omits any academic degrees or affiliations" (1.21).

### COPYRIGHT PAGE (rules 1.22–1.39)

- **[1.22–1.24] Copyright page (p. iv): notice, rights statement, publication data** — no notice is legally required since 1989, but publishers carry one "to discourage infringement" (1.22). Notice = three parts: **© + year of first publication + name of copyright owner**, then "All rights reserved" (1.24). MECHANICAL. The publisher's (and any copublisher's) address also normally appears here, possibly abbreviated to a city and postal code, with the press's URL (1.23); self-publishers may add contact information for reader correspondence (1.23).
  - ex: "© 2023 by The University of Chicago" / "© 2024 by Alison A. Author" (1.24); reprint form "© 1963 by Maurice Sendak. © renewed 1991 by Maurice Sendak." (1.26)
  - "The year of publication should correspond to the copyright date"; if a book appears at the very end of a year but is published the next, "the later date is preferred" (1.24).
- **[1.25–1.28] Edition vs impression vs printing** — a substantially new edition gets a new copyright date; a new *impression*, reprint, or unrevised paperback/ebook does not (1.25). Publishing history follows the notice, period-separated or on separate lines (1.27). MECHANICAL (form).
  - ex: "First edition published 1906. Eighteenth edition 2024." / "Revised edition originally published 2010" / "University of Chicago Press edition 2019" (1.27)
  - **[1.28] the 20-percent test**: to call itself a new edition "a significant portion should consist of new or revised material—typically at least 20 percent, but sometimes less." Text unchanged except for typo corrections = "new impression or a reissue"; with a new preface or afterword only, "enlarged or expanded edition," not a new edition (1.28). Reissues after rights revert, or a self-published book picked up by a trade publisher, are not new editions without significant change (1.28).
- **[1.29–1.32] Printing/versioning detail** — country of printing (1.29), impression lines and their digit conventions (1.30), version numbers and date stamps for print-on-demand and ebooks (1.31), original-language title/publisher/copyright for translations (1.32). Not writing-relevant beyond supplying accurate data.
- **[1.33–1.35] Credits, grants, disclaimers** — the copyright page may carry permissions and illustration credits if they appear nowhere else (1.33); grants are acknowledged there, with wording and logo as the grantor specifies (1.34); disclaimers ("persons and events are fictional"; "not a substitute for medical or legal advice") and content/trigger warnings may be added, and "any such notice should be worded to avoid spoilers" (1.35). **Note:** CMOS says such disclaimers "offer no legal protection" (1.35).
- **[1.36–1.39] Identifiers and standards** — one ISBN per format (1.36); DOI listed on the copyright page and "Crossref recommends always presenting the DOI as a link" (1.37); CIP/LCCN data (1.38); the paper-durability statement (1.39). Not writing-relevant; supply accurate data to the publisher.
- **Newer-material flag:** land acknowledgments (1.22) and accessible cover descriptions (1.51) are recent additions to the front-matter inventory.

### DEDICATION AND EPIGRAPH (rules 1.40–1.41)

- **[1.40] Dedication: use "To" or "For," not "Dedicated to"** — "the word dedicated is superfluous; a simple 'to' or 'for' is usually sufficient (e.g., 'To my parents')" (1.40). JUDGMENT (it is the author's call whether to have one). Placement: alone on page v, facing the copyright page; it may share page v with an epigraph only in the less common case (1.40–1.41). Editors of contributed volumes do not add a dedication unless jointly offered; translators do not add their own unless it is clear it is not the original author's (1.40).
- **[1.41] Epigraph = "a quotation that is pertinent but not integral to the text"** — that definition is the test for whether a quotation is an epigraph or part of the argument (1.41). Rules:
  - Location: page v if there is no dedication, otherwise usually page vi opposite the contents; some publishers put it just before the main text (1.41); it may sit under the dedication if space is tight (1.41); epigraphs are "also occasionally used at chapter openings and, more rarely, at the beginnings of sections within chapters" (1.41).
  - Length: "Some book epigraphs consist of more than one quotation, but they are usually limited to one page" (1.41).
  - Attribution: the source goes "on a line following the quotation, sometimes preceded by a dash" (12.35); "Only the author's name (in the case of a well-known author, only the last name) and, usually, the title of the work need appear; beyond this, it is customary not to annotate book epigraphs" (1.41; but see 13.55). No page numbers, no notes, usually.
  - Permissions: "some quotations may require permission before they can be used as epigraphs" (4.91).
  - Related: a chapter epigraph is "sometimes considered part of the chapter display"; it may carry a note reference, "though traditionalists may prefer an unnumbered note" (1.55; 13.55).

### TABLE OF CONTENTS AND LIST OF ILLUSTRATIONS OR TABLES (rules 1.42–1.44)

- **[1.42] Contents: list everything that follows it, nothing that precedes it** — "It should include all preliminary material that follows it but exclude anything that precedes it. It should list the title and beginning page number of each section of the book: front matter, text divisions, and back matter, including the index" (1.42). Further:
  - Part titles appear in the contents **without page numbers** unless the parts carry their own introductions (1.42).
  - "Subheads within chapters are usually omitted from the table of contents, but if they provide valuable signposts for readers, they may be included (as in the print edition of this manual)" (1.42).
  - In a multiauthor volume, list each chapter's author with its title (1.42).
  - Illustration galleries are not itemized; note their location at the end (e.g., "Illustrations follow pages 130 and 288") (1.42).
  - For novels, children's books, and other creative works, naming and numbering parts/chapters/sections — and whether to have a contents page at all — "is a creative decision that is usually the responsibility of the author" (1.42).
- **[1.43] Ebooks require a contents list; audiobook narration usually skips it** — a contents list is "a required navigational element for the EPUB standard" and appears as a menu item; ebook contents typically also list cover, title page, copyright, dedication, author bio (1.43).
- **[1.44] Lists of illustrations/tables are optional** — skip them when there are very few items or very many tied closely to the text; multiauthor volumes and proceedings usually omit them (1.44). When used, title the list "Illustrations" but enter it in the contents as "List of Illustrations" "to avoid ambiguity," and match the contents page in type size and style (1.44). Group under subheads (Figures, Tables, Plates, Drawings, Maps) when varied (1.44; fig. 1.6). Unpaginated plate galleries are listed as "Following page 000" (1.44).

### FOREWORD, PREFACE AND ACKNOWLEDGMENTS, AND INTRODUCTION (rules 1.45–1.48)

- **[1.45] Foreword = someone else's opening remarks** — "The term foreword should be reserved for prefatory remarks by someone other than the author—including those of an editor or compiler, especially if a work already includes an author's preface" (1.45). Set in text type; "normally runs only a few pages"; the author's name usually appears **at the end**, unless the piece is substantial, in which case it may go at the beginning (1.45). The publisher may flag it on the title page ("With a foreword by Imani Perry") (1.45).
- **[1.46] Preface = the author's own statement about the work** — set in text type; contents: "reasons for undertaking the work, method of research (if this has some bearing on readers' understanding of the text), brief acknowledgments..., and sometimes permissions granted for the use of previously published material" (1.46). "A preface need not be signed," but if there is doubt about authorship or the author wishes to sign (sometimes with initials), the signature goes at the end (1.46).
  - New editions: a new preface **precedes** the original; the original is retitled *Preface to the First Edition* and the new one *Preface to the Second Edition*, *Preface to the Paperback Edition*, *Preface 2024*, etc. (1.46).
  - Editor's preface comes first and is often retitled *Editor's Foreword*, bearing the editor's name at the end (1.46).
- **[1.47] Acknowledgments: long ones get their own section; a preface that is only thanks is retitled** — "If the author's acknowledgments are long, they may be put in a separate section following the preface; if a preface consists only of acknowledgments, its title should be changed to 'Acknowledgments'" (1.47). Front-matter placement makes sense "when it is important to acknowledge up front the contributions of people beyond those listed on the title page" (edited volumes, corporate authors) — "For most books, however, the acknowledgments are better placed at the back of the book, preceding other back matter—now the preferred placement for most University of Chicago Press books" (1.47; see 1.65). A multivolume work's acknowledgments may appear in volume 1 only (1.47).
- **[1.48] "Introductions as such rarely belong in the front matter"** — the decision rule (1.48):
  - Short, author-written, about the book's contents or origins → call it a **preface**.
  - Substantial and by the author, or integral to the text → put it at the **beginning of the text**, paginated in arabic.
  - Substantial but by someone else (e.g., a new introduction to a reprint) → front matter, just before the main text; "if it is not more than three to five pages, it may more appropriately be called a foreword" and placed before any preface (1.48).

### OTHER FRONT MATTER (rules 1.49–1.51)

- **[1.49] Lists of abbreviations: alphabetize by the abbreviation, and never as a substitute for first-use expansion** — "In the list, alphabetize terms by the abbreviation, not by the spelled-out form" (1.49). "A list of abbreviations is generally not a substitute for using the full form of a term at its first occurrence in the text" (1.49; 10.3). Include a list only if many abbreviations are used, or a few are used frequently; its location must be in the contents (1.49). If abbreviations occur only in the back matter, the list goes before the first such element (appendixes, endnotes, or bibliography) (1.49; 13.64). MECHANICAL (alphabetization by abbreviation).
- **[1.50] Publisher's, translator's, and editor's notes** — a translator's note, "like a foreword, should precede any element, such as a preface, that is by the original author" (1.50); a publisher's note is rare and sits before or immediately after the contents; an editor's statement of method may go last in the front matter or as an appendix, but "brief remarks about editorial method... are often better incorporated into an editor's preface" (1.50).
- **[1.51] Accessible cover descriptions** — add a description of the cover ("About the Cover" or on the copyright page) for readers with print disabilities; it should describe images and "any significant typographic features used for the title and other text"; purely ornamental or all-text covers may not need one (1.51; 3.28).

## Text (rules 1.52–1.53)

- **[1.52] Where arabic 1 falls** — the first page of chapter 1 or of an introduction is normally page 1; when front matter is extensive, a **second half title** identical to page i may be inserted, counted as page 1, usually with a blank or illustrated/epigraph verso (1.52). A book opening with a part title treats the part title as page 1 the same way (1.52).
- **[1.53] A text introduction belongs to the argument, not to the apparatus** — "a text introduction is integral to the subject matter of the book and should not include acknowledgments or other material that belongs in the front or back matter" (1.53). Referring to the book's contents is acceptable ("In the first two chapters I discuss . . ."), "though some authors and editors may prefer to limit such information to a preface" (1.53). If titled simply *Introduction* it normally carries no chapter number and is considerably shorter than a chapter (1.53).
  > "Authors should consider adding a descriptive subtitle even to such shorter introductions, and an author who has titled chapter 1 Introduction should be encouraged to give the chapter a more evocative title." (1.53)

## TEXT DIVISIONS (rules 1.54–1.60)

- **[1.54] Parts: number them, title them, and keep chapter numbering continuous** — "Each part usually carries a number and a title and should contain at least two chapters (an exception may be made for a part that includes only an introductory or concluding chapter). Chapters are numbered consecutively throughout the book; they do not begin with 1 in each part" (1.54). Terminology: "Parts are sometimes called sections, though section is more commonly used for a subdivision within a chapter" (1.54) — so *section* means something inside a chapter. A part title without an introduction begins recto, followed by a blank verso and a recto chapter opening; a part introduction may sit on a new recto, on the part title's verso, or on the part title itself (1.54). A book-level text introduction precedes the part title to part 1 and needs no title of its own; a conclusion needs none but should begin recto so it does not seem to belong to the last part (1.54). No part title before the back matter, though one may be useful before a run of appendixes or a notes section (1.54).
- **[1.55] Chapters: short, descriptive titles; the chapter display is number + title + subtitle** — one of the chapter's clearest writing principles:
  > "Authors should aim for short, descriptive titles, which tend to give readers a better overview of a book's contents than longer, more whimsical titles." (1.55)

  Fiction "may choose more creative titles—or no titles at all" (1.55). Each chapter starts a new page; its opening carries a drop folio or none, and no running head (1.55; 1.10–1.16). "Chapter is often omitted" from the display (1.55). "Note reference numbers or symbols traditionally do not appear anywhere in the chapter display"; a note about the chapter as a whole stays unnumbered and precedes the numbered notes (1.55; 13.55).
- **[1.56] Multiauthor books: name at the chapter head, affiliation in an unnumbered footnote** — the same unnumbered note may disclose that the chapter is reprinted or was previously published; when both appear, "it is customary, but not essential, that the affiliation come first" (1.56; 13.59).
- **[1.57] Poetry: one poem per page in a book of new poems; part titles on rectos** — in a collection of previously published poems, poems may share a page (1.57).
- **[1.58] Letters and diaries: chronological order, headings instead of chapters** — dates may serve as headings for diary entries, senders/recipients for correspondence; these headings "do not usually begin a new page" (1.58).
- **[1.59] Conclusion: say what the study implies and what remains open** — it "typically makes some final statement about the subject presented and the implications of the study or poses questions inviting further investigation" (1.59). If it grows to chapter size, with or without a number, "authors should consider using a more descriptive title" (1.59) — never a bare *Conclusion* when it does real work.
- **[1.60] Epilogue/afterword vs. postscript** — an epilogue or afterword is "a comparatively brief section that comments on the text, sometimes obliquely, or brings a fiction or nonfiction narrative up to date"; when added to a new edition and written by a different author, it is "usually called an afterword" (1.60; cf. 1.45). "An epilogue or an afterword is sometimes called a postscript, a term best reserved for comments by the book's author" (1.60). It follows any conclusion and (in a book with parts, or when added to a new edition) begins recto (1.60).

## TEXT SUBDIVISIONS (rules 1.61–1.64) — the heading-hierarchy core

- **[1.61] Subheads: short, meaningful, and parallel — and never syntactically continuous with the sentence below them.** This is the highest-value rule in the chapter for prose writers.
  > "Subheads within a chapter should be short and meaningful and, like chapter titles, parallel in structure and tone. Well-organized subheads help readers navigate the text, making it more accessible. The first sentence of text following a subhead should not refer syntactically to the subhead; words should be repeated where necessary." (1.61)

  - ex: `SECONDARY SPONGIOSA / The secondary spongiosa, a vaulted structure...` — **not** `SECONDARY SPONGIOSA / This vaulted structure...` (1.61)
  - *Parallelism* is explicit: sibling subheads must match in grammatical form and register, the way sibling chapter titles should.
- **[1.62] Use as few heading levels as the argument needs; three is the ceiling for almost everything.**
  > "Many works require only one level of subhead throughout the text. Some, particularly scientific or technical works, require further subdivision... Only the most complicated works need more than three levels." (1.62)

  Levels are named A-level (the first-level heading after the chapter title), B-level, C-level (or A-head, B-head, C-head), and "the number of subhead levels required may vary from chapter to chapter" (1.62). Other rules:
  - A lower-level head may follow an upper-level head with no intervening text (a "stacked head"), but "when a section of text is subdivided, there should ideally be at least two subsections" — two or more A-level heads in a chapter, two or more B-levels under an A-level (1.62). A single subdivision is acceptable "to emphasize a unique case or a special consideration" (1.62).
  - Subheads "usually do not need to begin on a new page," are set on their own line, and are "differentiated by type style and placement" (1.62; 2.22, 2.65) — i.e., legibility of hierarchy comes from a consistent visual ladder, not from text.
  - The lowest level may be **run-in**: italic or bold at the head of a paragraph, followed by a period, and "usually capitalized in sentence case" (1.62; 8.159). MECHANICAL (run-in = italic/bold + period + sentence case).
- **[1.63] Do not number subheads unless you cross-reference them** — "Unless sections in a chapter are cited in cross-references elsewhere in the text, numbers are usually unnecessary with subheads. In general, subheads are more useful to a reader than section numbers alone" (1.63). Scientific/technical work may use multilevel numeration (4.8 = eighth section of chapter 4; 4.12.3 = third subsection of the twelfth section of chapter 4), with numbers separated by periods, colons, or hyphens (1.63).
  - ex: footnote to 1.63 — "Multiple numeration using periods should not be confused with decimal fractions. Paragraph or section 4.9 may be followed by 4.10—quite unlike the decimal fraction system." (1.63n1)
- **[1.64] A break stronger than a paragraph but weaker than a subhead gets a typographic mark, not a heading** — asterisks, a rule, a type ornament (e.g., a fleuron), or a blank line, with the following paragraph typically flush left (1.64). "Whatever the book design ultimately calls for, authors should indicate such breaks with asterisks" (1.64; 2.10). A blank line "may be missed if the break falls at the bottom of a page," so either set the first few words after a break in SMALL CAPITALS or insert the asterisk only where the break coincides with a page break (print only) — in reflowable ebooks "all breaks must be visibly marked" and coded accessibly (e.g., `hr` + CSS) (1.64).

## Back Matter (rules 1.65–1.77)

- **[1.65] Acknowledgments now go at the back for most books** — "Chicago now prefers to place them in the back matter for most types of books. This placement is especially advantageous for novels and other books that depend on establishing and maintaining a certain tone that may be very different from the tone used for the acknowledgments" (1.65). Consequence: "any remarks intended to be read before the beginning of the book should be moved into a preface or a note on the text" (1.65). *Flagged as a change in Chicago's preference* — front-matter placement survives for edited volumes and organizational authors (1.47).
- **[1.66] An appendix holds elaboration, not leftovers.**
  > "An appendix may include explanations and elaborations that are not essential parts of the text but are helpful to a reader seeking further clarification, texts of documents, long lists, survey questionnaires, or sometimes even charts or tables. The appendix should not, however, be a repository for odds and ends that the author could not work into the text." (1.66)

  Appendixes follow the last chapter, though one may end a chapter (introduced by an A-level subhead) if it is essential to understanding that chapter; in multiauthor or separately sold chapters it must follow its own chapter (1.66). With two or more, "they should be designated by numbers (Appendix 1, Appendix 2, etc.) or letters (Appendix A, Appendix B, etc.), and each should be given a title as well" (1.66). MECHANICAL (Appendix 1 / Appendix A labeling). Unwieldy data may instead be posted on the publisher's website (1.66).
- **[1.67] Chronology or genealogy: front matter if the narrative depends on it, back matter otherwise** — as its own headed section (1.67; fig. 1.9).
- **[1.68] Glossary: alphabetical, one term per line, followed by its definition** — the term "may be followed by a period, a colon, or an em dash, or distinguished from the definition typographically, or both" (1.68). A glossary "usually precedes the notes and bibliography or reference list but may follow the notes, especially if terms listed in the glossary appear in the notes" (1.68). "A glossary that consists mainly of terms that do not appear in the text may be included as an appendix" (1.68). MECHANICAL (alphabetical, one per line).
- **[1.69] Notes (endnotes): after the appendixes, before the bibliography** — "Endnotes, generally under the title 'Notes,' follow any appendix material and precede the bibliography or reference list (if there is one)" (1.69). Notes to each chapter get a subhead giving the chapter number and often the chapter title (1.69); running heads identify the pages or chapters covered (1.15). Endnotes are "set smaller than the text but larger than footnotes" (1.69). Chapter endnotes are *required* in books whose chapters might be sold individually (1.69; 13.46).
- **[1.70] Bibliography/reference list: after the notes, before the index, in flush-and-hang style** — set smaller than the text (1.70; see chs. 13–14 for form; 13.68 kinds of bibliography; 13.111 reference lists).
- **[1.71] List of contributors: alphabetical by last name, not inverted** — "Names are arranged alphabetically by last name but not inverted ('Aiden A. Author,' not 'Author, Aiden A.')" (1.71). Preferred location: back matter, immediately before the index; brief bio, affiliation, contact info (1.71; fig. 1.10). Not needed for a handful of authors listed on the title page (1.71).
- **[1.72] Illustration credits: next to the illustration if possible, otherwise a list before the index** — placement may be stipulated by the rights holder (1.72; 3.30–38).
- **[1.73] Index: begins recto; name index precedes subject index** — set in two columns and smaller type in print (1.73; ch. 15 for indexing).
- **[1.74] Reading group guide: discussion topics, usually questions, just before the author note** (1.74).
- **[1.75] Biographical note: previous publications plus affiliation, and consistent everywhere** — may live in the back matter (usually last), front matter, or back cover/jacket flap; "If such biographical information appears in more than one place, the details, if not the wording, must be consistent" (1.75).
- **[1.76] Colophon = the facts of production, on the last page** (1.76). *Two senses:* production note (1.76) vs. a publisher's emblem/logo on a spine or cover (1.78) — CMOS flags both.
- **[1.77] Errata page: columns Page / For / Read** — for errors "severe enough to cause misunderstanding" in already-printed copies; place at the end of the front matter or the end of the book and list it in the contents (1.77). Today errata more often go online (1.77). MECHANICAL (table form).

## Covers and Jackets (rules 1.78–1.83)

- **[1.78–1.83] Cover, spine, jacket, endpaper, and barcode detail** — not writing-relevant except: spine carries author name (last name if tight), title, edition, publisher, **subtitle usually omitted** (1.78); paperback front cover carries author, title, and usually subtitle, plus translator/foreword contributor/edition (1.79); back cover carries description, review quotes or blurbs, bio, series (1.79); jacket flaps carry what the back cover cannot (1.80); endpapers are not paginated (1.81); cover-art credits go on the back flap or back cover, or on the copyright page for paperbacks (1.82); ISBN plus barcode at the foot of the back cover (1.83).

## Metadata, Abstracts, and Keywords (rules 1.84–1.85)

- **[1.84] Book metadata is a listed set of fields entered once and kept accurate** — ISBN/identifier, format, title and subtitle, edition, series and number, publisher, pub date, contributors, bios, extent, illustrations, subject/genre, description, blurbs/reviews, price, territory, audience, CC license; coded via ONIX/Thema/BISAC (1.84). Because metadata drives how the book appears in search and catalogs, "it is important to enter it accurately and update it as needed" (1.84).
- **[1.85] Book abstracts and keywords: a few hundred words, for discoverability** — abstracts and keywords, "long a feature of journal articles, are now routinely required by publishers of academic books"; an abstract "is a brief summary of a book's content, usually limited to a few hundred words or less," and publishers may also require a summary of each chapter (1.85). A keyword "is a word or phrase that identifies an important concept or name in the book," required per chapter as well as per book (1.85). Fiction and other nonacademic genres get a *description* instead (1.85). See 2.29, 2.30.

## THE PARTS OF A JOURNAL (rules 1.86–1.126) — compressed

Journal architecture mirrors the book; only the prose-craft rules are expanded here.

- **[1.86–1.92] Formats, identifiers, pagination, running heads** — print/online/online-only models (1.86); electronic-only supplementary data must be flagged in the print version (1.87); ISSN per format, article DOI as `https://doi.org/…` link (1.88); volume/issue conventions, issues restarting at 1, season names capitalized (1.89); page numbers run continuously across a volume, issues begin recto and end on an even page (1.90); continuous-publishing article IDs with per-article pagination from page 1 (1.91); journal running heads carry journal title, author surname (or "Lee et al."), and shortened article title or section name (1.92).
- **[1.93–1.100] Covers, front matter, home page, contents, contributor info, errata, retractions** — cover 1–4 contents and the masthead (1.93); prelims (1.94); home-page resources (1.95); the issue contents must give journal title, date, volume/issue, and for each article title, authors, and page range (1.96); information for contributors and editorial policy (1.97); announcements and calls for papers (1.98); errata, with links both ways for online articles and "a note indicating the nature of the changes and when they were made" (1.99); retractions carrying full article and author data, a two-to-three-sentence explanation, and cross-links, with "RETRACTED ARTICLE" at the top of HTML and as a PDF watermark (1.100).
- **[1.101] Article metadata** — journal title/ISSN, volume, issue, authors and affiliations (with ORCID/ROR/Ringgold IDs), article title, page range or article ID, publication date, DOI; shared as JATS XML with indexing services (1.101).
- **[1.102] Abstracts and keywords: short, self-contained, no internal pointers.** "Most journals in the sciences and increasingly in the humanities publish abstracts along with their articles. These summaries, typically limited to a few hundred words or less, are usually supplied by the author and appear at the beginning of an article" (1.102). Because abstracts are served as stand-alone items, "they normally do not contain cited references or figure callouts or any other direct links to the article itself" (1.102) — **write the abstract as a freestanding text.** Some journals mandate structured abstracts (1.102). Keywords are "words or phrases that are intended to facilitate indexing"; they "normally repeat key terms found in the title, abstract, and text," but "synonyms or other variations may be added to anticipate search terms," or drawn from a controlled vocabulary such as MeSH (1.102).
- **[1.103] Article vs. other components** — abstracts are part of the article; journals also run brief reports, letters, book reviews, book notes, announcements, calls for papers, errata, contributor notes (1.103).
- **[1.104] First-page furniture** — title, author(s), and the article's own copyright line at minimum; as needed also affiliation, conflicts of interest, correspondence address, submission/acceptance/online-publication dates, abstract and keywords, acknowledgment note, editor's note, and a section or subject heading above the title (e.g., *Original Article*, *Microbiology*) (1.104).
- **[1.105] Links** — internal links to tables, notes, references; external links to cited sources and supplementary material (1.105).
- **[1.106] Article subheads follow the book's rules** — "An article, like a chapter in a book, may be divided into sections and subsections headed by subheads, sub-subheads, and so on (see 1.61–64). The number of subhead levels required may vary from article to article" (1.106).
- **[1.107–1.109] Reviews, announcements, contributors** — review headings give author, title, date, publisher, page count including front matter, and price, plus series if any; the reviewer's name goes at the end (or after the heading, more often online) (1.107). Announcements (1.108). Contributor order is the lead author's call, usually fixed before submission (1.109).
  - **[1.108] Use full names for reviewers** — "reviewers may be listed by their initials, a traditional practice that this manual discourages in favor of using full names" (1.108). *Flagged as a CMOS preference change.*
- **[1.110–1.111] Letters and editorials** — letters are minor and irregular, but in some science journals a prominent regular feature with replies, equations, tables, figures (1.110); editorials announce and explain changes (new editor, policy, redesign) and are headed *Editorial* or *Introduction*, with the editor's name at the end (1.111).
- **[1.112–1.113] Copyright line and publication history** — each substantive article carries its own copyright line (or public-domain notice) at the foot of the first page; Chicago journals print issue info, then copyright notice, then DOI (1.112). For articles published online ahead of print, the online-publication date must appear in both versions, and submission/acceptance dates are often included: "These dates provide important context for interpretation of the article; in the sciences, especially, what is known—or at least what has been reported—can change rapidly" (1.113).
- **[1.114–1.116] Context, tables, illustrations** — preserve issue context around individual articles (1.114); online tables as HTML for accessibility, with links from text to table footnotes and back (1.115); illustrations may be thumbnails linked to high-resolution images, with color used freely online; "any differences between the content of the print and electronic versions should be noted explicitly in both formats" (1.116; 3.26).
- **[1.117] Documentation style is a journal's identifying mark: notes or author-date** — notes (often with a bibliography) prevail in humanities; endnotes appear at the end of the article "with the heading Notes"; author-date citations (parenthetical, keyed to a reference list) serve science and social science; some science journals use numbered references in citation order or alphabetical (1.117; chs. 13–14).
- **[1.118–1.120] Links and indexes** — citations link to notes/references and on to outside sources (1.118); volume indexes cover authors, article titles, and books reviewed; subject indexes are now rare (1.119); discovery has moved to databases such as PubMed, with controlled vocabulary (1.120).
- **[1.121–1.124] Version control** — online is normally the version of record, so "it is extremely important to document any changes to the file after the online publication date" (1.121); preprints (arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv, PsyArXiv, etc.) are not the final edited article (1.122); "ahead of print" articles carry their own dates and placeholder folios (e.g., 000) until assigned to an issue (1.123); supplementary data needs a DOI and must be listed in print while marked online-only (1.124).
- **[1.125–1.126] Design and editorial style** — a journal's design is built for *categories* of text (article title, author, text, heads, subheads), so it must be "simple and flexible as well as visually pleasing and easy to read" (1.125). Editorial style "governs such things as when to use numerals or percent signs, how to treat abbreviations or special terms, and how to organize tables" (1.126).
  > "Consistency of design and style contributes to a journal's identity; readers know what to expect, and the substantive contribution of each article stands out more sharply when typographical distractions are at a minimum." (1.126)

- **[3.1] Illustrations = figures** — Call images presented separately from the run of text *illustrations* or *figures* (also "art"/"artwork"): paintings, photographs, line drawings, maps, charts, musical examples, multimedia. [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.2] Tables defined** — A table is a list presented as rows and columns, set apart from the run of text. Because it is alphanumeric text, it should be typeset as **text** (searchable/accessible), not delivered as an image. [MECHANICAL when checking for table-as-image]

## ILLUSTRATIONS

### Types of Illustrations and Their Parts (3.3–3.7)

- **[3.3] Continuous tone vs. halftone** — Production only: grayscale art → halftone (dot array); RGB screen images → CMYK for print. Writers: supply per publisher guidelines. [skipped detail]
- **[3.4] Line art** — Solid black on white, no gray screens (pen-and-ink drawings, software-generated charts, some musical examples). Shading conventions: see 3.19.
- **[3.5] Text figures vs. plates** — *Text figures* are illustrations interspersed in the text; a *plate* is a full-page illustration printed separately (often on coated paper), usually gathered into a gallery. A specialized illustration (a map, a musical example) may be referred to **by type** instead of "figure."
- **[3.6] Galleries** — A gallery/insert is a block of plates, often unpaginated, always starting recto. Production detail.
- **[3.7] Caption vs. legend vs. key vs. label** — Distinguish four parts: **caption** = explanatory text *outside* the illustration (usually below); **key** (sometimes called a legend) = identifies symbols *inside* the illustration (map/chart); **labels** = descriptive terms *within* the illustration, including symbol letters marking parts. CMOS collapses the old caption/legend distinction: "This manual uses caption to refer to both." [JUDGMENT]

### Placement and Numbering of Illustrations (3.8–3.14)

- **[3.8] Placement** — Put each illustration as soon as possible **after** its first text reference (electronic: a captioned thumbnail may follow the paragraph). In print it may precede the reference only if on the same page/spread or the text is too short. Placement **callouts** ("fig. 5 about here") are instructions to the typesetter and **never appear** in the published work — do not confuse them with text references. [MECHANICAL for stray callouts left in prose]
- **[3.9] Refer to illustrations by number, never by location** — With more than a handful of illustrations, number them and cite "as figure 1 shows . . ."; "compare figures 4 and 5." Never write "the photograph opposite" or "the graph on this page." In text, *figure* is **roman, lowercased, spelled out**, except in **parenthetical references**, where **fig.** is allowed ("(see fig. 10.7)"). *Plate* is **never** abbreviated to *pl.* (Some journals routinely capitalize these terms with a number; CMOS treats *figure*/*table* as lowercase in running text.) [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "as figure 1 shows . . ."; "compare figures 4 and 5"; "(see fig. 10.7)"
  - ex: print-only counterpart missing some figures → add "available online" after the reference (see also 1.87)
- **[3.10] Numbering sequences** — All illustrations may share one continuous sequence; a category may be numbered separately for reader convenience (*map 1, map 2*); illustrations in a different medium are **always** numbered separately (*video 1*). Print and electronic versions use the same numbers.
- **[3.11] Double numeration** — In scientific/technical, heavily illustrated, or multi-author books, number per chapter: **figure 9.6** = sixth figure in chapter 9. A chapter's only illustration is still *figure 10.1*. Appendix figures: A.1, A.2 (or A.1, B.1 . . .); introduction: I.1, I.2. CMOS itself uses double numeration for illustrations, tables, and text paragraphs. [JUDGMENT whether to adopt; MECHANICAL once adopted]
- **[3.12] Parts of a figure** — Use **Arabic numerals** for all illustrations ("figure 12," "fig. 10.7"). Parts of a composite may be lettered **A, B, C**, keyed by a single caption; cite as "fig. 10.7C" — the letter **may be italic in the caption but is not italic in the text reference**. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.13] Working numbers** — Even unnumbered illustrations carry working numbers during editing/production (in file names, on photocopies, in callouts). Editors' concern, not readers'.
- **[3.14] Gallery numbering** — If both text figures and plates need numbers, use two sequences: "figure 1 . . ." and "plate 1 . . ." [JUDGMENT]

### Preparation of Artwork (3.15–3.20) — mostly production

- **[3.15] Submitting artwork** — Consult the publisher before submitting files; supply software/font list, professional scans, no unoptimized web images; good practice: **provide alt text with any image that needs it (see 3.28)**. [production]
- **[3.16] Identifying artwork** — Descriptive file names that identify each file, matched to figure callouts; hard copy numbered in pencil on the back. [production, MECHANICAL]
- **[3.17] Author's inventory** — Complete list of illustrations: color, sizing limits, duplicates/extras, still to come; supply **all** art at manuscript submission (2.4).
- **[3.18] Publisher's inventory** — Publisher checks art against the inventory, numbering, labelling, caption match, and alt text. Writer-relevant: **authors should supply captions and alt text in separate files** to facilitate copyediting (see 2.31). [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.19] Cropping, scaling, shading** — Production, with three writing-adjacent rules: (a) labels/keys must survive reduction — consider font size and line weight for final printed size; (b) **avoid hairline rules**, which may disappear in print; (c) **never let meaning depend on color or shading alone** — use stripes, spots, black-and-white fills, labels.
  > "In general, images that will reproduce well in black and white will be more accessible than color images."
- **[3.20] Artwork to be redrawn** — When labels will be reset: wording, abbreviations, and symbols **should be consistent with those used in the text**, and capitalization reserved for terms that would be capitalized in running text. Map names go in a separate alphabetical list, grouped by type (countries, provinces, cities, rivers). [JUDGMENT]

### Captions and Alternative Text (3.21–3.29)

- **[3.21] Caption format and wording** — A caption may be "a word or two, an incomplete or a complete sentence, several sentences, or a combination." **Sentence case** for captions. Punctuation: **no end punctuation after a caption consisting solely of an incomplete sentence**; if one or more full sentences follow, **each sentence (including the opening phrase) takes closing punctuation**; if most captions in a work are full sentences, incomplete ones may take a period for consistency. Formal titles *within* captions keep title case (3.22). [MECHANICAL for case/punctuation; JUDGMENT for style choice]
  - ex: "Wartime visit to Australia, winter 1940" (phrase, no period)
  - ex: "The White Garden, reduced to its bare bones in early spring. The box hedges, which are still cut by hand, have to be carefully kept in scale with the small and complex garden as well as in keeping with the plants inside the 'boxes.'"
- **[3.22] Formal titles in captions** — Present titles of works per chapter 8 whether standalone or inside a caption: title case for most English titles; italics for paintings, drawings, photographs, statues, books; roman + quotation marks for others (8.164); foreign titles per 11.8–12. **Generic titles are not capitalized.** [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "The head of Venus—a detail from Botticelli's *Birth of Venus*."
  - ex: "Friedrich Overbeck and Peter Cornelius, double portrait, pencil drawing, 1812." (generic title, lowercase)
  - ex: "Frontispiece of *Christian Prayers and Meditations* (London: John Daye, 1569), showing Queen Elizabeth at prayer in her private chapel. Reproduced by permission of the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Trustees of the Lambeth Palace Library."
- **[3.23] Separate number from caption** — Make the illustration number distinct from the caption: **a period after the number usually suffices**, and that is the form authors should use. In print the number may instead be boldface (period then optional) or followed by an em space; *figure* may be spelled out or abbreviated *fig.* per journal/designer style. The word *figure*/*plate* may be omitted only in special cases (e.g., a long series of continuously numbered photographs). [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Figure 3. Detailed stratigraphy and geochronology of the Dubawnt Supergroup."
  - ex: "PLATE 5 [em space] Palace of the Governors, Santa Fe, New Mexico. Undated photograph, circa 1900."
- **[3.24] Locators inside captions** — **Italicize** *top, bottom, left, right, above, below, left to right, clockwise from left, inset* when identifying elements within one illustration, parts of a composite, or (in print) an illustration not on the same page as its caption. A leading locator is followed by a **comma** (or a **colon** if a list follows); mid-sentence or trailing locators may go in **parentheses**. [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Fig. 4. *Above left*, William Livingston; *above right*, Henry Brockholst Livingston; *below left*, John Jay; *below right*, Sarah Livingston Jay"
  - ex: "*Left to right*: Madeleine K. Albright, Dennis Ross, Ehud Barak, and Yasir Arafat"
  - ex: "Figure 2. Schematic block diagram showing upper plate (*top*) and lower plate (*bottom*) of the Battle Lake thrust-tear fault system."
- **[3.25] Key symbols or patterns** — If symbols or patterns are used, identify them **either in a key within the figure or in the caption** — one or the other. Coordinates such as letters labeling the parts of a figure are usually *italicized* (as are descriptive part-terms), but letters that label **study groups**, not parts, are **not** italicized. [MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]
  - ex: "A, wild-type sequence; B, sequence from a PC-deficient Micmac homozygous for the mutation."
  - ex: "Data are presented from shade-grown (filled squares) and open-grown (open squares) culms" (or give the key symbols in place of the words)
- **[3.26] Note online-only enhancements** — When a work exists in print and electronic versions, the **print** caption must flag features available only online ("This figure appears in color online."). Conversely, when the online version is the version of record (most journals), it is generally unnecessary to say "online." [MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]
- **[3.27] Original dimensions** — If a caption gives the dimensions of an original artwork, they follow the medium in order **height, width, (depth)**; include only if relevant to the text unless the rights holder requires it. Photomicrographs may give magnification (e.g., "original magnification, ×400; scale bar, 100 µm"). [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Oil on canvas, 45 × 38 cm"; "Bronze, 49 × 22 × 16 in."
- **[3.28] Alternative text (accessibility)** — Provide alt text for any image that needs it in electronic formats.
  > "Whereas a caption typically describes what an image is, alt text describes what an image shows (within the context of a publication)."
  Rules: some captions already do both, and when the caption or adjacent text fully describes the image, **alt text may be shorter**; purely **decorative images need none** (empty/null attribute so screen readers skip it). Keep it **brief — usually no more than 250 characters including spaces** (some screen readers truncate lower). **Punctuate alt text like ordinary text**; it **cannot contain markup** (italics, paragraphs). Authors are usually best positioned to draft it and should submit it with captions; supply alt text and captions in separate files (2.4, 2.31). Complex charts/graphs may also need an **extended description** linked from the markup (WAI, EPUB Accessibility). [JUDGMENT to write; MECHANICAL for length]
  - ex (caption): "Neil Armstrong's 'double horizon' shot of Buzz Aldrin, July 20, 1969. Marshall Space Flight Center, NASA."
  - ex (alt text): "Photograph of astronaut Buzz Aldrin standing on the surface of the moon, with Neil Armstrong visible as a tiny reflection in Aldrin's helmet visor. The lunar horizon behind Aldrin's head aligns with the opposite horizon reflected in his visor."
  - ex (shorter, when the text nearby covers details): "Photograph of astronaut Buzz Aldrin standing on the surface of the moon, his back to the horizon."
- **[3.29] Alt text for math** — Prepare math for the screen as **marked-up text, not images**: simple expressions need only italics for variables; anything complex belongs in **MathML** (author in LaTeX or an equation editor). If math must be an image (preferably SVG), alt text **restating the math in a combination of words and numerals** is required. [JUDGMENT]

### Credit Lines (3.30–3.38)

- **[3.30] Sources and permissions** — A **credit line** stating the source is usually appropriate and sometimes required. Copyrighted material (published or unpublished) usually requires permission before reproduction; a photograph of an **identifiable person** requires consent (e.g., a model release). Obtaining permissions is the **author's** responsibility; consult the publisher on what needs clearing. [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.31] Placement of credit lines** — Put the credit line **at the end of the caption**, sometimes **in parentheses** to set it off. If most illustrations come from one source, state that once in a note, preface, acknowledgments, or on the copyright page; heavily illustrated books may gather all credits in back matter — unless the grantor requires the credit with the image (some charge more otherwise). [MECHANICAL placement; JUDGMENT for wording]
  - ex: "Fig. 37. The myth that all children love dinosaurs is contradicted by this nineteenth-century scene of a visit to the monsters at Crystal Palace. (Cartoon by John Leech. 'Punch's Almanack for 1855,' *Punch* 28 [1855]: 8. Photo courtesy of the Newberry Library, Chicago.)"
- **[3.32] Credit the author** — Even when not required, include a credit for author-made illustrations, using the **author's full name as credited on the title page** ("Photo by Teju Cole") rather than "Photo by author," which is meaningless out of context; with coauthors, credit only the author who made the image.
- **[3.33] Credit copyrighted sources** — Absent fair use or a blanket license, a reproduction from a copyrighted published work (including anything found online) requires formal permission, and the credit line should give **author, title, publication details, (occasionally) copyright date, and the page or figure number**; if the work is in the bibliography/reference list, a **shortened form** suffices. [MECHANICAL template]
  - ex: "Reproduced by permission from Mark Girouard, *Life in the English Country House: A Social and Architectural History* (Yale University Press, 1978), 162."
  - ex: "Reproduced by permission from Nakano et al., 'Ecosystem Metabolism,' fig. 3."
  - ex: "Rebecca Belmore, *Fringe*, 2007. Collection of the National Gallery of Canada, Ottawa. Reprinted by permission of the artist."
  - If a grantor demands its own language, exercise editorial discretion — "follow the spirit rather than the letter" — to keep usage consistent across the work.
- **[3.34] Credit commissioned work** — For work made for hire, credit the creator as professional courtesy unless the piece is legibly signed and the signature is reproduced. [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Map by Megan Perry"; "Photo by Sergio Gonzalez"; "Drawing by Giulia Albertazzi"
- **[3.35] Credit free material** — For material supplied free and without restrictions, **courtesy** is the word: "Photograph courtesy of Ford Motor Company." Creative Commons credits **specify the license** and normally omit "courtesy": "Photo by Mehmet Karatay (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0)." [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.36] Credit public-domain material** — No permission needed, but a credit line is still appropriate for readers' information (e.g., "Illustration by Joseph Pennell for Henry James, *English Hours* (Houghton, Mifflin, 1905), facing p. 82."). [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.37] Credit agency material** — Commercial-agency photos take a credit line separating creator from agency with a **slash**, spaced on both sides when either name is an open compound. [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Photograph by Scott Olson / Getty Images."
  - ex: "Kapwani Kiwanga, *Oryza*, 2021. Photograph: Marc Domage / le Crédac. © Kapwani Kiwanga / ADAGP, Paris, 2021."
- **[3.38] Credit adapted material** — If you adjusted an illustration or used another's data, credit that source; note if the graphic was made by or with **AI**. [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "(Data from New York Zoological Park; National Zoological Park; Giron 1980.)"; "( . . . circles added by author.)"; "Adapted from DuVernay (2024, fig. 5)."
  - ex: "Fig. 3. Image generated by DALL-E 2, April 7, 2023, from the prompt 'An ornate bookshelf with a portal into another dimension.'"

### Lists of Illustrations (3.39–3.41)

- **[3.39] When to include a list of illustrations** — Only when the illustrations are of **intrinsic interest apart from the text**; most illustrated books do not need one. It follows the table of contents and may double as the list of credits. [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.40] Gallery illustrations in the list** — Gallery contents need not be listed individually; a line such as "Photographs follow pages 228 and 332" may be added after the detailed list of figures.
- **[3.41] Shorten captions in the list** — Condense each long caption to **one line (two at the most)**, with the page number. [MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]
  - ex: caption "Fig. 18. The White Garden, reduced to its bare bones in early spring. The box hedges, which are still cut by hand, have to be carefully kept in scale . . ." → list entry "18. The White Garden in early spring 43"
  - ex: "Plate 21. The tall trees of the valley, planted by Russell Page, are reflected among the water lilies . . ." → "21. Page's tall trees reflected among water lilies 75"

### Charts (3.42–3.47)

- **[3.42] Charts are figures** — A chart/graph is **line art**, numbered and labeled as a figure (*fig. 1*). Use one "only if it summarizes the data more effectively than mere words can."
  > "While integral to the text, it should, like a table, make sense on its own terms."
  Charts for black-and-white reproduction must not be created in color, and **meaning must never depend on color alone** (add labels, icons, cross-hatching). Online/e-book charts may need alt text or an extended description (3.28). [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.43] Consistency among charts** — Comparable charts in one work share one graphic and typographic style.
  > "elements of the same kind must always be represented in the same way. Different visual effects should be used only to distinguish one element from another, never just for variety."
- **[3.44] Axes and curves** — **Label both axes** (the x/y axes function like column heads and stubs in a table, 3.53); the y-axis label reads **from the bottom up** (or may sit at the top of the axis). Distinguish curves graphically (solid vs. broken line), never by color if for B/W; label all elements or key them in a key or caption (3.7, 3.25). [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.45] Chart titles and labels** — The chart's **title is part of the caption, immediately after the figure number, in sentence case** (contrast 3.55: table titles sit *above* the table). **Labels** inside the chart are normally **lowercased** (except proper nouns or other terms capitalized in running text); phrased labels may be sentence-cased; labels may be explained in the caption. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.46] Abbreviations in labels** — Abbreviations/symbols are acceptable in labels **if easily recognizable or explained in a key or caption**; an abbreviation taken from the text must be clear **from the figure alone**, since figures get reproduced out of context. Longer forms ("US$millions") suit nonspecialized readers; the short form ("US$M") is fine if clear and **used consistently**. [MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]
- **[3.47] Genealogical and pedigree charts** — Special layouts for family/genetic relationships; plan for minimal line crossing and minimal extraneous data. Production-level guidance.

## TABLES

### Introduction (3.48–3.50)

- **[3.48] Table preparation** — No single table in the chapter is a prototype; adapt patterns to the data and users. Tables are expensive to typeset and correct, so design them carefully; consult the publisher on number, size, and form.
  > "A table should be as simple as the material allows and understandable on its own; even a reader unfamiliar with the material presented should be able to make general sense of a table. The text may highlight the main points in a table and summarize its message but should not duplicate the details."
  [JUDGMENT — the "don't duplicate the details" half is a real prose rule]
- **[3.49] Use of tables** — A table suits many similar facts that must be **scanned and compared**; it can replace several paragraphs more clearly. Most appropriate for scientific, statistical, financial, technical material; if exact values don't matter to the argument, a graph, bar chart, or plain text may serve better. [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.50] Consistency among tables** — Consistency is indispensable within one table and across all tables.
  > "Because a prime virtue of tables is easy comparison, consistency in style is indispensable both within one table and among several."
  Hold one style for titles, column heads, abbreviations, line spacing, indentation, fonts, and rules — while allowing individual tables the rules they genuinely need. [MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]

### Table Numbering and Placement (3.51–3.52)

- **[3.51] Table numbers and text references** — Number tables **separately from illustrations** (*table 1, table 2*); double numeration by chapter is common (*table 1.1, table 2.3*; see 3.11). **Cite every table in the text by number** (directly or parenthetically), not by location; **lowercase** the word *table* in text references ("see table 5.3"). Numbers follow order of appearance, and first mentions should too; **give each table its own number** (*tables 14, 15, and 16*, **not** *tables 14A–C*). [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "The first column of table 2 displays the results of a model predicting the age trajectory of health . . ."
  - ex: "Ethnographic observation brought to light four analytically distinct but empirically interrelated types of worker response to the new regimes (see table 5.3)."
- **[3.52] Placement of tables** — A short list needing only a few columns can run in the text **unnumbered and untitled**. A numbered table appears as soon as possible after its first reference; it may break across pages or run broadside. Callouts ("table 3 about here") are production instructions and **do not appear in the published work** (for reader-facing references see 3.51). [MECHANICAL]

### The Parts of a Table (3.53–3.70)

- **[3.53] Structure** — Rows and columns correspond to the x and y axes of a graph. The top row (**column heads**) defines one set of variables; the far-left column (**stub entries**) defines the other; independent variables usually go in the stub. A row/column intersection is a **cell** (data cell). [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.54] Rules** — Use rules sparingly: most tables need **three horizontal rules** — top (below title, above column heads), below the column heads, bottom (above the notes). Add horizontal rules only to separate spanner heads (3.58) or enclose cut-in heads (3.59); a rule above totals is traditional but optional. **Avoid vertical rules** except for a doubled-up table (3.87) or an especially long/complex one. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.55] Table titles** — The title sits **above** the table, usually on the same line as the number, separated by punctuation or by space + typographic distinction (e.g., bold number + em space); the number is **always preceded by the word *table***. Capitals: **sentence case** (8.159) or traditional title case (8.160), **one style throughout the work**.
  > "Table titles should be as succinct as possible and should not suggest any interpretation of the data."
  Titles take **noun form**, and **participles are preferred to relative clauses**.
  - ex: "Grammar and punctuation errors at suburban schools" > "High degree of grammar and punctuation errors at suburban schools"
  - ex: "Households subscribing to ala carte channels," not "Households that subscribe to ala carte channels."
  - ex: "Table 6. Ratios of parental income coefficients to SAT score coefficients"
  [MECHANICAL case/format; JUDGMENT wording]
- **[3.56] Parenthetical info in table titles** — Key explanatory/statistical matter may sit in parentheses in the title, **in sentence case even if the main title is title case**; anything more detailed goes in a note (3.79–3.80). [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Federal employees in the Progressive Era (total plus selected agencies)"; "Gender as a factor in successful business transactions (N = 4,400)"
- **[3.57] Column heads** — Keep them **as brief as possible**, **sentence case**, abbreviations acceptable where space is at a premium; hold them consistent across tables in a work. The stub column does not always need a head. **Units of measurement or clarifying symbols belong after the head in parentheses** ($, %, km, *n*); parentheses may also flag cell content: "Children with positive results, % (no. positive/no. tested)" with a cell reading "27.3 (6/22)." If columns must be numbered for reference, use **Arabic numerals in parentheses, centered below the head, above the rule**. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.58] Spanner heads** — Two or more levels of head (a collective head over related columns) are allowed, with a horizontal **spanner (straddle) rule** between spanner and column heads; "spanner heads should seldom exceed two levels." [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.59] Cut-in heads** — Subheads spanning all columns but the first, usually between horizontal rules. A very long table with many cut-in heads is a candidate for splitting into two or more tables. [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.60] Stub entries** — The stub lists the categories. Give it a head if all entries are of like kind (even a general one helps: "Characteristic," "Variable," "Year"); **omit the head if entries are self-explanatory too unlike** to summarize. Entries are **sentence-cased**, take **no end punctuation unless they are questions**, and must be **parallel in syntax**. [MECHANICAL for case/punctuation; JUDGMENT for parallelism]
  - ex: "Authors, Publishers, Printers" — not "Authors, Publishing concerns, Operates print shop"
- **[3.61] Stub entries with subheads** — Subentries may be marked by indent, italics/bold for main entries, or a combination (plus a further level if needed). **Capitalize the first word of a subentry as well as of a main entry** (avoids confusion with runover lines). No colon after main entries unless journal style requires it. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.62] Runover lines in the stub** — Indent runovers in stub entries (typically 1 em); set flush left only if there is extra space between rows; if there are indented subentries, **runovers must be indented more deeply than the lowest level of subentry**. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.63] Abbreviations in the stub** — Symbols/abbreviations ($, %, km, *n*) are acceptable, but **ditto marks are not** ("they save no space and make work for readers"); **nonstandard abbreviations must be defined in a note** (3.80). [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.64] Totals** — "Total" at the foot of the stub is indented more deeply than the greatest indent above, or distinguished typographically (e.g., italic). [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.65] Leaders** — Leaders (spaced periods after a stub entry) are used when the entry-to-row connection would otherwise be unclear; common in journals, rare in books. Zebra shading is an alternative. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.66] Table body and cells** — The body is the grid of cells — the substance of the table; cells usually hold data but may be empty (3.68). [JUDGMENT]
- **[3.67] Column data** — "Whenever possible, columns should carry the same kinds of information" (money in one, percentages in another, words in another; two kinds may share a column if clear, see 3.70). **No column should repeat identical information in every cell** — put that in a note instead. [JUDGMENT; MECHANICAL partly]
- **[3.68] Empty cells** — If a head doesn't apply, leave the cell blank or, better, fill it with an **em dash** or **three unspaced ellipsis dots**. To distinguish "not applicable" (blank) from "no data available" (em dash/dots), say so in a note, or use *n/a* and *n.d.* with definitions in a note. **A zero means literally zero.** [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.69] Matrixes** — In a matrix the stub entries duplicate the column heads. Cells repeating order-independent data may be left blank; the intersection of matching heads may be blank or marked with an em dash or ellipsis dots. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.70] Two values in one cell** — To save columns, one cell may carry two values, one in parentheses; **clarify in the column head (3.57) or a note**. [MECHANICAL]

### Cell Alignment and Formatting (3.71–3.76)

- **[3.71] Alignment of rows** — Each cell aligns with its stub entry; if the stub entry runs over, align on its **last line** — but when both stub and cells run over, align **first lines** throughout. First-line alignment also applies where the first column is not a stub. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.72] Alignment of column heads** — Heads in a row align on the baseline, and on the **last line** if any head runs over; each head (except the stub's) is centered on its widest cell entry, or left-aligned if centering fails; **stub head and entries are always left-aligned**. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.73] Alignment of numbers** — Whole numbers align on the last digit ("ranged right"); numbers with decimals align on the decimal point (or on the decimal comma where spaces separate digit groups, 9.57); the column of numerals is centered within the column on the widest numeral. **In tables, drop the leading zero for quantities below 1.0** unless house style requires it (running text usually requires it; see 9.21). Columns of mixed numbers align on the most frequent type; ellipses and em dashes are centered. Print/PDF only — in HTML, left alignment or centering is acceptable (3.89). [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.74] Alignment of word columns** — Appearance governs: center short entries that don't run over; begin longer entries **flush left**; runovers may be indented or set flush with the first line if entries are well spaced. [MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]
- **[3.75] Totals, averages, means** — Extra vertical space or a short rule above a column total is optional; **no rule above averages or means**; be consistent and follow journal/series style. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.76] When to use totals** — Include totals and subtotals only as useful. When percentages rest on different *n*'s, a final percentage based on total *N* may be informative. Rounding may push a percentage total to 99% or 101%: **give the actual value and note the discrepancy**. [JUDGMENT]

### Notes to Tables (3.77–3.81)

- **[3.77] Order and placement** — Four kinds of notes, in this order: **(1) source notes, (2) general notes, (3) notes to specific parts, (4) notes on significance levels.** They appear **immediately below the table**, as table footnotes, **numbered separately from text notes**; in a multipage table, page-specific notes may sit at the foot of their page. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.78] Source notes** — If the data are not the author's own, acknowledge the source fully in an **unnumbered** note introduced by ***Source*** or ***Sources***, **italic, followed by a colon**; it precedes all other notes. If the sources are in the bibliography, a shortened form may be used. Reproducing a copyrighted table unchanged requires permission, credited in the source note (see 3.33). [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "Sources: Data from Richard H. Adams Jr., 'Remittances, Investment, and Rural Asset Accumulation in Pakistan,' *Economic Development and Cultural Change* 47, no. 1 (1998): 155–73; David Bevan, Paul Collier, and Jan Gunning, *Peasants and Government: An Economic Analysis* (Clarendon Press, 1989), 125–28."
  - ex (shortened): "Sources: Data from Adams (1998); Bevan, Collier, and Gunning (1989)."
- **[3.79] General notes** — A note on the table as a whole follows any source note and opens with ***Note***, **italic, followed by a colon**. A brief general note may instead be folded into the title in parentheses (3.56). Some scientific publications use a **headnote** right after the title, like the explanatory part of a figure caption (3.21). [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.80] Notes to specific parts** — Use **superscript letters** (generally preferred), numerals (better when the data are mostly words), or symbols (better where letters/numbers could be read as exponents) — **one system consistently across all tables**. Each table starts its own series, **beginning with *a* (usually roman), `1`, or `*`**; the sequence runs **left to right, top to bottom**. The same marker is reused for two or more elements when one note applies to them (unlike text note numbers). A marker on a **column head** applies to the whole column; on a **stub entry**, to that row. Repeat the marker at the foot of the table before its note, optionally followed by a space, **never by a period**. Symbol order: `*`, †, ‡, §, ‖, #, then doubled and tripled in the same sequence (`**`, ††, ‡‡ . . .). Do not use `*` if *p* values occur in the table. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.81] Significance-level (probability) notes** — Asterisks are standard: one for the least significant level, two for the next, three for the third; if other levels are reported, **use letters rather than asterisks**. **Lowercase italic *p***; **omit the zero before the decimal** (`*p < .05`). Probability notes come **last**. If set on one line, run them together separated by **semicolons** (no punctuation if spaced). Some journals capitalize *P*. [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: `*p < .05`; `**p < .01`

### Editing Tables (3.82–3.89)

- **[3.82] Editing table content** — Edit tables for capitalization, spelling, punctuation, abbreviations, numbers, and symbols; check internal consistency, consistency across tables (column heads, stub entries), and consistency with the surrounding text. Abbreviations lifted from the text must be intelligible from the table alone. Verify totals (refer discrepancies to the author), check note references and the sequence of markers, check each table's relevance to the text and redundancy against other tables.
  > "A lay reader or a non-technical editor should be able to make logical sense of a table, even if the material is highly technical."
- **[3.83] "Percent" vs. "percentage"** — *Percent* is an adverb ("10 percent of the class") or, less often, an adjective ("a 10 percent raise"); *percentage* is the noun ("a significant percentage of her income"). The symbol **% may stand for either word**. [MECHANICAL-ish; JUDGMENT]
- **[3.84] Number ranges** — Ranges must not overlap, must leave no gaps, and must be as precise as the data require; make clear whether "up to" means "up to and including." **Use > and < only in their strict senses** (>60 = 61 and up; ≥60 includes 60); insert the correct symbols, never underline them. [MECHANICAL]
  - ex: "less than $5, $5–$9, $10–$14, and $15–$19" — not "$1–$5, $5–$10"; more precision: "$1.00–$4.99, $5.00–$9.99"
- **[3.85] Signs and symbols** — In a column of all dollars or all percentages, move the sign into the **column head** (3.57) or occasionally the stub entry, not every cell. Operational signs (+/−) stay immediately left of their numbers, not aligned with each other. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.86] *N* vs. *n*** — **Italic capital *N*** = total number of a group; **italic lowercase *n*** = a portion of the total. [MECHANICAL]
- **[3.87] Adjusting and checking tables** — Production list: (1) long narrow tables are "doubled up" side by side with heads repeated; wide shallow tables may be turned (heads ↔ stubs) or turned broadside; (2) oversize tables: shorten wording, use abbreviations, allow the table to extend into the margin, or replace heads with numbers keyed to a general note (fig. 3.25); (3) check rules and spanner rules; (4) check alignment of numbers and stub entries. Writer-relevant: **avoid old-style numerals in tabular matter** (they don't align), and **shortening wording/using abbreviations is the first remedy for an oversize table**. [production]
- **[3.88] "Continued" tables** — For a vertical table longer than a page, **repeat the column heads on each page** and add the table number + "continued"; for broadside tables, heads repeat on each new verso. Notes usually go at the end of the whole table. [production, MECHANICAL check]
- **[3.89] Accessibility for tables** — Mark up tables as **text, not images**, with headers, cells, columns, and rows identified so their relationships and **logical reading order** are clear; complex tables (heads spanning rows/columns) need extra markup, and may benefit from a **summary/descriptive text**; if a table must be an image, provide alt text (3.28). [JUDGMENT for markup quality]
