# Capitalization, names, terms, and titles of works

Source: CMOS ch 8 (rules 8.1-8.202). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch08a, ch08b. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch08a

1. **Titles before a name, lowercase elsewhere** — capitalize *President Lincoln*, *Senator Warren*,
   *General Grant*, *Pope Francis*, *Professor Meltzer*; lowercase *the president*, *the senator*, *the
   general* (8.20, 8.23, 8.25, 8.27, 8.28, 8.29).
2. **Capitalize a title in apposition before a name even with an article or adjective** — *the Empress
   Elisabeth*, *German Chancellor Olaf Scholz*, *former President Carter*, *then-Secretary of State
   Hillary Clinton*; lowercase once a comma separates it (8.22 — 18th-ed. change).
3. **Never attach a descriptive role to a bare surname** — *historians* yes, *historian Kruse* no
   (8.31).
4. **Initials get a space between them** — *M. F. K. Fisher*, but *LBJ* (8.4).
5. **Respect the bearer's spelling, including lowercase names** — *bell hooks*, *eBay*; capitalize at
   a sentence start unless it begins with @, then reword (8.4, 8.6, 8.70).
6. **Keep every element of a hyphenated or compound surname** — *Vaughan Williams*, *Mies van der
   Rohe*, *Sackville-West* (8.7).
7. **Lowercase particles in full names per language tradition, and follow the language's rule when the
   surname stands alone** — French drops *de*, Dutch capitalizes *Van* alone, Italian usually keeps
   *D'*/*Da*, Arabic keeps *Ibn*/*al-* (8.5, 8.8–8.15).
8. **Epithets are capitalized, a *the* in them is not, and an epithet added to a name takes quotation
   marks** — *the Sun King*, *Ivan IV, "the Terrible"* (8.35).
9. **Kinship terms: lowercase for relations, capitalize in place of a name or as part of one** — *my
   sister Clarice* but *Aunt Maud*, *Please, Mom* (8.37).
10. **Capitalize ethnic, national, and regional names and their adjectives** — and keep *Indigenous*,
    *Native*, and *Black* capitalized for people (8.39).
11. **Do not hyphenate compound nationalities** — *African American*, *Italian American* (8.40).
12. **Do not use *Black* (or similar) as a noun** — *Black people*, not *Blacks* (8.39).
13. **Lowercase socioeconomic, orientation/gender, and disability terms; capitalize *Deaf* only for the
    cultural community** (8.41, 8.42, 8.44).
14. **Capitalize generational labels containing *generation*, lowercase *baby boomer*, *millennial***
    (8.43 — 18th-ed. change).
15. **Compass points lowercase for direction; region names capitalized but check the dictionary** —
    *northern Ohio* vs. *the North*, *Southern California* vs. *southern Minnesota* (8.47, 8.48).
16. **Don't add an English generic to a non-English place-name** — *the Rio Grande*, *Denali*, *the
    Sierra Nevada* (8.56).
17. **Lowercase a generic place/building term standing alone, capitalize it inside a name or shared by
    two names** — *Pennsylvania and New York Avenues*, *the Empire State and Chrysler Buildings*, *the
    Plaza* → *the plaza* (8.52, 8.54, 8.57, 8.58).
18. **Check Merriam-Webster before lowercasing a word derived from a proper noun** — the 18th edition
    moved *toward* capitals (*Gruyère*, *Swiss cheese*, *French dressing*), while *french fries*,
    *brussels sprouts*, *diesel engine* stay down (8.62).
19. **Capitalize political parties, movements, and their formal adherents, but lowercase ideologies and
    metaphorical uses** — *a Republican*, *communist parties*, *nazi tendencies* (8.67).
20. **Lowercase a leading *the* in names of organizations, institutions, and periodicals' parent
    bodies**, and lowercase generic *university, company, press, museum, orchestra* alone (8.69, 8.71).
21. **Cap the big named events, lowercase the generically described ones — "if in doubt, opt for
    lowercase"** — *the Great Depression* vs. *the crash of 1929*, *the civil rights movement* (8.76).
22. **Italicize legal case names, including *v.*** (8.83).
23. **Lowercase academic subjects and seasons; capitalize days, months, holidays, and spelled-out time
    zones** (8.86, 8.89, 8.90, 8.91).
24. **Consistency beats the individual call** — for *White*/*Brown* capitalization, style terms labeled
    "often capitalized," and party/ideology forms, pick one and apply it throughout (8.39, 8.67, 8.80).
25. **When the dictionary and the logic disagree, the dictionary wins** (8.1, 8.48, 8.62).

### From ch08b

1. **English title in running text → title case + (italics | "quotes") — never one without the other** [8.158,
   8.160, 8.164]. Decide the work type first (master table under 8.164), then apply the capitalization rules.
2. **Capitalize first and last words, all nouns/pronouns/verbs/adjectives/adverbs, and subordinating
   conjunctions; lowercase *the/a/an*, *and/but/for/or/nor*, *to* (any use), *as* (any use), and prepositions
   of four letters or fewer** [8.160 rules 1–5].
3. **Capitalize prepositions of five letters or more** — *About, After, Against, Between, Through, Under,
   Without* (18th-ed change, replacing the old four-letter cutoff) [8.160 rule 3].
4. **Exceptions to the preposition rule: capitalize a short preposition used adverbially/adjectivally** (*Look
   Up*, *Turn Down*, *The On Button*, *Come To*) **or inside a Latin phrase** (*In Vitro*, *De Facto*)
   [8.160 rule 3; 8.161].
5. **Hyphenated compounds in titles: capitalize every element except articles, short prepositions,
   coordinating conjunctions, and *flat*/*sharp*; capitalize the second element of a prefix-formed compound
   and of a spelled-out number or fraction** — *Anti-Intellectual*, *Pre-Authorization*, *Two-Thirds
   Majority*, *Twenty-First-Century*, *E-flat Concerto*, *Lying-In* [8.162].
6. **Lowercase the species epithet even when it ends the title** — *From Homo erectus to Homo sapiens: A Brief
   History*; also *sp.*/*spp.* [8.160 rule 7].
7. **Never lowercase *de*, *von*, *da*, *van* inside a title; they are part of the name** — *Defenders of da
   Vinci Fail the Test* [8.160 rule 6].
8. **Italicize whole works; quote contained ones**: books, periodicals, news sites/blogs, films, TV/radio/
   podcast series, video games, plays, long poems, operas, albums, artworks, exhibitions, pamphlets vs.
   articles, chapters, stories, essays, episodes, songs, short poems, unpublished works [8.170, 8.179, 8.183,
   8.185, 8.187, 8.190, 8.191, 8.192, 8.196, 8.199, 8.200, 8.204].
9. **Websites are roman, title case, no quotes; pages/sections *on* a website are quoted; news sites and blogs
   are italicized** [8.193, 8.194].
10. **Keep an initial *The* that belongs to a periodical — capitalized and italicized** (*The New York Times*),
    but lowercase it when the syntax claims it (*the Washington Post reporter's sources*) [8.172].
11. **Drop an initial *a/an/the* from a book title only if the syntax demands it; when in doubt, keep it**
    [8.171].
12. **Don't italicize *magazine*, *journal*, *review*, *series*, or *edition* unless part of the official
    title** [8.173, 8.178].
13. **The title is a singular noun: singular verb, and never let a title's own punctuation govern your
    sentence** — *The Waves is not a typical novel*; no comma after *Play It Again, Sam* [8.168].
14. **Never use a title as the subject of a sentence** — *a book about crime and punishment*, not *about Crime
    and Punishment* [8.176].
15. **Inside an italic title, leave italic what would be italic anyway (foreign words, words as words, genus
    names, ship names); put a work-title *within* a title in quotation marks** [8.175].
16. **A subtitle always starts with a capital and is joined to the title by a colon; after an em dash, the
    first word is lowercased** [8.166].
17. **Preserve the original spelling and hyphenation of a title; you may change only capitalization, add a
    colon, restore an omitted comma, convert dashes to unspaced em dashes, and change **&** to *and*** [8.167].
18. **Quote generic part-words and lowercase them: *the preface*, *the appendix*, *the bibliography*, *chapter
    4*, *table 4.2*, *act 3, scene 2*** [8.181, 8.182, 8.186].
19. **Substitute the generic term for a trademark where you can; capitalize the trademark if you must use it;
    omit ® and ™** [8.154].
20. **Leave intercapped product names alone (*iPhone, PowerPoint*) and do not capitalize them at the start of a
    sentence; but give an all-lowercase name a capital (*Mac Mini*)** [8.155].
21. **Italicize species binomials (genus capitalized, epithet lowercase); roman for phyla, orders, families,
    and for English derivatives (*hominid*, *feline*)** [8.121, 8.127, 8.128].
22. **Lowercase diseases, procedures, and generic drug names but capitalize eponyms, acronyms, and brand names
    — *Alzheimer disease*, *COVID-19*, *interferon beta-1a (Avonex)*** [8.145, 8.147].
23. **Capitalize formal names, lowercase the generic stand-alone: *the Episcopal Church* / *an Episcopal
    church*; *the Missouri Synod* / *the synod*; *the Thirty-Third Division* / *the division*** [8.98, 8.101,
    8.102, 8.113].
24. **Scripture names and books/sections of the Bible are capitalized but roman — never italicized; lowercase
    *biblical*, *scriptural*, *a psalm*** [8.104, 8.106, 8.107].
25. **Italicize vessel names; capitalize but roman-set vehicle models, train runs, and space programs** [8.117,
    8.118].

## Overview (8.1–8.2)

- **[8.1] Proper nouns and adjectives** — Capitalize proper nouns (specific people, places, named
  entities) and the adjectives/terms derived from or associated with them; **but** Chicago's
  traditional instinct is a "down" style, and the 18th edition now *supports* capitals for **more**
  terms than previous editions (see 8.62). **[JUDGMENT]** (with a dictionary check).
  - ex: *Brussels* and *French* are capitalized, yet Chicago recommends lowercase **brussels sprouts**
    and **french fries**, "to signal that such sprouts are not necessarily from Brussels and such
    fries are not literally French."
  - ex: *President Taft* (title) and *University of Chicago* (part of the name) are capitalized;
    generic *president* and *university* are not, "even when they refer to a specific person or
    university" (see 8.20–34).
  - ex: *Black* and *Indigenous*, applied to people, "have become accepted as proper adjectives
    despite not being derived from proper nouns" (see 8.39).
  > "In general, the recommendations in this chapter are designed to recognize common usage (which
  > may favor capitalization), supported by logic (which in turn may argue for lowercase); when in
  > doubt, follow Merriam-Webster (see 7.1) or, for terms not listed there, an up-to-date
  > encyclopedia or other reputable source."
- **[8.2] Italics vs. quotation marks for titles** — Use **italics** for the titles of major or
  freestanding works (books, journals and other periodicals, movies and TV shows, musicals and plays,
  paintings) and for names of **ships and other craft, species names, and legal cases**. Use
  **quotation marks** only for subsections of larger works (chapters, articles, TV episodes, poems in
  a collection). Titles of a **series or a website** — an umbrella for many works — get **neither**.
  **[MECHANICAL]** (with 8.164 as the deciding table).

## Personal Names (8.3–8.19)

- **[8.3] Personal names — reference sources** — Take the spelling of a deceased well-known person
  from Merriam-Webster, else *Encyclopaedia Britannica*; for a living person consult the person's own
  preferred usage, else M-W/Britannica, else *Who's Who*; Wikipedia is a lead, not a source.
  **Double-check every name in at least one additional source**; where sources disagree (Brueghel /
  Breughel / Breugel), "the writer or editor must make a choice and stick with it." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.4] Capitalization of personal names** — Capitalize names and initials of persons, real or
  fictitious; put a **space between initials** ("M. F. K. Fisher", "P. D. James") except when initials
  stand alone ("LBJ"). Respect a bearer's unconventional spelling (*bell hooks*); "E. E. Cummings can
  be safely capitalized; it was one of his publishers, not he himself, who lowercased his name."
  Most editors "draw the line at beginning a sentence with a lowercased name" — rewrite or capitalize.
  A lowercase particle is capitalized at the start of a sentence or note, lowercase at the start of a
  bibliography entry. **[MECHANICAL]** for initials/particles; **[JUDGMENT]** for pen names.
  - ex: *Jane Doe*; *M. F. K. Fisher*; *George S. McGovern*; *Malcolm X*; *P. D. James*; *LBJ*
- **[8.5] Names with particles or prefixes** — Capitalization and spacing of *de, d', de la, el, von,
  van, ten* vary "widely": confirm in a biographical dictionary. When the surname stands alone the
  particle is usually retained, styled as in the full name. *Le/La/L'* are usually capitalized unless
  preceded by *de*; the *the* of an English-form Native American name may stay lowercase except at the
  start of a sentence. *Fitz, Mc, O'* stay with the surname and are capitalized. Lowercase particles
  **do** get capitals in an all-caps context ("MELISSA MCCARTHY (not McCARTHY)"). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Walter de la Mare; de la Mare* · *Farouk El-Baz; El-Baz* · *W. E. B. Du Bois; Du Bois* ·
    *Daphne du Maurier; du Maurier* · *John le Carré; le Carré* · *Robert M. La Follette Sr.; La
    Follette* · but *Wernher von Braun; von Braun* — and at a sentence start, *Von Braun's designs…*
- **[8.6] Screen names and other aliases** — Follow the bearer's usage as far as determinable, including
  for usernames ("readers will expect them to match what they find online"). A *the* in a stage name
  may be capitalized (*The Weeknd*). An all-lowercase name takes an initial capital at the start of a
  sentence **unless** it begins with @, in which case reword. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *André the Giant*; *Megan Thee Stallion*; *tracks featuring The Weeknd*; *a photo on Flickr by
    rvjak* → *Rvjak's photos included…*
  - ex (fix): *@nytimes posted that…* → *The New York Times (@nytimes) posted that…*
- **[8.7] Hyphenated and extended names** — Retain every element of a hyphenated or multi-element
  surname; use a shorter form only after the fuller form has been established. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Victoria Sackville-West; Sackville-West* · *Ralph Vaughan Williams; Vaughan Williams* (not
    *Williams*) · *Ludwig Mies van der Rohe; Mies van der Rohe* (not *van der Rohe*) · but *John Hope
    Franklin; Franklin* · *Charlotte Perkins Gilman; Gilman*. For unhyphenated compound names of
    lesser-known persons, use the **last element** only (plus any particle).
- **[8.8] French names** — Lowercase *de* and *d'* (capital at sentence start); when the surname is
  used alone *de* is often **dropped** (*Alfred de Musset; Musset*), though tradition keeps it in *de
  Gaulle*. *de la*: *la* is usually capitalized and always retained (*Jean de La Fontaine; La
  Fontaine*). *du* is lowercase in a full name but retained and capitalized alone (*Philippe du Puy de
  Clinchamps; Du Puy de Clinchamps*). Article *le* is capitalized with or without the first name
  (*Gustave Le Bon; Le Bon*). Closed-up forms keep a capital *D* (*Debussy*, *Dupont*). Initials of a
  hyphenated given name are hyphenated: *Jean-Paul Sartre; J.-P. Sartre*. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.9] German and Portuguese names** — Lowercase the particle and usually **drop** it when the
  surname stands alone (*von Humboldt; Humboldt*; *van Beethoven; Beethoven*; *da Silva Quadros;
  Quadros*); exceptions follow usage (*Ursula von der Leyen; von der Leyen*; *Vasco da Gama; da
  Gama*). Consult a biographical dictionary. **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.10] Italian names** — Particles are **most often uppercased and retained** alone (*Gabriele
  D'Annunzio; D'Annunzio*; *Lorenzo Da Ponte; Da Ponte*), but older aristocratic names lowercase and
  drop them (*Lorenzo de' Medici; Medici*; *Beatrice d'Este; Este*). *Leonardo da Vinci* is referred to
  as **Leonardo** — "'da Vinci' is an epithet, not a surname." **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.11] Dutch names** — Lowercase *van, van den, ter* in the full name but **capitalize when only
  the surname is used** (*Vincent van Gogh; Van Gogh*; *Corrie ten Boom; Ten Boom*); honor a family's
  own capital (*Eddie Van Halen; Van Halen*). **[MECHANICAL]** (given the name).
- **[8.12] Spanish names** — Use both family names when the given name is omitted ("It is never
  incorrect to use both") or one, per the person's preference or established usage. Treat articles and
  prepositions as in French (*Tomás de Torquemada; Torquemada*; *Bartolomé de Las Casas; Las Casas*).
  **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *José Ortega y Gasset; Ortega y Gasset* or *Ortega* · *Federico García Lorca; García Lorca*
    (popularly *Lorca*)
- **[8.13] Russian names** — Give given name, patronymic, family name in that order and do not mistake
  a patronymic for a middle name or surname; note the gendered endings (*Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov* /
  *Maria Ilyinichna Ulyanova*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.14] Hungarian names** — Family name precedes given name in Hungarian (*Molnár Ferenc*); invert
  for English contexts (*Ferenc Molnár*), and don't read an initial in *E. Kiss Katalin* as a middle
  initial when inverted (*Katalin E. Kiss*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.15] Arabic names** — Treat *Abu, Abd, Ibn, Al, El* as integral parts of the surname: **do not
  drop them** (*Abdul Aziz Ibn Saud; Ibn Saud*), except where a person is better known without
  (*Anwar el-Sadat; Sadat*; *Nawal El Saadawi; Saadawi*). Hyphenated elements may be lowercased
  (*Tawfiq al-Hakim; al-Hakim*). Older rulers are often shortened to the **first** part (*Harun
  al-Rashid; Harun*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.16] Chinese names** — Keep family name first except for people known to use Western order
  (*Chiang Kai-shek; Chiang*; *Li Bai; Li*; but *Laura Cha Shih May-lung; Laura Cha*; *Anthony Yu;
  Yu*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.17] Japanese names** — Family name first in Japanese usage (*Yoshida Shigeru; Yoshida*), but
  many Japanese names are westernized in English (*Akira Kurosawa; Kurosawa*; *Shinzo Abe; Abe*).
  **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.18] Korean names** — Family name first even in English (*Kim Dae-jung; Kim*; *Oh Jung-hee; Oh*),
  unless the person has inverted it (*Chang-rae Lee; Lee*). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.19] Other Asian names** — Some cultures use the given name as the short form: the Indonesian
  writer *Pramoedya Ananta Toer* is *Pramoedya*, not *Toer*. If in doubt, use the full form in all
  references. **[JUDGMENT]**

## Titles and Offices (8.20–8.34)

- **[8.20] The general rule** — Capitalize civil, military, religious, and professional titles **when
  they immediately precede a personal name** (i.e., used as part of the name); **lowercase** them when
  they follow a name or stand in place of a name. **[MECHANICAL]** (the highest-value mechanical rule in
  the chapter).
  - ex: *Abraham Lincoln, president of the United States* (or *President Abraham Lincoln of the
    United States*); *President Lincoln*; *the president* · *General Bradley; the general* · *Cardinal
    Newman; the cardinal* · *Governors Ige and Brown; the governors*
  - ex: *Elizabeth Warren, senator from Massachusetts* (or *Senator Elizabeth Warren of
    Massachusetts*); *Senator Warren*; *Warren*; *the senator*
  - Also: "once a title has been given, it need not be repeated each time a person's name is
    mentioned." Many writers avoid a title before a **full** name in formal prose, especially for
    civil, corporate, and academic titles (8.23, 8.28, 8.29).
- **[8.21] Exceptions** — Capitalize titles that follow a name in **promotional or ceremonial**
  contexts (donor lists, corporate-officer lists) and where courtesy or diplomacy calls for it. A title
  used **alone** is capitalized only in a toast, a formal introduction, or **direct address**.
  **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Maria Martinez, Director of International Sales*
  - ex: *Ladies and Gentlemen, the Prime Minister.* · *I would have done it, Captain, but the ship was
    sinking.* · *Thank you, Mr. President.* — but *We thanked the prime minister for her time.*
- **[8.22] Titles in apposition** — Unless a comma separates it from the name, **capitalize a formal
  title used in apposition before a name** (it is normally preceded by an article or adjective that
  limits it). **18th-ed. change:** "This departure from the advice in previous editions aligns with
  common usage (and with 8.20)." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Empress Elisabeth of Austria* · *German Chancellor Olaf Scholz* · *the Argentinian-born
    Pope Francis* · *former President Carter* · *former Presidents Reagan and Ford* ·
    *then-Secretary of State Hillary Clinton* (en dash; see 6.86)
  - ex (contrast): *a meeting with the German chancellor, Olaf Scholz* — comma → lowercase. And
    *the novelist Virginia Woolf* (not a formal title; see 8.31).
- **[8.23] Civil titles** — In formal prose, capitalize only before a name (official government
  literature over-capitalizes and is not the model). Note the standing exception: **Speaker** is "best
  capitalized in all contexts." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the president; President Washington; the presidency; presidential; the Washington
    administration* · *the vice president; Vice President Adams; vice-presidential duties*
  - ex: *the secretary of state; Secretary of State Blinken* or *Secretary Blinken* · *the senator; New
    York Senator Kirsten E. Gillibrand; Senators Gillibrand and Schumer* · *the representative; the
    congresswoman; Congresswoman Kelly or Rep. Robin Kelly (D-IL)* · *the Speaker; Speaker Johnson*
  - ex: *the chief justice; Chief Justice Roberts* · *the associate justice; Justice Kagan; Justices
    Kagan and Sotomayor* · *the governor; Governor Whitmer* · *the mayor; Mayor Johnson* · *the prime
    minister; Rishi Sunak, the British prime minister* · *the chancellor; Chancellor Scholz* · *the
    Lord Privy Seal* (UK; always capitalized)
- **[8.24] Sovereigns and other rulers** — Lowercase most royal titles when they stand alone. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *King Charles; Charles III; the king* (in a British Commonwealth context, *the King*) ·
    *Nero, emperor of Rome; the Roman emperor* · *the shah of Iran* · *Wilhelm II, emperor of Germany;
    Kaiser Wilhelm II; the kaiser* · *the führer* · *Il Duce* (both *I* and *D* capitalized) ·
    *the Holy Roman emperor*
- **[8.25] Military titles** — Capitalize only when part of a person's name; exceptions only to avoid
  ambiguity. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the general; General Ulysses S. Grant, commander in chief of the Union army; General Grant;
    the commander in chief* · *the admiral; Admiral Nimitz, commander in chief of the Pacific Fleet* ·
    *Captain Dominique Jackson, company commander* · *Sergeant Ajay Patel; a noncommissioned officer
    (NCO)* · *the private; Private T. C. Alhambra*
- **[8.26] Quasi-military titles** — Treat police, merchant marine, and Salvation Army ranks exactly
  like military titles: *Frederick Day, Parkdale chief of police; Chief Day*; *Warden Simmons*.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.27] Religious titles** — Same pattern as civil and military titles. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the rabbi; Rabbi Avraham Yitzhak ha-Kohen Kuk; the rabbinate* · *the cantor; Cantor Bard* ·
    *the imam; Imam Shamil* · *the ayatollah; Ayatollah Khomeini* · *the Dalai Lama* (traditionally
    capitalized) *but previous dalai lamas* · *the pope; Pope Francis; the papacy; papal* · *the
    cardinal; John Cardinal Dew* (in formal contexts) or *Cardinal John Dew* · *the archbishop of
    Canterbury; Archbishop Welby* · *the bishop of Toledo; Bishop Thomas* · *the minister; the
    Reverend Shirley Stoops-Frantz* · *the sacred college of cardinals*
- **[8.28] Corporate and organizational titles** — Such titles are **rarely** used as part of a name;
  use the generic term or the bare name. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Mary Barra, chief executive officer of General Motors; the CEO* · *Christie Henry, director of
    Princeton University Press* · *Janice Bayder, superintendent of Coriander Township High School
    District*
- **[8.29] Academic titles** — Follow the civil-title pattern (*Professor Meltzer*, but *Françoise
  Meltzer, professor of comparative literature*). Named professorships are capitalized as part of the
  name. Note: *emeritus*/*emerita* "are honorary designations and do not simply mean 'retired'."
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the provost; Katherine Baicker, provost of the University of Chicago; Baicker* · *the dean;
    Dean Hale* · *Wendy Doniger, Mircea Eliade Distinguished Service Professor Emerita of the History
    of Religions; Professor Doniger*
  - ex: *the professor emeritus* (masc.), *the professor emerita* (fem.), *professors emeriti*,
    *professors emeritae*
- **[8.30] Other academic designations** — Lowercase student-status terms (*freshman* or *first-year
  student*, *sophomore*, *junior*, *senior*) and generic degrees, fellowships, and the like (*a
  master's degree; a doctorate; a fellowship; master of business administration (MBA)*).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.31] Descriptive titles** — Lowercase generic role words (*philosopher, historian, novelist*)
  even before a name — and **never** use one with the surname alone. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the historian Kevin M. Kruse* or *historian Kevin M. Kruse* — but **not** *historian Kruse*;
    contrast *Professor Kruse* (8.29)
  - ex: *the panel's only historian, Kevin M. Kruse, spoke first* (commas required when the name is a
    nonrestrictive appositive; 6.30)
- **[8.32] Civic and academic honors** — Capitalize titles denoting honors **when they follow a
  personal name**: *Roberta Bondar, Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada; the fellows*. (Awards: see
  8.84; abbreviations: 10.24.) **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.33] Titles of nobility** — Capitalize them: unlike offices, they are "an integral and, with rare
  exceptions, permanent part of a person's name." Lowercase the generic element (*duke, earl*) when it
  stands alone as a short form. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Prince William; the Prince of Wales* · *the Duke and Duchess of Windsor* · *the Marquess of
    Bath; Lord Bath* · *Anthony Ashley Cooper, 7th (or seventh) Earl of Shaftesbury; previous earls of
    Shaftesbury* · *Baroness Thatcher; Lady Thatcher* · *Dame Judi Dench; Dame Judi* (**not** *Dame
    Dench*) · *Sir Paul McCartney; Sir Paul* (**not** *Sir McCartney*) · *the duc de Guise*
    (lowercased per French usage) · *Count Helmuth von Moltke* or *Graf Helmuth von Moltke*
- **[8.34] Honorifics** — Capitalize honorific titles and respectful forms of address **in any
  context**: *the Honorable Angus Stanley King Jr.*; *the Right Honourable Justin Trudeau*; *the First
  Gentleman; the First Lady*; *the Queen Mother*; *Pandit Nehru*; *Mahatma Gandhi*; *Her (His, Your)
  Majesty*; *His (Her, Your) Royal Highness*; *the Most Reverend Thomas Anthony Daly*; *Your (Her,
  His) Excellency*; *Mr. President; Madam President*; *Madam Speaker*; *Your Honor*. But lowercase
  *sir, ma'am* and *my lord, my lady*. **[MECHANICAL]**

## Epithets, Kinship Names, and Personifications (8.35–8.38)

- **[8.35] Epithets (nicknames) and bynames** — Capitalize a descriptive word or phrase used as, or as
  part of, a name; a *the* in such a name stays lowercase (except at a sentence start). When the
  epithet is **added** to a name, put it in **quotation marks**, within or after the name — no
  parentheses. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Great Emancipator* (Lincoln) · *the Sun King* (Louis XIV) · *the Wizard of Menlo Park*
    (Edison) · *Stonewall Jackson* · *Old Hickory* · *Catherine the Great* · *Ivan the Terrible* ·
    *the People's Princess* (Diana)
  - ex: *George Herman "Babe" Ruth* · *Earvin "Magic" Johnson* · *Jenny Lind, "the Swedish
    Nightingale"* · *Ivan IV, "the Terrible"*
- **[8.36] Epithets as character names** — In drama and fiction, capitalize epithets or generic titles
  used in place of a character's name. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *John Barrymore performed brilliantly as Chief Executioner.* · *Alice encounters the Red Queen
    and the Mad Hatter.* · *Batman eluded the Joker.*
- **[8.37] Kinship names and the like** — Lowercase a kinship term unless it forms part of a personal
  name or is used **in place of** one; "Some editorial discretion will be required" (*mother, father,
  sister, brother* rarely name; *aunt, uncle* often do). Job titles used in lieu of a name are
  capitalized only if they would also work as a title before a name. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *my father and mother* · *the Bronté sisters* · *I told my mom that this wouldn't take long.*
    but *Please, Mom, this will only take a minute.* · *Our sister Clarice worked as an editor.* but
    *You can always count on Aunt Maud.* · *Where is Grandpa Smith?* · *Ask Coach Wilson.* but *Hey,
    waiter, where's my order?* · *Yes, Doctor, but will this hurt?*
  - ex: *The note referred to a certain Brother Thomas, one of the brothers from the Franciscan
    monastery.* · figurative: *You can say that again, brother.*
- **[8.38] Personifications** — Capitalizing abstractions is "rare in today's writing" and "best
  confined to quoted material." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (quoted verse): "The Night is Mother of the Day, / The Winter of the Spring…" (Whittier)
  - ex (prose, lowercase): *In springtime, nature is at its best.* · *It was a battle between head and
    heart; reason finally won.*

## Ethnic, Socioeconomic, and Other Groups (8.39–8.44)

- **[8.39] Ethnic, national, and regional groups and their adjectives** — Capitalize group names and
  their associated adjectives. **[MECHANICAL]** (with a **[JUDGMENT]** layer below).
  - ex: *African Americans; an African American; African American culture* · *Asian Americans* ·
    *Chicanos; a Chicano; a Chicana* · *French Canadians* · *Hispanics* · *the Hopi people or the Hopi;
    a Hopi person* · *Inuit* (pl.) / *an Inuk* (sing.) · *Irish Travellers (or Mincéirs)* · *Jews; a
    Jewish person or a Jew* · *Latinos; a Latino; a Latina* · *Métis* · *Native Hawaiians and other
    Pacific Islanders* · *the Romani people or the Romani or Roma; a Romani person* · *Pygmy peoples;
    African Pygmies; a Mbuti Pygmy family*
  - **Outdated vs. current names:** *Aboriginal peoples of Australia; Aboriginal Australians* (**not**
    *Aborigines*) · *American Indians; an American Indian* ("preferred to *Native American* by some who
    trace their roots to the Indigenous peoples of the Americas") · *First Peoples; First Nations*
    (Canada) · *Indigenous peoples* (more than one group) vs. *peoples and cultures that are indigenous
    to the Americas* · *people of color; writers of color* (lowercase).
  - **18th-ed. rule on Black / White / Indigenous:** *Indigenous* and *Native*, "though neither is
    derived from a place-name or related proper noun, are capitalized when referring to people."
    *Black* "is now usually capitalized to refer to people." *White* and *Brown* are optional: some
    capitalize "on the principle that the capital letter in both instances signals a person rather than
    a color; others maintain that only *Black* refers to a meaningful (and often shared) cultural
    identity. The decision … is therefore best left up to the writer." Editors should intervene only
    for consistency or to suggest a more specific term. **Noun vs. adjective:** avoid using *Black* and
    similar terms as nouns — write "*a Black person*" or "*Black people*," **not** "*a Black*" or
    "*Blacks*." (See also 5.255–67.)
- **[8.40] Compound nationalities** — **Do not hyphenate** *African American, Italian American, Chinese
  American*, etc.: "since the hyphen does not aid comprehension in such terms … Chicago advises
  omitting it unless a particular author or publisher prefers otherwise." (Table at 7.96 §2.)
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.41] Socioeconomic status** — Lowercase class terms: *the middle class; a middle-class
  neighborhood*; *the upper-middle class*; *the 1 percent*; *blue collar; blue-collar workers*; *the
  aristocracy*; *the proletariat*; *homeless people; people without housing; the unhoused*.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.42] Sexual orientation and gender identity or expression** — Lowercase: *lesbians; gay men;
  bisexual and pansexual people; asexual people; nonbinary people; cisgender (or cis) men and women;
  transgender (or trans) men and women*. *two-spirit* is lowercase by default, but some authors prefer
  **Two-Spirit** "in line with other terms related to Indigenous identity" — query the author.
  **[MECHANICAL]** + **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[8.43] Generation** — Capitalize generational terms **when they include the word *generation***;
  otherwise lowercase. **18th-ed. change** ("This slight departure from previous editions … accords
  with common usage"); define such terms at first use. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Lost Generation* · *the Silent Generation* · *Generation Jones* · *the Me Generation* ·
    *Generation X, Generation Y, Generation Z* (or *Gen X, Y, and Z*); *a Gen Xer* · *Generation
    Alpha, Generation Beta*
  - ex (lowercase): *baby boomer(s); boomers; a baby boom* · *baby buster(s)* · *millennial(s)*
- **[8.44] Ability status or disability** — Lowercase: *wheelchair users*; *people with autism; an
  autistic person*; *neurotypical people; a neurodivergent* (**not** *neurodiverse*) *person;
  neurodiversity*; *blind people (or persons)*; *deafness; a person who is deaf*. **Capitalize *Deaf***
  when it denotes the cultural/linguistic community (*Deaf culture*, primary language ASL) and
  lowercase it for the audiological condition; similarly *deafblind* often appears as **DeafBlind**.
  (Person-first vs. identity-first: 5.261.) **[JUDGMENT]**

## Names of Places (8.45–8.60)

- **[8.45] Place-name references** — Spell place-names per Merriam-Webster's geographic entries, else
  *Britannica*, else the US Board on Geographic Names; prefer frequently updated online sources for
  modern names (CIA *World Factbook* for countries); in historical works use "the form of names
  appropriate to the period." **[JUDGMENT]**

### Parts of the World (8.46–8.51)

- **[8.46] Continents, countries, cities, oceans** — "Entities that appear on maps are always
  capitalized, as are adjectives and nouns derived from them." Lowercase an initial *the* in running
  text — except in a city name. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Asia; Asian* · *Ireland; Irish* · *California; Californian* · *Atlantic Ocean; Atlantic* ·
    *the Netherlands; Dutch* · *the Gambia* · but *The Hague*
- **[8.47] Points of the compass** — Lowercase compass points and derivatives when they indicate mere
  direction or location: *pointing toward the north; a north wind; a northern climate; to fly east; an
  eastward move; in the southwest of France; southwesterly*. (For regions, see 8.48.) **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.48] Regions of the world and national/local regions** — Capitalize region names and a few
  derivatives, but **"If an otherwise generic term is not listed or discussed there … opt for
  lowercase."** Expect "capitalized exceptions based on specific regional, political, or historical
  contexts," and respect an author's strong preference. **[JUDGMENT]** — the chapter's messiest rule;
  the contrasts below are the operative patterns.
  - **Capitalized as regions:** *the East, Eastern, an Easterner*; *the Eastern Seaboard (or Atlantic
    Seaboard), East Coast*; *the Middle East, Middle Eastern*; *the Midwest, Midwestern, a
    Midwesterner*; *the North, Northern, a Northerner*; *the Northeast, the Northwest, Northwestern*;
    *the South, Southern, a Southerner*; *the Deep South*; *the Southeast, the Southwest,
    Southeastern*; *the West, Western, a Westerner*; *the West Coast*; *the Pacific Northwest*; *the
    Far North*; *the Great Plains*; *Northern California; Southern California*; *Central Europe* (as
    the WWI political division); *Eastern Europe* (post-WWII division); *North Africa; Southeast
    Asia*; *South Africa, South African* (the Republic); *the Upper Peninsula* (of Michigan)
  - **Lowercase as direction or generic area:** *eastern Massachusetts*; *central Illinois; central
    France*; *northern Ohio*; *southern Minnesota*; *western Arizona*; *northern Africa*; *southern
    Africa* (the continent's south); *the middle of Texas*; *upstate New York*; *the northern plains*;
    *east, eastern, eastward, to the east*
  - **Idioms and special cases:** *an Alpine village* (in the Alps) but *alpine pastures in the
    Rockies*; *the Arctic, Arctic waters* but metaphorically "an arctic stare" (8.62); *the continent
    of Europe* but *on the Continent*; *Continental cuisine* but *continental breakfast*; *the
    Continental Divide*; *the equator, equatorial climate* but *Equatorial Guinea*; *the tropics,
    tropical* but *the Tropic of Cancer, the Neotropics, Neotropical*; *East Tennessee, Middle
    Tennessee, West Tennessee* (Grand Divisions) as against *eastern Massachusetts*; *the Upper
    Peninsula* of Michigan
- **[8.49] Popular place-names** — Capitalize them; **quotation marks are not needed**; never use one
  where it won't be readily understood. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Badger State* · *the Bay Area, the Bay* · *the Beltway* · *the Bible Belt* · *the Big
    Island* · *the Cape* · *City of Light* · *the Delta* · *the East End* · *the Eternal City* · *the
    Fertile Crescent* · *the Five Towns* · *the Gaza Strip* · *the Gulf* · *the Holy City* · *the Iron
    Curtain / Third World* (may be capitalized for Soviet-era politics; lowercase generically, and
    prefer *developing nations*) · *the Lake District* · *the Left Bank* · *the Loop* (Chicago) ·
    *Midtown* (Manhattan) · *the Old World* · *the Panhandle* · *the Promised Land* · *the Rust Belt* ·
    *Silicon Valley* · *Skid Row* · *the South Seas* · *the South Side* · *the Sunbelt* · *the Twin
    Cities* · *the Upper West Side* · *the Village* · *the West End* · *the Wild West* · *the Windy
    City*
- **[8.50] Urban areas** — Lowercase generic urban terms: *the business district; the inner city; the
  metropolitan area; the tristate area*; but *Greater London* (an official administrative region), and
  a work may honor local usage (*Greater Boston*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.51] Real vs. metaphorical names** — Capitalize the real place, lowercase the metaphor: *Stratford-
  upon-Avon is a mecca for Shakespeare enthusiasts.*; *She is trying to create a utopia for her
  children.* (Also 8.62.) **[JUDGMENT]**

### Political Divisions (8.52–8.53)

- **[8.52] Political divisions** — Capitalize words like *empire, republic, state, province, ward,
  precinct* **when they follow a name** as an accepted part of it; capitalize them before a name in
  **country** names but lowercase them before names below the national level; lowercase them when they
  stand alone (subject to "reasonable exceptions based on specific regional, political, or historical
  contexts"). In the plural, a generic that is part of an official name stays capitalized for both.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Ottoman Empire; the empire* (but *the Empire style*) · *the United States; the republic;
    the Union* (Civil War era) · *the Confederacy* (Civil War era) · *the United Kingdom; Britain*
    (not *the kingdom*) · *the Russian Federation (the Soviet Union); the federation* · *the Fifth
    Republic* · *the Commonwealth of Australia; the commonwealth; the state of New South Wales* · *the
    State of Washington* (the governmental entity) vs. *the state of Washington* · *Jiangxi Province*
    · *Massachusetts Bay Colony; the colony at Massachusetts Bay* · *the thirteen colonies* · *the
    Indiana Territory; the territory of Indiana* · *Lake County; the county of Lake; the county* ·
    *New York City; the city of New York* · *the City* (the old city of London, now the financial
    district — always capitalized) · *Shields Township; the township* · *the Eleventh Congressional
    District; the congressional district* · *the Fifth Ward; the ward*
  - ex (plural): *Lake and Cook Counties*; *the Republics of Indonesia and South Africa*
- **[8.53] Governmental entities** — When a **body** (not the place) is meant, capitalize *State, City,
  Village* as part of the full name: *She works for the Village of Forest Park.*; *That is a City of
  Chicago ordinance.*; but *Residents of the village of Forest Park enjoy easy access to the city of
  Chicago.* **[MECHANICAL]**

### Topographical Divisions (8.54–8.56)

- **[8.54] Mountains, rivers, oceans, islands** — Capitalize the names **and the generic term** when it
  is part of the name; in the plural, capitalize the generic when it belongs to a single name
  (*Hawaiian Islands*) or applies to two or more names, whether it leads (*Mounts Washington and
  Rainier*) or follows (*the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans*). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Walden Pond* · *Lake Michigan; Lakes Michigan and Erie; the Great Lakes* · *the Illinois
    River; the Illinois and Chicago Rivers* · *the River Thames* · *the Nile River Valley; the Nile
    Valley; the Nile Delta* · *the Bering Strait* · *the Mediterranean Sea; the Mediterranean* · *the
    Great Barrier Reef* · *the Hawaiian Islands; but the island of Hawaii (the Big Island)* · *the
    Windward Islands; the Windwards* · *the Iberian Peninsula* · *Cape Verde* · *the Black Forest* ·
    *Mount Washington; Mounts Washington and Rainier* · *the Rocky Mountains; the Rockies* · *Death
    Valley; the Valley of the Kings* · *the Horn of Africa; the Horn*
- **[8.55] Descriptive geographic terms** — Lowercase a generic used descriptively or alone (and note
  the deliberate exception for a named region): *the Amazon basin*; *along the Pacific coast* (but *the
  West Coast*); *the California desert*; *the Hudson River valley*; *the delta* but *the Delta* (as a
  region); *the Indian subcontinent* (descriptive, not a proper name). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.56] Non-English geographic terms** — When a non-English generic is part of a name, **do not add
  the English equivalent**: *the Rio Grande* (not *the Rio Grande River*); *Denali* (not *Mount
  Denali*); *Fujiyama* (not *Mount Fujiyama*); *Mauna Loa*; *the Sierra Nevada* (not *the Sierra
  Nevada Mountains*). **[MECHANICAL]**

### Public Places and Major Structures (8.57–8.60)

- **[8.57] Thoroughfares and the like** — Capitalize names of streets, avenues, squares, parks;
  lowercase a generic used alone, capitalize it as part of a name or of two or more names.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Black Lives Matter Plaza; the plaza* · *Broadway* · *Fifty-Fifth Street; Fifty-Seventh and
    Fifty-Fifth Streets* · *Hyde Park Boulevard; the boulevard* · *Interstate 80; I-80; an interstate
    highway* · *the Ishtar Gate; the gate* · *London Bridge; the bridge* · *Pennsylvania Avenue;
    Pennsylvania and New York Avenues; both avenues* · *Piccadilly Circus* · *the Spanish Steps; the
    steps* · *Tiananmen Square; the square* · *US Route 66; Routes 1 and 2; a state route*
- **[8.58] Buildings and monuments** — Capitalize names; lowercase the generic alone, capitalize it as
  part of a name or of two or more names. Distinguish *the Capitol* (where Congress meets) from *the
  capital* (the city). Major sculpture read as a monument is **not italicized**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Capitol* (where the US Congress meets, as distinct from the *capital* city) · *the
    Chrysler Building; the building; the Empire State and Chrysler Buildings* · *the Houses
    of Parliament; Parliament* (but *a parliament*) · *the Leaning Tower of Pisa; the tower* · *the
    Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial; the memorial* · *the Pyramids* (but *the Egyptian pyramids*) ·
    *the Stone of Scone; the stone* · *the Washington Monument; the monument* · *Westminster Abbey;
    the abbey* · *the White House* · *the Statue of Liberty; the statue* · *Mount Rushmore National
    Memorial* · *the Colossus of Rhodes; the Colossus*
- **[8.59] Rooms, offices, and such** — Capitalize official room names but not numbered designations:
  *the Empire Room* (but *room 421*); *the Amelia Earhart Suite* (but *suite 219*); *the Lincoln
  Bedroom*; *the Oval Office*; *the West Wing of the White House*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.60] Non-English place and building names** — Do not italicize them; an English *the* may
  precede if the original language would use a definite article: *the Champs-Élysées*; *the
  Bibliothèque nationale*; *the Bois de Boulogne*; *the Marktstrasse*; *the Piazza delle Terme* — but
  *Unter den Linden* (**never** preceded by *the*). **[MECHANICAL]**

## Words Derived from Proper Nouns (8.61–8.62)

- **[8.61] Adjectives from personal names** — Capitalize them (*Aristotelian, Jamesian, Machiavellian,
  Shakespearean*). Coin with *-ian* (after a consonant), *-an* (after *e* or *i*), or *-esque*; a first
  name may be added for clarity; "If a name does not seem to lend itself to any such coinage, it is
  best avoided." **[JUDGMENT]**

    *Sartrean* · *Shavian* (Shaw) · *Wollstonecraftian*

  *French dressing* and *French windows* take a capital. "A term listed there in lowercase (even if it
  carries the label 'often capitalized') can usually remain lowercase." **[MECHANICAL]** (dictionary-
  verified) + **[JUDGMENT]**

    *philistine, philistinism* (nonliteral) · *plaster of paris* · *platonic* (nonliteral) ·

    blinds* · *venturi tube* · *vulcanize* · *wiener*
  - **Capitalized:** *Arabic numerals* · *Bordeaux* · *Brie* · *Bunsen burner* · *Burgundy* (wine and

## Names of Organizations (8.63–8.71)

- **[8.63] Legislative and deliberative bodies** — Capitalize full names; lowercase derived adjectives
  and most generic names used alone; **for unlisted generics, opt for lowercase**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the United Nations General Assembly; the assembly* · *the League of Nations; the league* ·
    *the United Nations Security Council; the Security Council; the council* · *the United States
    Congress; the 119th Congress; Congress; congressional* · *the United States Senate; the Senate;
    senatorial; the upper house of Congress* · *the House of Representatives; the House; the lower
    house* · *the Electoral College* · *the Committee on Foreign Affairs; the Foreign Affairs

  royal court) · *executive, legislative, or judicial branch* · *federal; the federal government;
  federal agencies* · *government* · *monarchy* · *parliament, parliamentary* (but *Parliament*, UK) ·
  *state; church and state; state powers*. **[MECHANICAL]**

### Political and Economic Organizations and Movements (8.67–8.68)

- **[8.67] Organizations, parties, alliances, political movements** — Capitalize official names;
  capitalize *party, union, movement* when part of a name; capitalize formal adherents (*a Socialist*,
  *a Republican*); **lowercase names of systems of thought and general adherents** (*an
  eighteenth-century precursor of socialism*; *a communist at heart*) and **metaphorical uses**
  (*fascist parenting techniques*; *nazi tendencies*). **[JUDGMENT]** with a strong consistency
  escape clause:
  > "For consistency, however—as in a work about communism in which the philosophy and its adherents,
  > the political party, and party members and adherents are discussed—capitalizing the philosophy,
  > together with the organization and its adherents, in both noun and adjective forms, will prevent
  > editorial headaches."
  - ex: *the African National Congress party* (*party* is not part of the official name); *the ANC* ·

## Religious Names and Terms (8.92–8.112)

### Deities and Revered Persons (8.92–8.96)

- **[8.92] Capitalize names of deities; lowercase generic *god*/*gods*** — in monotheistic and polytheistic

  - ex: *Allah, Jehovah, Waheguru, Astarte, Kali, Yahweh, Freyja, Mithra, Itzamna, Serapis* — but *a god,
    gods; the Greek god of war (Ares)*; *God, Satan* but *a devil, the devil* (for Satan and others).

- **[8.113] Capitalize titles of armies, navies, air forces, fleets, battalions, companies, corps, and the
  like; lowercase *army*, *navy*, etc. when standing alone, in the collective plural, or outside an official
  title.** Unofficial but well-known names are capitalized too. In a book about the US military, *Army,
  Navy, Air Force* are usually capitalized; for non-US forces, capitalize when a country designation
  precedes (*Luftwaffe*, or *German Air Force*; but *air force* alone). **[MECHANICAL]/[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *American Expeditionary Force; the AEF; Army Corps of Engineers; the corps. Army of the Potomac.
    Fifth Army; the army. First Battalion, 178th Infantry; the battalion; the 178th. Green Berets; Navy
    SEALs. Royal Air Force; RAF; British air force. Royal Canadian Mounted Police; the Mounties; a Mountie.
    Thirty-Third Infantry Division; the Thirty-Third Division; the division. United States Marine Corps; the
    Marine Corps; a marine. Confederate army; Union army; Continental navy; the Resistance; the French
    Resistance; a resistance movement. the Allies; the Axis powers; the Central powers.*
- **[8.114] Capitalize the names of most major wars and revolutions; lowercase the generic term standing
  alone; more recent, unresolved conflicts can usually be lowercased.** **[MECHANICAL]/[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *American Civil War; the War Between the States. American Revolution; the Revolutionary War; the
    revolution. French Revolution; the Revolution (usually capitalized to distinguish 1789 from 1830 and
    1848); revolutionary France. Korean War; the war. Napoleonic Wars. Norman Conquest; the conquest of
    England. Opium Wars. Persian Gulf War or Gulf War. Russian Revolution; the revolution. Seven Years' War.
    Shays's Rebellion. Six-Day War; the June War; the Third (or 1967) Arab-Israeli War. South Sudanese Civil
    War* (but *the civil war in South Sudan*). *Spanish-American War. Vietnam War. War of 1812. Whiskey
    Rebellion. World War I; the First World War; the Great War; the war. World War II; the Second World War;
    World Wars I and II; the First and Second World Wars; the two world wars.* — but *the 2022 Russian
    invasion of Ukraine; the war in Ukraine*.
- **[8.115] Capitalize the names of major battles and campaigns; in more generic descriptions capitalize only
  the proper names.** **18th-ed change: several of these (lowercased in the 17th ed.) are now capitalized to
  align with common usage.** For unlisted names, consult an encyclopedia. **[MECHANICAL]/[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Battle of Britain. Battle of Bunker Hill; Bunker Hill; the battle. Battle of the Bulge (or Battle of
    the Ardennes). Battle of the Little Bighorn. the Blitz. the European Theater of Operations; ETO.
    Operation Devil Siphon. Third Battle of Ypres (or Battle of Passchendaele). Vicksburg Campaign. Western
    Front.* — but *the battle for the Aleutian Islands*.
- **[8.116] Capitalize the specific names of medals and awards** (for civil awards see 8.84).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Croix de Guerre; Distinguished Flying Cross; DFC; Distinguished Service Order; DSO; Medal of Honor*
    (US congressional award; *the medal*) *; Purple Heart; Silver Star; Victoria Cross; VC.*

## Names of Ships and Other Vehicles (8.117–8.119)

- **[8.117] Capitalize the names of ships and other vessels; italicize the specific name.** Roman *USS*/*HMS*
  take the name with no intervening vessel word. Hull numbers go in parentheses at first mention where
  needed to distinguish same-named vessels. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Eagle* (lunar module for Apollo 11) *; Mars Global Surveyor; Mars Polar Lander; the Phoenix Mars
    lander; Phoenix; the Phoenix lander; the Perseverance Mars rover; Perseverance. the space shuttle
    Discovery; the space shuttle. the Spirit of St. Louis. HMS Frolic; the British ship Frolic. SS United
    States; the United States. USS SC-530; the US ship SC-530.* — but *Sputnik 1* (the satellite) roman.
  - ex: *USS Enterprise (CVN-65) was already on its way to the Red Sea.*
- **[8.118] Capitalize but do not italicize makes and classes of aircraft, models of automobiles and other
  vehicles, names of trains or train runs, and names of space programs.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Acela Express; Boeing 787 Dreamliner; Comac ARJ21 Xiangfeng; Concorde; Ford F-150 Lightning;
    Metroliner; Project Apollo; Subaru Impreza.*
- **[8.119] Prefer *it*/*its* (not *she*/*her*) for a vessel.** **[MECHANICAL]** (see also 5.46, 8.78)

## Scientific Terminology (8.120–8.153)

### Scientific Names of Plants and Animals (8.120–8.128)

- **[8.120] Additional resources** — Chicago gives only general guidelines; the authorities are *The CSE
  Manual* (bibliog. 1.1), the **ICN** (algae, fungi, plants) and the **ICZN** (zoology). Virology and some
  other fields differ slightly: follow the field's own standard.
- **[8.121] Italicize Latin species names; capitalize the genus, lowercase the specific epithet** — even when
  the epithet is a proper adjective. Do not italicize phyla, orders, and the like (see 8.127).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The Pleistocene saber-toothed cats all belonged to the genus Smilodon. Many species names, such as
    Rosa caroliniana and Styrax californica, reflect the locale of the first specimens described. The pike,
    Esox lucius, is valued for food as well as sport. For the grass snake Natrix natrix, longevity in
    captivity is ten years.*
- **[8.122] After first use, abbreviate the genus to a single capital letter; double it before a list of
  several species of the same genus** — though repeating the letter with each species is more common. Don't
  abbreviate when species of different genera beginning with the same letter appear in one context.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *…stride durations of both C. draconoides and Uma scoparia do not change…*; *…an analogous phenomenon
    has been noted in the cottonwood, P. deltoides.*; *Among popular species of the genus Cyclamen are CC.
    coum, hederifolium, and persicum* [or, more commonly, *C. coum, C. hederifolium*, and *C. persicum*].
- **[8.123] Italicize a subspecific zoological epithet after the binomial; if the two names are identical the
  first may be abbreviated.** In horticulture, insert roman *subsp.* (or *ssp.*), *var.*, *f.* before the
  epithet. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Noctilio labialis labialis* (or *Noctilio l. labialis*); *Trogon collaris puella*; *Buxus
    microphylla* var. *japonica*; *Hydrangea anomala* subsp. *petiolaris*; *Rhododendron arboreum* f.
    *album*.
- **[8.124] Leave *sp.*, *spp.*, and unattached *var.* roman (not italic).** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Rhododendron* spp.; *Rosa rugosa* var.; *Viola* sp.
- **[8.125] Set the author (describing authority) name in roman, never italic**; abbreviate freely (*L.* =
  Linnaeus; *Mill.* = Miller). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Quercus alba* L.; *Felis leo* Scop.; *Linaria spuria* (L.) Mill. — parentheses mean the species was
    originally described under another genus.
- **[8.126] Mark a plant cross with the multiplication sign × (not the letter x), spaced on both sides**;
  older primary hybrids put × immediately before the epithet, with space only before it. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Magnolia denudata × M. liliiflora* (crossing of species); *Magnolia ×soulangeana* (hybrid name).
- **[8.127] Capitalize but do not italicize divisions above genus — phylum, class, order, family,
  subfamily**; the words *order*, *family*, etc. are lowercase. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Chordata* (phylum); *Chondrichthyes* (class); *Monotremata* (order); *Ruminantia* (suborder);
    *Hominidae* (family); *Felinae* (subfamily).
- **[8.128] Lowercase English derivatives of taxonomic names and treat them as ordinary words.**
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *carnivore(s)* (from Carnivora); *hominid(s)* (Hominidae); *irid(s)* (Iridaceae); *feline(s)*
    (Felinae); *astilbe(s)* (Astilbe); *mastodon(s)* (Mastodon).

### Vernacular Names of Plants and Animals (8.129–8.131)

- **[8.129] In common names, capitalize only proper nouns and adjectives** (per Merriam-Webster).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Dutchman's-breeches; jack-in-the-pulpit; mayapple; Cooper's hawk; rhesus monkey; Rocky Mountain
    sheep.*
- **[8.130] Follow the dictionary for breeds and horticultural categories** — first-listed capitalization in
  Merriam-Webster when in doubt. Note that professional bodies (American Kennel Club, Cat Fanciers'
  Association) capitalize all breed names, including otherwise generic components (*Golden Retriever, Maine
  Coon*). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *German shorthaired pointer; golden retriever; Hereford; Maine coon* or *coon cat; Thoroughbred*
    horse (but *purebred* dog) *; Rhode Island Red; boysenberry; rambler rose.*
- **[8.131] Preserve the fanciful registered names of horticultural cultivars** — they may be trademarks.
  Some horticultural publications put them in single quotation marks with any following punctuation *after*
  the closing quote, and put no punctuation between the Latin name and an English cultivar name.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Peace rose; a Queen of the Market aster.*
  - ex: *The hybrid* Agastache *'Apricot Sunrise', best grown in zone 6, mingles with sheaves of cape fuchsia
    (*Phygelius *'Salmon Leap').*

### Genetic Terms (8.132–8.134)

- **[8.132] Additional resources** — *AMA Manual of Style*, *The CSE Manual*, the **HGNC** human gene
  nomenclature database, the Mouse Genome Database. Guidelines here are minimal.
- **[8.133] Italicize gene names/symbols (including any Arabic numerals in them); set protein symbols in
  roman.** Gene symbols contain no Greek letters or Roman numerals. Human (and other primate) gene symbols
  are ALL CAPS; mouse and rat gene symbols take an initial capital; other organisms vary. **Only a very few
  gene symbols contain hyphens.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: human *BRCA1, GPC3, IGH@* (@ marks a family or cluster), *SNRPN*; mouse *Cmv1, Rom1, Wnt1*
    (a further mouse symbol is OCR-garbled here); *NLP3* (gene symbol) vs. *NLP3p* (encoded protein, note the
    *p* suffix); *GIF* (gene symbol) vs. GIF (gastric intrinsic factor, roman).
  - ex: *HLA-DRB1*, for human leukocyte antigen D-related β chain 1 (a rare hyphenated symbol).
- **[8.134] Italicize the first three letters of an enzyme name (the organism abbreviation); keep the Roman
  numeral series number and any strain letters roman.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Ava*I, *Bam*HI, *Cla*I, *Eco*RI, *Hind*III, *Sau*3AI (italic span shown by the split).

### Geological Terms (8.135–8.137)

- **[8.135] Capitalize formal geological terms in noun and adjective forms; lowercase generic uses.**
  **18th-ed change: Eon, Era, and the like are capitalized when they immediately follow a formal name.**
  Lowercase *ice age* in scientific contexts (cf. *Little Ice Age*; 8.75). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the Archean* (or *the Archean Eon*); *the eon. the Mesoproterozoic* (or *the Mesoproterozoic Era*).
    *the Tertiary Period of the Cenozoic Era. the Paleocene* (or *the Paleocene Epoch*). *the
    Pleistocene-Holocene boundary* (*boundary* is descriptive; en dash per 6.83). *the Illinoian Glacial
    Stage* or *the Illinoian glaciation.*
- **[8.136] Capitalize the modifiers *early*, *middle*, *late* in formal use, lowercase them informally.**
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Early Archean; Middle Cambrian; early Middle Cambrian; Late Quaternary; in late Pleistocene times.*
- **[8.137] Capitalize formal stratigraphic names** (for prehistoric cultural terms see 8.75).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Fleur de Lys Supergroup; Niobrara Member; Ramey Ridge Complex; Morrison Formation.*

### Astronomical Terms (8.138–8.143)

- **[8.138] Additional resources** — *The CSE Manual* and the website of the International Astronomical Union.
  The paragraphs that follow are general guidance only.
- **[8.139] Capitalize the names of galaxies, constellations, stars, planets, and such** (for *earth*, *sun*,
  *moon* see 8.141–42). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Aldebaran; Alpha Centauri* or *α Centauri; the Big Dipper* or *Ursa Major* or *the Great Bear;
    Cassiopeia's Chair; the Crab Nebula; 85 Pegasi; Halley's Comet* (or *Comet Halley*) *; the Magellanic
    Clouds; the Milky Way; the North Star* or *Polaris, polestar; Saturn* — but *the solar system* lowercase.
- **[8.140] Designate catalog objects by catalog name (often abbreviated) plus number.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Bond 619; Lalande 5761; Lynds 4251* or *L 1251; NGC 6165.*
- **[8.141] Lowercase *earth* in nontechnical senses** (after *the*, or in idioms like *down to earth*, *move
  heaven and earth*); **capitalize it as the proper name of the planet**, especially alongside other planets,
  usually without *the*. **[MECHANICAL]/[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Some still believe the earth is flat.* / *Where on earth have you been?* — but *The astronauts have
    returned successfully to Earth.* / *Does Mars, like Earth, have an atmosphere?*
- **[8.142] Lowercase *sun* and *moon* in nontechnical contexts; always lowercase in the plural.** Some
  astronomy publications capitalize them as proper nouns. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The moon circles the earth, as the earth circles the sun.* / *Some planets have several moons.*
- **[8.143] Do not capitalize merely descriptive terms for celestial objects or phenomena.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *aurora borealis* or *northern lights; interstellar dust; gegenschein; the rings of Saturn.*

### Medical Terms (8.144–8.147)

- **[8.144] Additional resources** — *AMA Manual of Style*, *The CSE Manual*.
- **[8.145] Lowercase names of diseases, syndromes, procedures, and anatomical parts except for proper names
  in the term; capitalize acronyms and initialisms.** Possessive eponyms (*Alzheimer's, Down's, Hodgkin's*)
  may be preferred in a general context though less common in medical literature. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *acquired immunodeficiency syndrome* or *AIDS; Alzheimer disease; computed tomography* or *CT;
    coronavirus disease 2019* or *COVID-19; Down syndrome; finger-nose test* (or *finger-to-nose test*; 6.83)
    *; islets of Langerhans; non-Hodgkin lymphoma; severe acute respiratory syndrome* or *SARS; ultrasound;
    ultrasonography.*
- **[8.146] Treat names of infectious organisms like other scientific names; leave the common and
  condition-based forms roman and lowercase except for a proper noun.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Microorganisms of the genus* Streptococcus *are present in the blood of persons with streptococcal
    infection.* / *The larvae of* Trichinella spiralis *are responsible for the disease trichinosis.* / *The
    Ebola virus* (species *Zaire ebolavirus*) *derives its name from the Ebola River.*
- **[8.147] Lowercase generic drug names and prefer them; capitalize brand names, often in parentheses after
  the first use of the generic.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The patient takes weekly injections of interferon beta-1a (Avonex) to control their multiple
    sclerosis.*

### Physical and Chemical Terms (8.148–8.153)

- **[8.148] Additional resources** — American Institute of Physics author resources, *Physical Review
  Letters*; for chemistry the *ACS Guide to Scholarly Communication*.
- **[8.149] Lowercase the names of laws and theories except for proper names attached to them** —
  capitalization may be applied to follow common usage or avoid ambiguity. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Avogadro's hypothesis* (or *Avogadro's law*) *; Boyle's law; (Einstein's) general theory of
    relativity; Newton's first law* — but *the Big Bang theory* capitalized.
- **[8.150] Lowercase spelled-out chemical names; capitalize element symbols and set them without periods,
  with atom counts as subscripts.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *ozone; O₃. sulfuric acid; H₂SO₄. sodium chloride; NaCl. tungsten carbide; WC.*
- **[8.151] Place the mass number as a left superscript in formal chemistry, or after a hyphen in full size
  for a general audience.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: ¹⁴C (formal) vs. *C-14* or *carbon-14*; ²³⁸U (formal) vs. *U-238* or *uranium-238*.
- **[8.152] Spell radiations as follows** — Greek-letter forms for scientific use, spelled-out forms in
  nonscientific contexts; hyphenate the attributive adjective. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *β-ray* (noun or adj.) or *beta ray*; *γ-ray* or *gamma ray*; *x-ray* (noun, verb, adj.); *cosmic ray*
    (noun) but *cosmic-ray* (adj.); *ultraviolet ray* (noun) but *ultraviolet-ray* (adj.).
  - The verb *x-ray* is acceptable in general contexts but is not normally used in scholarly medical
    literature — write *obtain an x-ray image*, or *a radiograph*, or *subject something to x-ray analysis*.
- **[8.153] Use the traditional American spellings *meter*, *liter*, etc.** Some US business, government, and
  professional organizations have adopted *metre*, *litre*. Unit abbreviations: 10.57–65. **[MECHANICAL]**

## Brand Names and Trademarks (8.154–8.155)

- **[8.154] Capitalize a trademarked name if you must use it; better, substitute the generic term.** Omit ®
  and ™ wherever possible (no legal requirement to use them); if one must appear at the end of a product
  name, place it **before** any period or comma. Company restrictions ("Kleenex facial tissue," no verbing)
  are not legally binding. Merriam-Webster already lists lowercase verbs *google*, *photoshop*, *xerox*.
  **[MECHANICAL]/[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex (trademark → preferred generic): *Bufferin* → *buffered aspirin*; *Post-it Note* → *sticky note*;
    *Coca-Cola, Coke* → *cola, soda*; *Pyrex* → *heat-resistant glassware*; *Google* → *search engine,
    search*; *Scotch tape* → *adhesive tape*; *Jacuzzi* → *whirlpool bath*; *Kleenex* → *(facial) tissue*;
    *Sharpie* → *permanent marker*; *Levi's* → *jeans*; *Vaseline* → *petroleum jelly*; *Photoshop* →
    *image-editing software*; *Xerox* → *photocopier, copy*; *Ping-Pong* → *table tennis*; *Zoom* →
    *videoconference*.
  - Fiction writers may use trademarked names freely provided the use is not defamatory (4.77); lowercase is
    occasionally allowed for creative license (as in a fictionalized text-message exchange), but
    capitalization is the best choice in most contexts.
- **[8.155] Leave intercaps alone; add an initial capital to an all-lowercase name.** A name with a lowercase
  first letter and an internal capital (*iTunes*, *iPhone*, *eBay*) need not be capitalized at the start of a
  sentence or heading — the internal capital already signals a proper noun; mid-word capitals
  (*PowerPoint*, *AccuWeather*) stay as is. **But a name that is all lowercase gets an initial capital even
  mid-sentence** (*Mac Mini*, *Mini*). In all-caps setting these distinctions are overridden (*POWERPOINT*);
  with caps + small caps they are preserved (*IPHONE*). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *iTunes is both an app and a media service.* / *Does your iPhone have an AccuWeather app?* /
    *PowerPoint has become virtually synonymous with presentation software.* — but *The Mac Mini is a good
    solution for cluttered workspaces.*

## Software and Devices (8.156)

- **[8.156] Set names of apps, operating systems, and devices in roman, without quotation marks** (but see
  8.192 for video games); **reflect the capitalization used by the software itself** (consult a help menu or
  user's guide if in doubt) — though an apparent lowercase preference may be overridden (*Mac Mini*). Generic
  references are ordinary text. Key/menu/command typography: 7.81–86. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *macOS Monterey; Mac Mini* (contrary to corporate usage) *; Mac. Windows 11; HP desktop computer;
    notebook computer; PC. Microsoft Word; Apple Pages for macOS; LibreOffice Writer; Google Docs;
    word-processing program; word processor. iOS 16; iPhone; the Maps app for iOS 16; the Ring/Silent switch.
    Apple Watch; an Android watch; a smartwatch. Firefox; a browser; the Firefox app for Android. Kindle; the
    Kindle app for Apple devices.*
  - ex: *The iPhone's Clock app includes a stopwatch, a countdown timer, and an alarm.* / *Does your phone
    have a clock app?* / *I prefer* The New Yorker*'s iPhone app to the printed magazine.*

## Titles of Works (8.157–8.204)

- **[8.157] Scope of the section** — 8.157–204 govern titles *as mentioned or cited in text and notes* for
  books, journals, newspapers, websites; shorter works (stories, poems, articles); divisions of longer works
  (parts, chapters, sections); unpublished works (dissertations, lectures); plays and films; radio and TV
  programs; musical works; artworks. Bibliographic citation form: chs. 13–14. Non-English titles: 11.8–12.

### Capitalization, Punctuation, and Italics (8.158–8.169)

- **[8.158] Capitalize titles in title case (formerly "headline style"), whatever the source's cover styling.**
  Cover/title-page aesthetics may deviate for design reasons, but a title *mentioned or cited* is adjusted to
  Chicago style. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.159] Sentence case: capitalize only the first word of the title, the first word of the subtitle, and
  words that would normally be capitalized.** Used in library catalogs, many news headlines, some journals'
  reference lists; **recommended for most titles from other languages** (11.8); may be used for subheads
  (2.22), useful when headings are long or contain internally capitalized terms (species names). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Science periodicals in nineteenth-century Britain: Constructing scientific communities.* /
    *Crossing* Magnolia denudata *with* M. liliiflora *to create a new hybrid: A success story.*
  - But **title case, not sentence case, is applied to an English-language title mentioned in text** no
    matter how the source styles it: *The essays collected in the book Science Periodicals in
    Nineteenth-Century Britain . . .*
- **[8.160] TITLE CASE — the seven numbered rules.** "The conventions of title case … are governed mainly by
  emphasis and grammar. The following rules, though occasionally arbitrary, are intended primarily to
  facilitate the consistent styling of titles." **Chicago now capitalizes prepositions of five letters or
  more** (18th-ed change). **[MECHANICAL at word-class level]**

| # | Rule (near-verbatim) | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Capitalize the first and last words in titles and subtitles (but see rule 7) and all other **major words** — nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and *some* conjunctions (but see rule 4) | first/last word; nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, subordinating conjunctions (*While*, *Than*, *Because*, *If*) |
| 2 | Lowercase the articles **the, a, an** | articles |
| 3 | Lowercase **prepositions of fewer than five letters**, except (a) when used adverbially or adjectivally, (b) when part of a Latin expression used adjectivally/adverbially, (c) in rare cases a short preposition paired with a longer one. The five-letter rule includes abbreviations (*Versus* capped, *vs.* not) | *at, by, for, in, of, off, on, out, per, to, up, via* lowercase; *About, Above, After, Against, Among, Before, Between, Despite, During, Through, Under, Until, Within, Without* capitalized |
| 4 | Lowercase the common coordinating conjunctions **and, but, for, or, nor** | those five only |
| 5 | Lowercase **to** — as preposition *and* as part of an infinitive — and lowercase **as** in any grammatical function | *to Run, to Hide; The Editor as Anonymous Assistant* |
| 6 | Lowercase the part of a proper name that would be lowercased in text, such as **de** or **von** | *da Vinci, de Beauvoir, van Gogh* |
| 7 | Lowercase the second part of a species name, **even if it is the last word** in a title or subtitle, including *sp.*/*spp.* | *Acipenser fulvescens; Homo sapiens* |

  - The same conventions generally apply to names of organizations and companies (elsewhere in ch. 8).

- **[8.161] Title-case examples (numbers refer to the rules above).** **[MECHANICAL — use as a test set]**
  - ex: *Mnemonics That Work Are Better Than Rules That Do Not* / *Singing While You Work*
  - ex: *A Little Learning Is a Dangerous Thing* (2)
  - ex: *Four Theories Concerning the Gospel According to Matthew* (2, 3)
  - ex: *"The Trouble with Tribbles": Much Ado About Nothing?* (3)
  - ex: *The Case For and Against AI* (3 — *For* capitalized only because it is paired with *Against*)
  - ex: *Writing Under a Deadline and Without an Editor* (2, 3, 4)
  - ex: *Taking Down Names, Spelling Them Out, and Typing Them Up* (3, 4 — *Out*/*Up* adverbial)
  - ex: *Tired but Happy* (4)
  - ex: *The Editor as Anonymous Assistant* (5)
  - ex: *From* Homo erectus *to* Homo sapiens*: A Brief History* (3, 7)
  - ex: *Defenders of da Vinci Fail the Test: The Name Is Leonardo* (2, 3, 6)
  - ex: *Sitting on the Floor in an Empty Room* (2, 3) — but *Turn On, Tune In, and Enjoy* (3, 4)
  - ex: *Ten Hectares per Capita* — but *Landownership and Per Capita Income* (3)
  - ex: *Progress in In Vitro Fertilization* (3 — Latin preposition capitalized)
- **[8.162] HYPHENATED COMPOUNDS IN TITLE CASE — three rules.** **18th-ed change: the second element of a
  prefixed compound (*Anti-Intellectual*, *Pre-Authorization*) is now capitalized**, a departure from
  previous editions. **[MECHANICAL]**

| # | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Always capitalize the first element | *Self-Sustaining, Cross-Stitching, Record-Breaking* |
| 2 | Capitalize any subsequent element **unless** it is an article, a preposition of fewer than five letters, a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, for, or, nor*), or a modifier like *flat*/*sharp* after a musical key symbol. **Extends to prefixed compounds.** | *Under-the-Counter, Out-of-Fashion, Bed-and-Breakfast, Medium-Sized, Anti-Intellectual, Pre-Authorization, Non-English-Speaking, E-Commerce; Lying-In* (*In* is adverbial) *; E-flat Concerto* |
| 3 | Capitalize the second element of a hyphenated spelled-out number or simple fraction | *Twenty-One, Twenty-First-Century, Ninety-Fifth, Two-Thirds* |

  - ex: *A History of the Chicago Lying-In Hospital* (2; *In* functions as an adverb, not a preposition)
  - ex: *A Two-Thirds Majority of Non-English-Speaking Representatives* (2, 3)
  - **Rejected alternative:** under a simplified practice "not recommended by Chicago," only the first element
    and any subsequent proper noun/adjective are capitalized.
- **[8.163] A title that is a direct quotation of a sentence or independent clause may be set in title case**
  even when long. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *"I Will Not Allow Books to Prove Anything": Establishing Truth in Fiction.*
- **[8.164] ITALICS vs. QUOTATION MARKS — decided by the type of work, not by length or importance.** Books
  and periodicals italicized (8.170); articles, chapters, and other shorter works roman + double quotation
  marks (8.179). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Many editors use* The Chicago Manual of Style. / *Refer to the article titled "A Comparison of MLA
    and APA Style."*

- **[8.165] When italics are unavailable** (e.g., some social platforms), distinguish such titles **by
  capitalization alone**, and keep an initial *The* capitalized (*The Chicago Manual of Style; The New York
  Times* — but *the Los Angeles Times*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.166] A subtitle always begins with a capital letter**, in title case or sentence case. On a title page
  the subtitle may be distinguished by typeface alone, but in text it is separated from the title by a
  **colon**; when an **em dash** is used instead, the first word after it is usually **lowercased**. (General
  colon capitalization — capital after a colon that introduces a complete sentence, 18th-ed change — is
  6.67; this rule is that principle's title-specific application.) **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Manuals of Style: Guidelines, Not Strangleholds* (title case) / *Tapetum character states:
    Analytical keys* (sentence case) — but *Chicago—a Metropolitan Smorgasbord*.
- **[8.167] Preserve the title's original spelling (including non-Latin characters) and hyphenation**,
  whatever the surrounding style. **Permissible changes only:** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - capitalization changed to title case (8.160) or sentence case (8.159);
  - a colon added when title and subtitle were distinguished by typeface alone (unless the main title ends in
    a question mark or exclamation point; 13.98);
  - a comma omitted at a line end on the title page restored, including one before a date on its own line —
    but add a serial comma only if the work itself uses serial commas (6.19);
  - a dash retained but converted to an unspaced Chicago em dash (6.91); a semicolon or period between title
    and subtitle may usually become a colon (for two subtitles, 13.92);
  - a hyphen in a number or range changed to an en dash (6.83), likewise a hyphen next to an open compound
    (6.86);
  - quotation marks adjusted per 12.7, item 1;
  - **&** changed to *and*, or, more rarely, a numeral spelled out (13.90).
- **[8.168] A title is a singular noun and always takes a singular verb; punctuation belonging to a title does
  not govern the surrounding text** — except that a sentence-ending period is omitted after a title that ends
  in ? or ! (6.133). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The Waves is not a typical novel.* (singular verb despite plural title)
  - ex: *Her role in Play It Again, Sam confirmed her stature.* (no comma after *Sam*)
  - ex: *Three stories she never mentioned were "Are You a Doctor?," "The Library of Babel," and "The Diamond
    as Big as the Ritz."* (comma after the first title in spite of its question mark)
- **[8.169] For old-fashioned double titles joined by *or*, reproduce the punctuation on the title page; if
  unavailable, prefer the simpler form.** **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *The Tempest, or The Enchanted Island* (Shakespeare) — but *Moby-Dick; or, The Whale* (Melville).

### Books and Periodicals (8.170–8.178)

- **[8.170] Italicize book and periodical titles in title case** (some publications require sentence case in
  reference lists). A book cited in full in a note may be shortened in text (e.g., drop the subtitle). Short
  titles in notes: 13.33. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[8.171] Drop an initial *a*, *an*, or *the* from a book title in running text only when it does not fit
  the syntax**; when in doubt, or if the article is indispensable, retain it. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Fielding, in his introduction to* The History of Tom Jones, a Foundling*, announces himself as a
    professional author.* → *Fielding's* History of Tom Jones*. . .*
  - ex: *That dreadful* Old Curiosity Shop *character, Quilp . . .* — but *In* The Old Curiosity Shop*,
    Dickens . . .*
- **[8.172] Include and capitalize an initial *The* that belongs to a periodical's title — italicized with
  it.** **18th-ed change** (reflects the publications' own usage; makes periodicals consistent with book
  titles). In source citations and index entries the initial *The* may be omitted (14.69, 14.91, 15.56,
  15.57). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *I prefer the paper editions of* The Washington Post *and* The New York Times. / *I read the* Los
    Angeles Times *during the week and the* Chicago Tribune *on Sundays.* / *She reads the* Journal of Labor
    Economics *at work and* The American Naturalist *at home.* / *Do you get your information from* The Wall
    Street Journal *or from* Forbes*?*
  - When the surrounding syntax claims the article, do not italicize it: *The letter objected to the
    Washington Post reporter's sources.*
  - When a periodical title is shortened, treat the initial *the* as surrounding text: *We subscribe to the
    Post.* / *The Times bought Wordle in 2022.*
  - Non-English titles keep the article in the original language **only if it is officially part of the
    title**: *We read* Le Monde *and* Die Zeit *while traveling in Europe* — but *Did you see the review in
    the* Frankfurter Allgemeine*?*
- **[8.173] Italicize a word like *magazine*, *journal*, or *review* only when it is part of the official
  title**; otherwise treat it as surrounding text. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *I read it both in* Time *magazine and in* The Wall Street Journal. / *The archives included copies
    of* Ebony *and* Life *magazines from the 1950s.* — but *His article was reprinted in* The New York Times
    Magazine.
- **[8.174] Do not italicize a periodical title when it is part of the name of a building, organization, or
  prize.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the* Los Angeles Times *Book Prize;* Chicago Defender *Charities;* Tribune *Tower.*
- **[8.175] Inside an italicized title, keep italics on anything that would be italic in running text** — a
  word as a word, a foreign word, a genus name, a ship name — rather than reversing to roman. **18th-ed
  change** (makes the boundaries of the title clearer). **But a title of a work *within* a title goes in
  quotation marks**, even if it is italic on the title page. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Y Is for Yesterday* (Grafton) — the *Y* stays italic.
  - ex: *The Big E: The Story of the USS Enterprise* — *Enterprise* stays italic; **not** roman.
  - ex: *Live from New York: An Uncensored History of "Saturday Night Live."*
- **[8.176] Never use a title to stand for its subject.** **[JUDGMENT — high value for writers]**
  - ex: *Dostoevsky wrote a book about crime and punishment.* (not *about Crime and Punishment*)
  - ex: *In their book* The Craft of Translation*, Biguenet and Schulte . . .* (not *In their book on* The
    Craft of Translation)
  - ex: *Edward Wasiolek's book on Dostoevsky's* Crime and Punishment *is titled "Crime and Punishment" and
    the Critics.*
- **[8.177] Treat multivolume works like single-volume works**; named volume titles likewise. Lowercase and
  abbreviate *volume* in parentheses and notes; capitalize (and never abbreviate) it only if it is part of
  the title. Citation form: 14.18–24. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The Day of the Scorpion*, volume 2 of *The Raj Quartet*.
  - ex: *Art in an Age of Counterrevolution, 1815–1848* (vol. 3, *A Social History of Modern Art*).
- **[8.178] Capitalize series and edition titles but do not italicize them; capitalize *series*/*edition*
  only when part of the title.** Special case: **italicize a series title if it is based on the title of one
  of the books** in the series (or if the books were published together under that series title); **use
  roman if the series is named for a character, place, or other element.** Numbered or named editions of a
  cited publication are usually not part of the title: roman and lowercase. **[MECHANICAL]/[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *the Loeb Classical Library; a Modern Library edition;* Late Editions: Cultural Studies for the End
    of the Century*; the* Crime and Justice *series; a book in the* Heritage of Sociology *Series.*
  - ex: Twilight*, the first novel in Meyer's* Twilight *series* (series italicized — named after the book).
  - ex: Prince Caspian*, the second book in C. S. Lewis's* The Chronicles of Narnia; The Fellowship of the
    Ring*, the first novel in the* Lord of the Rings *series.*
  - ex: Beezus and Ramona*, the first book in the* Ramona *series;* The Other Wind*, the final novel in the*
    Earthsea *series; the* Harry Potter *books* (roman — named for a character).
  - ex: Black's Law Dictionary, *10th ed.; Kuhn*, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, *50th anniversary
    ed.* — but *Hayek*, The Constitution of Liberty: The Definitive Edition (*The Definitive Edition* is part
    of the title).

### Articles in Periodicals and Parts of Books (8.179–8.182)

- **[8.179] Set article titles, chapter titles, section titles, and short-story and essay titles in roman, in
  quotation marks.** Normally italicized material stays italic inside an article title; a term already quoted
  in the original title takes **single** quotation marks. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Claire Curtis's article "Remainders of the American Century" appeared in the journal* American
    Political Thought.
  - ex: *In chapter 3 of* The Footnote*, "How the Historian Found His Muse," Anthony Grafton explores . . .*
  - ex: *"Sweat," a story by Zora Neale Hurston, was first published in 1926.*
  - ex: *The article "Schiller's 'Ode to Joy' in Beethoven's Ninth Symphony" received unexpected attention.*
  - ex: *Neuberger's "Sergei Eisenstein's* Ivan the Terrible *as History" calls the filmmaker's approach to
    history "serious and nuanced."*
  - **Departments and columns:** roman, **no** quotation marks, initial *the* lowercase mid-sentence: *In the
    opening piece for the Talk of the Town, Steve Coll investigates . . .*
- **[8.180] Italicize (don't quote) separate works reprinted in one volume** (collected works of an author).
  **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: *Baldwin's* If Beale Street Could Talk*, included in the collection* Later Novels*, . . .*
- **[8.181] Lowercase and set roman such generic part-words as *foreword*, *preface*, *acknowledgments*,
  *introduction*, *appendix*, *bibliography*, *glossary*, *index*.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The author states in her preface that . . .* / *For further documentation, see the appendix.* / *Full
    details are given in the bibliography.*
- **[8.182] Lowercase and spell out *chapter*, *part*, *appendix*, *table*, *figure*, etc. in text** (they may
  be abbreviated in parenthetical references); use Arabic numerals regardless of the original. Exceptions:
  references within a work to its own Roman-numeral parts, and 9.28/9.30. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *This matter is discussed in chapters 4 and 5.* / *The Latin text appears in appendix B.* / *The range
    is presented numerically in table 4.2 and diagrammed in figure 4.1.* / *These connections are illustrated
    in table A3.* / *Turn to section 5(a) for further examples.*

### Poems and Plays (8.183–8.186)

- **[8.183] Quote most poem titles; italicize a very long poetic work, especially one constituting a book.**
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Langston Hughes's poem "I, Too" in his 1926 collection* The Weary Blues. / *Dante's* Inferno. /
    *Eliot's* The Waste Land. — but *Shakespeare's sonnets; Sonnet 18* (roman, no quotes).
- **[8.184] When a poem is referred to by its first line, capitalize it in sentence case even if the
  original's first word is lowercase** — but keep any words capitalized in the original capitalized.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *E. E. Cummings, in "My father moved through dooms of love," . . .* (*my* is lowercase in the
    original)
  - ex: *"Shall I compare thee to a Summer's day?"* (Sonnet 18)
- **[8.185] Italicize titles of plays regardless of length.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Shaw's* Arms and the Man*, in volume 2 of his* Plays: Pleasant and Unpleasant.
- **[8.186] Lowercase words for divisions of plays and long poems; neither italicize nor quote them; use
  Arabic numerals.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *canto 2; stanza 5; act 3, scene 2.*

### Fairy Tales and Nursery Rhymes (8.187)

- **[8.187] Treat folktales, fables, fairy tales, and nursery rhymes like short poems** (roman + quotes);
  **italicize when the fairy tale is published as a book, play, or opera.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *"Aladdin" is arguably the most well-known tale in* A Thousand and One Nights.
  - ex: *"Rumpelstiltskin" originally appeared in the Grimm brothers'* Children's and Household Tales.
  - ex: *Everybody knows at least one verse of "Jack and Jill."*
  - ex: Ella Enchanted *is a retelling by Gail Carson Levine of "Cinderella."* / *The opera* Hansel and Gretel
    (*Hänsel und Gretel*) *is based on the fairy tale of the same name.*

### Pamphlets, Reports, and Forms (8.188–8.189)

- **[8.188] Italicize titles of pamphlets, reports, and other freestanding publications**, like books
  (also 8.170, 14.117). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Paine's* Common Sense*, first published anonymously . . .*
  - ex: E-Stats 2020: Measuring the Electronic Economy*, a report on e-commerce published by the US Census
    Bureau.*
- **[8.189] Capitalize titled or numbered forms according to the usage in the form itself**, and follow the
  document's own wording (shortening if necessary); treat standards the same way (but see 14.159).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Form 1040-ES, Estimated Tax for Individuals* (or *1040-ES*); *DHS TRIP Traveler Inquiry Form* (or
    *DHS TRIP*).
  - ex: *United States Census 2020; the Census* (but *a census form*); *the Unicode Collation Algorithm; the
    XML standard; XML 1.1, 2nd ed.*

### Unpublished Works (8.190)

- **[8.190] Set titles of unpublished works — theses, dissertations, manuscripts in collections, unpublished
  speech transcripts — in roman, capitalized as titles, in quotation marks. Manuscript collections take no
  quotation marks. A not-yet-published book under contract may be italicized but must be followed by
  (*forthcoming*) or (*in press*).** Speeches: 8.77. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *In a master's thesis, "Charles Valentin Alkan and His Pianoforte Works," . . .*
  - ex: *"A Canal Boat Journey, 1857," an anonymous manuscript in the Library of Congress Manuscripts
    Division, describes . . .*
  - ex: *Letters and other material may be found in the Collis P. Huntington Papers at the George Arents
    Library of Syracuse University.*
  - ex: *Gianfranco's* Fourth Millennium *(forthcoming) continues this line of research.*

### Movies, Television, Radio, and Podcasts (8.191)

- **[8.191] Italicize titles of films, film series, and television, radio, and podcast programs and series;
  quote a single episode.** Number sequels as in the source, preferring Arabic numerals if in doubt (9.45).
  Set network, channel, and streaming-service names in roman. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Black Panther*, a film produced by Marvel Studios. /* The Godfather, Part II. /* The Hunger Games; The

  - ex: Dr. No*; *the James Bond franchise. /* Sesame Street *on PBS. /* The Ten O'Clock News*, WGBH's

  - ex: *"The Alibi," the first episode in the podcast* Serial*, a* This American Life *spin-off.*
  - ex: *"How to Pay for College," an episode in the series* How to College*, on YouTube's CrashCourse
    channel.*
  - ex: *"Thirsty Bird," the first episode in the second season of the Netflix series* Orange Is the New

- **[8.192] Italicize titles of video games** — treated like movies, as narrative/audiovisual works, and older

### Websites and Blogs (8.193–8.194)

- **[8.193] Set website titles in roman, title case, without quotation marks; lowercase an initial *The*
  except at the start of a sentence.** Quote titled sections, pages, and special features (generic labels
  like *Help* and *Settings* may go bare; when in doubt, use quotes). Works covered elsewhere in the chapter
  keep their treatment online. **Wikipedia is treated as a website** (roman), not as an encyclopedia. When in
  doubt: title case, no quotes, no italics. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Project Gutenberg; Jane Austen's* Emma*, available as an audiobook from Project Gutenberg.*
  - ex: *the Internet Movie Database; IMDb* (note lowercase *b*) *; IMDb's page for* Live and Let Die*;
    "Roger Moore (I)" . . .*
  - ex: *Google; Google Maps; Google Maps Help. Facebook; Twitter or X; Instagram; the Facebook app.*
  - ex: *Wikipedia; Wikipedia's "Let It Be" entry; Wikipedia's entry on the Beatles' album* Let It Be.
  - ex: The Chicago Manual of Style Online*; *the Q&A at CMOS Online; "New Questions and Answers." Britannica;
    the online version of* Encyclopaedia Britannica*. the* Oxford English Dictionary*; the OED Online.*
  - If a site has no formal title, identify it by the responsible entity plus a short form of the URL: *the
    website for Apple Inc.; Apple.com. Microsoft's website; Microsoft.com.*
- **[8.194] Italicize the titles of news websites and blogs** (like periodicals), including an initial *The*;
  **quote the titles of articles and blog posts** (untitled posts may be referred to by date).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Vox; Politico; Breitbart News; The Intercept; HuffPost* (news sites).
  - ex: Wasted Food*; "Food Waste Stump Speech," in* Wasted Food*, a blog by Jonathan Bloom.*
  - ex: *"Billie Eilish Pauses London Show over Fan Safety," by Mark Savage, an article published on BBC
    News* — but *articles published online by Fox News, CNN, and the BBC* (roman).
  - **Principle:** the name of a news channel or company is not the title of a news site; when in doubt, use
    roman.

### Works of Art and Exhibitions (8.200–8.204)

- **[8.200] Italicize titles of paintings, drawings, photographs, statues, and other works of art, including
  titles of series** — whether original, added by someone other than the artist, or translated. **Set works of
  antiquity (creators often unknown) in roman.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Rothko's* Orange Yellow Orange*; Leonardo da Vinci's* Mona Lisa *and* The Last Supper*;* North Dome*,
    one of Ansel Adams's photographs of Kings River Canyon; Hogarth's series of drawings* The Rake's
    Progress*; Michelangelo's* David.
  - ex: *the Winged Victory; the Venus de Milo* (roman).
- **[8.201] Refer to maps by generic description in text; italicize a formal map title.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *a fifteenth-century reconstruction of Ptolemy's world map (ca. AD 150); the* Yu ji tu *(Map of the
    tracks of Yu), from 1136; the* Tabula Hungariae*, Lázár's map of Hungary; Arno Peters's projection of the
