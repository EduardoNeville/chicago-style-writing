# Spelling, italics, and compounds/hyphenation

Source: CMOS ch 7 (rules 7.1-7.96). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch07. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch07

Ranked by how often they bite in ordinary prose. Rule numbers in brackets.

1. **Hyphenate a compound modifier before the noun, open it after** — *a well-read student* / *the student
   is well read*; *high-quality alkylate* / *the neighborhood is middle class* [7.91, 7.96 §2].
2. **Never hyphenate an *-ly* adverb** — *a highly paid analyst*, *a smartly dressed couple* [7.93].
3. **Default to open compounds; add a hyphen only for a dictionary form, a listed analogy, or to prevent
   misreading** — Chicago's "spare hyphenation style" [7.96].
4. **Singular possessives take *'s* even after *s/x/z***: *Kansas's, Dickens's, Marx's, Berlioz's,
   Descartes's, Euripides's*; plurals ending in *s* take the bare apostrophe: *puppies', the Lincolns'*
   [7.16–7.19, 7.22].
5. **Never use an apostrophe to pluralize** — *the Smiths*, *the 1990s*, *IRAs*, *PhDs*, *dos and don'ts*,
   *ifs, ands, or buts*; letters do take one: *three A's*, *x's and ys*, *p's and q's* [7.9, 7.14, 7.15,
   7.68].
6. **Numbers: hyphenate 21–99 (*twenty-eight*) and spelled-out number + noun before a noun
   (*a 250-page book*, *a three-year-old*), but *50 percent*, *a 10 percent raise*, *three hundred*,
   *on the third floor* stay open** [7.96 §1].
7. **Keep hyphenated compounds hyphenated only where they precede the noun** — *cutting-edge methods* /
   *their approach was cutting edge*; but *fat-free*, *ill-advised*, *first-rate*, *old-fashioned*,
   *short-lived* keep the hyphen in both positions [7.91, 7.92, 7.96 §2–3].
8. **Use suspended hyphens in parallel compounds** — *fifteen- and twenty-year mortgages*, *five-, ten-,
   and twenty-dollar bills*, *over- and underfed cats*; but repeat a closed compound (*overfed and
   overworked*), and don't suspend for a single entity (*a five-by-eight-foot rug*) [7.95].
9. **Italicize a term only when it is a word as a word, an unfamiliar foreign term, a first-occurrence key
   term, or a title; then return to roman** [7.50, 7.55, 7.58, 7.66].
10. **Keep familiar borrowings roman** — *croissants, kaiser, bourgeois, Weltanschauung, a priori, et al.,
    in vitro* — and be consistent within a passage (italicize both or neither of a pair) [7.56, 7.57].
11. **Use italics for emphasis sparingly: never a whole sentence, never a passage, never ALL CAPS in
    formal prose** [7.51, 7.54].
12. **Drop scare quotes from idioms and slang, and from *so-called*** — *So-called child protection
    sometimes fails to protect* [7.60, 7.62].
13. **Lowercase internet terms unless trademarked** — *the internet, the web, website, web page, email*;
    capitalize *Wi-Fi, Ethernet, HTTP (as a protocol name)* [7.85, 7.96 §3].
14. **Prefixes close up** — *nonbinary, prewar, coworker, antiracist, deconstruct* — **but hyphenate before
    a capital or numeral, before a compound, to avoid misreading (*co-op*, *re-creation*), and in double
    prefixes (*re-review*)** [7.96 §4].
15. **Fix the classic apostrophe traps**: *'tis → tis*, *rock 'n' roll*, *for goodness' sake* but
    *for expedience's sake*, *a friend of Nora's* but *a student of Kierkegaard* [7.21, 7.26, 7.30].
16. **Choose *a/an* by sound**: *a hotel*, *an heir*, *an MS treatment* but *a MS in the library*, *a URL*,
    *an EU member*, *an 800 number* [7.32, 7.33].
17. **Pluralize compounds at the head noun** — *fathers-in-law*, *attorneys general* pattern,
    *bachelors of science*, *coups d'état* [7.7, 7.96 §3 *general*, *in-law*].
18. **Century ranges and modifiers**: *the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries*, *from the twentieth to the
    twenty-first century*, *eighteenth-century technologies* [7.8, 7.96 §3 *century*].
19. **Colors hyphenate before the noun only** — *emerald-green tie* / *his tie is emerald green*;
    *black-and-white print* / *the truth isn't black and white* [7.96 §1].
20. **Don't hyphenate proper nouns or open compound nouns used as modifiers** — *United States policy*,
    *high school student*, *guest room access* [7.91].
21. **Set the apostrophe in contractions and possessives as a right single quotation mark (’)** [7.30,
    6.125].
22. **Reword rather than force a possessive or plural onto a quoted or italicized title** — *admirers of
    "Ode on a Grecian Urn"*, *copies of Madame Bovary* [7.12, 7.13, 7.29].
23. **Prefer a hyphen when it prevents ambiguity, drop it when it doesn't** — *much-needed clothing*,
    *re-creation*, *co-op* vs. *public welfare administration* [7.90].
24. **Close compounds that have become single words, and make related pairs consistent** — *online* /
    *on-screen*; *website* / *web page* [7.89, 7.96 §3 *on*, *web*].
25. **CamelCase hashtags; don't break URLs with hyphens** [7.47, 7.86].

## Overview (7.1–7.4)

- **[7.1] Recommended dictionaries** — Spell to the **first-listed** entry in *Merriam-Webster*
  (Merriam-Webster.com, the online update of the *Collegiate* 11th ed.). If a word is not there, use
  *Merriam-Webster Unabridged*; **when the two disagree, follow Merriam-Webster.com** (more frequently
  updated). Only then fall back to another standard dictionary (Dictionary.com / *Random House
  Unabridged*). **[MECHANICAL]** (a unique "correct" spelling per word).
  - The chapter's consistency principle, stated outright: "At least for spelling, one source should be
    used consistently throughout a single work."
- **[7.2] Variant spellings** — Merriam-Webster separates **equal variants** with *or* and marks
  **secondary variants** with *also* (or *or less commonly*, *chiefly British*). Chicago's rule:
  **choose the first-listed term, even among equal variants**, with exactly **two** exceptions:
  1. an author's **discipline-specific** preference is respected (*archeology*, a second-listed equal
     variant of *archaeology*, is preferred by specialists);
  2. an author's preference for a variant that **reflects the word's non-English origin** is respected
     (*Hawai‘i* with the ‘okina over first-listed *Hawaii*; *crépe* over first-listed *crepe* whenever the
     French word is meant).
  - This applies **only to English words listed in an English dictionary** — not to foreign-language
    terms (see 7.55, 11.40).
- **[7.3] Non-US spelling** — In English-language works by non-US authors edited and produced in the US,
  Chicago **converts to American spelling** (*colour → color*, *analyse → analyze*); consistency is
  easier to keep and few authors object. **Leave spelling unchanged in quoted material** (12.7).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.4] Supplementing the dictionary** — Dictionaries don't settle plurals/possessives of hard nouns,
  end-of-line breaks, italics vs. caps vs. quotes for words as words, or hyphenation of compounds —
  hence the rest of the chapter, with the master key placed last for reference (**7.96**).

## Plurals (7.5–7.15)

- **[7.5] Standard plural forms** — Add **s**, or **es** after *ch, j, s, sh, x, z* (*thumbs, churches,
  fixes, boys*). No need to look these up; dictionaries list the hard ones (nouns in *y → ies*, in *o*,
  Latin/Greek loans like *crocus, datum, alumna*, and all irregulars). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.6] Alternative plural forms** — Where Merriam-Webster gives two plurals (*zeros/zeroes*;
  *millennia/millenniums*), **Chicago takes the first**. But **sense may split the plural**: a book has
  two *indexes*, a math expression two *indices*. **[MECHANICAL]** (default = first form).
- **[7.7] Plurals of compound nouns** — Pluralize the **first** element in *-in-law* / *of*-phrases:
  *fathers-in-law, attorneys general* class of forms per dictionary, *courts-martial, coups d'état,
  chefs d'oeuvre, bachelors of science, masters of arts, spheres of influence, child laborers*.
  **[MECHANICAL]** (use common sense when unlisted).
- **[7.8] Plurals for centuries** — **Plural** when ordinals are joined by *and* or a range is expressed
  with *through*; **singular** with *to*, and with *or/nor* alternatives; **singular** in compound
  modifiers. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: the eighteenth and early nineteenth **centuries**; in the fifth through eighth **centuries**
  - ex (wrong → right): from the twentieth to the twenty-first **century**; as of the fifth or sixth
    **century**; eighteenth- and nineteenth-century technologies
- **[7.9] Plurals of proper nouns** — Add **s** or **es**: *Toms, Dicks, and Harrys*; the *Joneses*; the
  *Martinezes*; the *Brunos*; *Sundays*; *Germanys*; *Pakistanis*. Irregulars are dictionary-driven
  (*Romani*, pl. *Romani*). **"An apostrophe is never used to form the plural of a family name"**:
  "The Smiths live here" (not "Smith's"). **[MECHANICAL]** — high-value, high-frequency error.
- **[7.10] Plural form for names of Indigenous groups** — Defer to Merriam-Webster's first-listed form
  absent an author/publisher preference; for unlisted names, use an up-to-date encyclopedia, a
  nation-sponsored website, or consistency with names already used in the text. Some groups use **no
  definite article**; when in doubt keep *the* and add *people* or *Nation* (capitalized).
  - ex: the **Hopi people** (or the Hopi); the **Cherokee Nation**; the **Maori people**; the
    **Haudenosaunee**; the **Seneca and Cayuga Nations**
- **[7.11] Singular form used for the plural** — Names ending in **unpronounced s or x** stay singular:
  *the seventeen Louis of France*; *the two Dumas, father and son*; *two Charlevoix*; *three Margaux*
  (but *two Felixes*). A pronounced *s* may occasionally stay singular by editorial discretion
  (*three Mercedes*). **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
- **[7.12] Plural form of italicized words** — Add the plural ending **in roman**; a title already
  plural may stay unchanged; **when in doubt, rephrase to avoid the plural**. Plural endings of
  *foreign* italic words stay **italic** (*Blume, Blumen*; *cheval, chevaux*; *señor, señores*).
  - ex: two Chicago *Tribunes* and three *Milwaukee Journal Sentinels*; several *Madame Bovarys*
    (better: several copies of *Madame Bovary*); too many *sic*s; but four *New York Times*
- **[7.13] Plural form for words in quotation marks** — Form the plural normally, **without an
  apostrophe**, inside/after the quoted phrase; **Chicago discourages a plural ending after a closing
  quotation mark** — if it's awkward, reword. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: How many more "To be continued**s**" can we expect? (not "To be continued's"); better: How many
    more times can we expect to see "To be continued"?
- **[7.14] Plurals of noun coinages** — Words and hyphenated phrases used as nouns take **s/es**, no
  apostrophe: *ifs, ands, or buts*; *dos and don'ts* (apostrophe only in the contraction *don't*);
  *threes and fours*; *thank-yous*; *maybes*; *yeses and noes*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.15] Plurals for letters, abbreviations, and numerals** — Numerals and abbreviations add **s**
  (*1990s, IRAs, URLs, BSs, MAs, PhDs, vols., eds.*). Letters take **apostrophe + s** — and,
  **in a change from earlier editions, now for uppercase letters too**, to avoid *As, Is, Us* reading as
  words: *A's, R's, x's and ys*. **[MECHANICAL]** — 18th-ed. change.
  - ex: an acronym spelled with two **A's**; the three **R's**; *x's and ys*
  - exceptions: *p./pp.*, *n./nn.*, *MS/MSS*; *pj's* (as Merriam-Webster lists it)

## Possessives (7.16–7.29)

### The General Rule (7.16–7.19)

- **[7.16] Possessive of most nouns** — Singular: add **'s**. Plural ending in *s*: add **apostrophe
  only**. Irregular plurals not ending in *s* take **'s**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: the horse's mouth; a bass's stripes; the virus's toll; puppies' paws; children's literature;
    a flock of sheep's mysterious disappearance
- **[7.17] Possessive of proper nouns, abbreviations, and numbers** — The 7.16 rule **extends to names
  ending in s, x, or z, singular or plural**, and to abbreviations and numbers — i.e. **always 's for
  the singular**: *Kansas's, Alexis's, Darius's, Marx's, Jesus's, Berlioz's, Tacitus's, Borges's,
  Dickens's, Malraux's, Josquin des Prez's*; plurals: *the Lincolns' marriage, the Williamses' new house,
  the Martinezes' daughter, dinner at the Browns'*; *FDR's legacy, HP Inc.'s offerings, Apollo 11's
  fiftieth anniversary*. **Don't form the possessive of an abbreviation that is followed by its
  spelled-out form** (or vice versa). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (wrong → right): ~~IBM's (International Business Machines') long history~~ → the long history of
    IBM (International Business Machines)
- **[7.18] Possessive of words and names ending in unpronounced “s”** — Still **'s** (the *s* is usually
  pronounced in the possessive): *Descartes's three dreams, the marquis's mother, François's efforts,
  Vaucouleurs's assistance, Albert Camus's novels*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.19] Possessive of names like “Euripides”** — Classical names of **two or more syllables ending in
  an *eez* sound** take **'s** (though the extra *s* is not pronounced): *Euripides's tragedies, the
  Ganges's source, Xerxes's armies*. **[MECHANICAL]**

### Exceptions to the General Rule (7.20–7.22)

- **[7.20] Possessive of nouns plural in form, singular in meaning** — Where singular = plural (no
  inflected plural), **apostrophe only**: *politics' true meaning, economics' forerunners, this species'
  first record*. Same for place/organization/publication names ending in *s* even when the entity is
  singular: *the United States' role, Highland Hills' late mayor, Callaway Gardens' former curator, the
  National Academy of Sciences' new policy*. **If ambiguity or awkwardness threatens, reword with *of*.**
  **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
- **[7.21] “For . . . sake” expressions** — Traditional formulations drop the *s*: *for goodness' sake,
  for righteousness' sake*. Otherwise use the normal possessive: *for expedience's sake, for
  appearance's sake, for Jesus's sake*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.22] An alternative practice for words ending in “s”** — The older system of omitting the
  possessive *s* everywhere (*Dylan Thomas' poetry*, *Etta James' singing*, *that business' main
  concern*) is **"not recommended by Chicago"**: it "frequently disregards pronunciation and risks
  appearing inconsistent relative to singular names that do not end in *s*." **[MECHANICAL]**

### Particularities of the Possessive (7.23–7.29)

- **[7.23] Joint versus separate possession** — One thing possessed → possessive on the **last**
  element only (*my aunt and uncle's house*; *Gilbert and Sullivan's Iolanthe*). Discrete things →
  **both** nouns possessive (*my aunt's and uncle's medical profiles*; *Gilbert's or Sullivan's
  mustache*; *New York's and Chicago's transportation systems*). **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
- **[7.24] Compound possessives** — The **final element** takes the possessive, even in the plural:
  *student assistants' responsibilities*, *my daughter-in-law's address*, *my sons-in-law's addresses*.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.25] Possessive to mean “of”** — Genitive expressions standing in for *of*: *in three days' time*
  (or *in three days*), *an hour's delay* (or *a one-hour delay*), *six months' leave of absence* (or
  *a six-month leave of absence*). Reword if ambiguous. **[JUDGMENT]** (see also 7.96 "number + noun")
- **[7.26] Double possessive** — *of* + possessive is **allowed** where "one of several" is implied
  (*a friend of Nora's*, *a student of mine*). **Where the meaning is not literally possessive, drop the
  possessive**: *a student of Kierkegaard* (observer/follower), not *Kierkegaard's*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.27] Possessive versus attributive forms for groups** — Drop the apostrophe **only** in proper
  (usually corporate) names that officially lack one (*Publishers Weekly*, *Diners Club*, *Department of
  Veterans Affairs*), plus a handful of established attributive singulars (*taxpayer association*,
  *consumer group*). **Irregular plurals (*children, women*) must always be possessive**; if in doubt,
  **choose the plural possessive**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: children's rights (or child rights); farmers' market; women's soccer team; boys' clubs;
    veterans' organizations; taxpayers' associations (or taxpayer associations)
  - subtlety: the *Lakers'* game plan (the team's plan) vs. the *Lakers* game (the game featuring them)
- **[7.28] Possessive with gerund** — Use the possessive when the **gerund**, not the preceding noun, is
  the subject/object of the clause; use the plain noun when the *-ing* word is really a **participle**.
  After a preposition the possessive is **optional**. Limit the practice to proper names and pronouns;
  "use with caution." **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: We all agreed that **Jerod's running** away from the tigers had been the right thing to do.
  - ex: None of us actually saw **Jerod running** away from the tigers. (participle, not gerund)
  - ex: She was worried about her **daughter (or daughter's)** going there alone.
- **[7.29] Possessive with italicized or quoted terms** — Set the apostrophe and *s* in **roman** when the
  term is italic; if the last element is plural in form, add **apostrophe only, in roman**. **Do not
  attempt the possessive of a term in quotation marks — reword with *of*.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *The Atlantic*'s editor in chief; *The New York Times*' new fashion editor; *The Great Gatsby*'s
    admirers
  - ex (wrong → right): ~~"Ode on a Grecian Urn"'s admirers~~ → admirers of "Ode on a Grecian Urn"

## Contractions and Interjections (7.30–7.31)

- **[7.30] Contractions** — An **apostrophe (the right single quotation mark, ’, not ‘)** replaces omitted
  letters; irregulars (*won't, ain't*) are dictionary forms. **Colloquialisms take no apostrophe**
  (*gonna, wanna*; *tis*, not *'tis*). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: singin'; govt; **tis** (not *'tis*); dos and **don'ts**; rock **'n'** roll
  - A "contraction" ending in a period (*Dr.*) is an **abbreviation**, not a contraction (10.2).
- **[7.31] Interjections** — Follow Merriam-Webster (*achoo, ugh, er, um, sh*); for unlisted ones, find a
  plausible spelling in literature or invent one (*atchoo!, shhh!*). **Distinguish *oh* from vocative *O*
  (always capitalized)**; *oh* is capitalized only at sentence start. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: Where, **oh** where, have you been? / **Oh!** It's you! / but "Thine arm, **O** Lord, in days of
    old . . ."

## “A” and “An” (7.32–7.33)

- **[7.32] “A” and “an” before “h”** — Use **a** before a **pronounced** *h* (*a hotel, a historical
  study*); **an** before a silent *h* (*an honor, an heir*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.33] “A” and “an” before abbreviations, symbols, and numerals** — Choose by **sound of the spoken
  form**, not the letter: *an MS treatment* (em ess) but *a MS in the National Library* (manuscript);
  *an NBC anchor* / *a CBS anchor*; *a URL* (you-are-ell); *a EULA violation* (yoo-luh);
  *an EU member*; *an @ sign*; *an 800 number*; *an 007 field* but *a 007-style agent* (double-oh).
  **[MECHANICAL]** — testable per token.

## Ligatures (7.34–7.35)

- **[7.34] When not to use ligatures** — **Do not use *æ* or *œ*** in Latin or transliterated Greek, nor
  in words adopted into English (which are in English dictionaries). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: aesthetics; Encyclopaedia Britannica (contrary to corporate usage); oedipal; a trompe l'oeil
    mural; a tray of hors d'oeuvres; Emily Dickinson's oeuvre
- **[7.35] When ligatures should be used** — Use *æ* for **Old English** in an Old English context; *œ*
  for **French** in a French context (*Ælfric*; *le nœud gordien*; *œuvres complètes*). **[MECHANICAL]**

## Word Division (7.36–7.48)

> Relevance note: this section governs **typesetting**, not prose. The writer-facing rules are: never
> hyphenate in a manuscript, never add hyphens to URLs, and don't hand-break words.

- **[7.36] Dictionary word division** — Applies only to **published fixed-layout** works (print/PDF),
  **never at the manuscript stage** (2.16). Use Merriam-Webster's dots; this section only *supplements*
  the dictionary. **[MECHANICAL — production]**
- **[7.37] Word divisions that should be avoided** — **Never divide single-syllable words** (incl. *aimed,
  helped*); need **at least two letters before** and **three letters after** the break.
  - ex: **women** (not *wom-en*); **losses** (not *loss-es*); **sur-prises** (not *surpris-es*)
- **[7.38] Dividing according to pronunciation** — Divide by **sound, not etymology**: *knowl-edge* (not
  *know-ledge*); *democ-racy* or *de-mocracy* (not *demo-cracy*). Watch meaning-changing pairs:
  *proj-ect* (n.) / *pro-ject* (v.).
- **[7.39] Dividing after a vowel** — Prefer a break **after** a vowel; keep a medial vowel on the first
  line; treat diphthongs as single vowels: *criti-cism*, *liga-ture*, *an-tipodes*/*antipo-des*,
  *aneu-rysm*.
- **[7.40] Dividing compounds, prefixes, and suffixes** — Break at **natural** boundaries:
  *poverty- / stricken*, *thanks-giving*, *dis-pleasure*, *re-inforce*.
- **[7.41] Dividing words ending in “ing”** — Break **before -ing**; with a doubled consonant, break
  **between** the consonants; check *-ling* words: *certify-ing, giv-ing, run-ning, dab-bing, fiz-zling,
  bris-tling*.
- **[7.42] Dividing proper nouns and personal names** — Break **between elements** of a name
  (*Alek-sis*, *Ana-stasia*); break **after initials** (*Susan B. / Anthony*, *M. F. K. / Fisher*); avoid
  breaking before a number or *Jr./Sr.* (nonbreaking space); *Elizabeth II* stays whole if possible.
- **[7.43] Dividing numerals** — Keep large numerals intact; if unavoidable, break **only after a comma,
  never after a single digit**.
- **[7.44] Dividing numerals with abbreviated units of measure** — Keep **numeral + abbreviated unit**
  together (*345 m*, *24 kg*, *55 BCE*, *6:35 p.m.*).
- **[7.45] Dividing mathematical expressions** — Run-in expressions may break before or after an
  operation/relation sign; displayed expressions break **before** the sign, aligned on the relation sign.
- **[7.46] Division in run-in lists** — Never leave a list number/letter (*(3)*, *(c)*) at a line end —
  carry it over with its text.
- **[7.47] Dividing URLs at the end of a line** — **Never add a hyphen** to break a URL; break **after
  `http://`/`https://`**, or **before** a single slash, period, comma, colon (other than the first),
  hyphen, tilde, underscore, question mark, number sign, percent; or **before/after** `=` or `&`.
  **Authors should not insert breaks in URLs in manuscripts** (2.16); unnecessary in reflowable formats;
  keep a trailing slash copied from an address bar (6.120). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.48] Hyphenation and appearance** — **No more than three successive lines may end in hyphens**
  ("hyphen stack"/"ladder"). Never hyphenate in manuscript preparation; may suppress hyphenation in
  headings/titles. **[MECHANICAL — production]**

## Italics, Capitals, and Quotation Marks (7.49–7.86)

- **[7.49] Setting off proper names and titles of works** — Italics/caps/quotes for **proper nouns and
  titles** belong to chapter 8; italics for unspoken discourse to 12.49. This section covers only
  **words as words** and similar distinctive treatment.
- **[7.50] Italics and markup** — Roman = regular; italics are the **default** way to set text off
  (emphasis, key terms, foreign terms, words as words, titles). Boldface is a lesser option; underlining
  on manuscript means italics (wavy underline = bold). Prefer **semantic markup** in electronic formats
  (accessibility, text-to-speech) (2.86–88). **[MECHANICAL]**

### Emphasis (7.51–7.54)

- **[7.51] Italics for emphasis** — **JUDGMENT, and one of the chapter's core principles:**
  > "Use italics for emphasis only as an occasional adjunct to efficient sentence structure. Overused,
  > italics quickly lose their force. Seldom should as much as a sentence be italicized for emphasis,
  > and never a whole passage."
  - ex (no italics needed — position carries the stress): The damaging evidence was offered not by the
    arresting officer, not by the injured plaintiff, but by the boy's own mother.
  - ex (italics necessary): It was *Leo*!
- **[7.52] Boldface or underlining for emphasis** — In formal prose **italics are the traditional
  choice**; bold "risks appearing too emphatic," underlining collides with hyperlinks and tracked
  changes. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.53] Asterisks for emphasis** — Where rich text is unavailable, `*like this*` (~bold) or `_like
  this_` (~italic). **Never** substitute Unicode bold/italic letters (breaks screen readers and search).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.54] Capitals for emphasis** — Initial caps for emphasis are now used **only ironically or in
  jest** (8.95). **"Capitalizing an entire word or phrase for emphasis is rarely appropriate in formal
  prose"**; full caps read as **shouting** — avoid, or use small caps. Note the terminology: *capitalizing*
  = initial letter only; *setting in full caps* = ALL CAPS. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "OK, so I'm a Bad Role Model," admitted Mary cheerfully. / "Be careful—WATCH OUT!" she yelled.

### Words from Other Languages (7.55–7.57)

- **[7.55] Unfamiliar words and phrases from other languages** — **Italicize** non-English words/phrases
  that are **not in Merriam-Webster or not familiar** to readers; a term that becomes familiar need be
  italicized **only on first occurrence**. Italicize **only Latin-alphabet words** (not 拼音/汉字, which
  take quotation marks plus translation). **Do not italicize proper nouns** (*Musée d'Orsay*). Query the
  author when a multilingual narrator/character uses such vocabulary naturally. **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: The *quatrième chant* in Bocage . . .; a personalized *Kaffeetasse* (coffee cup); fans of
    *fútbol* (soccer); instances of "bureaucratic *shiyuzheng*" ([Chinese characters] . . .)
- **[7.56] Roman for familiar words from other languages** — Familiar loans appear in **roman**:
  *croissants, banh mi, pasha, Weltanschauung, kaiser, recherché, bourgeois, telenovelas, anime, eros*
  (but *épater le bourgeois* — listed only as a "French phrase"). **Consistency rule:** if a familiar term
  and an unfamiliar one appear in the same context, **italicize both or neither** (*mise en scène* /
  *mise en bouteille*). **18th-ed. change:** capitalize German nouns **unless Merriam-Webster lowercases
  them**; entries labeled "often capitalized" may be lowercased. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.57] Roman for Latin words and abbreviations** — Common Latin is **roman**: *a priori, de novo, in
  vitro, et al., passim, ibid.*; when in doubt, check Merriam-Webster, and **when still in doubt,
  italicize**. **Always italicize *sic*** (*we [sic] as agents*). **[MECHANICAL]**

### Highlighting Key Terms and Expressions (7.58–7.65)

- **[7.58] Italics or boldface for key terms** — Italicize a key term **on first occurrence only**, roman
  thereafter; boldface is for textbooks/glossaries and must be flagged in the text. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.59] Linked text** — Link text should be **brief, descriptive, and meaningful without the link**;
  exclude surrounding punctuation (except a terminal ? or ! that belongs to the title); include an
  article only if it is part of the name; **avoid using a URL as link text** except in citations; give
  images alt text. **[JUDGMENT — web]**
- **[7.60] “Scare quotes”** — Quotes may signal slang, irony, or a term that is "not mine"; **"scare
  quotes lose their force and may irritate readers if overused."** **[JUDGMENT]**
  - ex: My rotary simulation app allows me to "dial" phone numbers again. / "Child protection" sometimes
    fails to protect.
- **[7.61] Mixing single and double quotation marks** — Chicago **discourages** using single quotes for
  special terms and double for their definitions; prefer **italics, quotation marks, and parentheses**
  in combination. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.62] “So-called”** — With *so-called*, **no quotation marks are needed**; add them only to isolate
  one part of a phrase: *So-called child protection sometimes fails to protect*; but *so-called "running"
  shoes*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.63] Common expressions and figures of speech** — **No quotation marks** for idioms, slang, or
  familiar figures; reserve them for **verbatim borrowing** or irony (7.60).
  - ex: Myths of paradise lost are common in folklore. / I grew up in a one-horse town. / but: not one to
    ask "for whom the bell tolls."
- **[7.64] Signs and notices** — Short signs go in **title case, no quotes** (*Authorized Personnel Only*);
  **longer notices are treated as quotations**. **[MECHANICAL]** (see 8.160)
- **[7.65] Mottoes** — Treat like signs (*Don't Tread on Me*; the motto "All for one and one for all");
  **foreign mottoes are italic, sentence case** (*Souvent me souviens*). **[MECHANICAL]**

### Words as Words and Letters as Letters (7.66–7.74)

- **[7.66] Words and phrases used as words** — **Italicize** (or quote) a word referred to as a word:
  *the term critical mass*; *What is meant by neurobotics?* **Proper nouns used as words may be
  italicized** (a slight 18th-ed. departure); **personal names used as names are not**: *iPhone* is
  spelled with a lowercase *i*; *The word Celsius derives from the name Celsius.* Use **quotes** instead
  where italics would collide — for the translated gloss (*ser* and *estar* . . . "to be") or to suggest
  speech (*Many people say "I" even when "me" would be more correct*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.67] Letters as letters** — **Italicize** letters of the Latin alphabet (*the letter q*; *a capital
  W*; *He signed with an X*). **Roman** in set expressions: *mind your p's and q's*; *dotting the i's and
  crossing the t's*; *the three R's*. **Phonetic symbols are always roman.** **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.68] Scholastic grades** — **Roman capitals**, plural with **apostrophe + s** (an 18th-ed. change):
  *She finished with three A's, one B, and two C's.* **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.69] Letters standing for names** — Roman caps; **no period** if unrelated to a real name (*A sues
  B*); a single initial abbreviating a real name takes a period (*Professor D.*); a concealed name may
  take a 2-em dash and no period (*Senator K—*); **two or more initials for a whole name take no periods**
  (*JFK, LBJ*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.70] Letters as shapes** — Capitalized roman: *an S curve*, *an L-shaped room*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.71] Names of letters** — Spell the **name** in roman, no quotes: *bee, cee, dee, eff, gee,
  aitch, jay, kay, el, em, en, pee, cue, ar, ess, tee, vee, double-u, ex, wye, zee*. **Vowels are not
  named in standard dictionaries** — give an example instead ("*a* as in apple"). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.72] Rhyme schemes** — Lowercase **italic** letters, no spaces: *abab, cdcd, efef, gg*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.73] Mathematical variables** — Single letters as variables are **italic** (including lowercase
  Greek); **numerals, operation/relation signs, units, function names, chemical symbols, multi-letter
  abbreviations, and uppercase Greek are roman**; **vectors/tensors/matrices are bold**. Statistical
  notation: *P_x*; subscripts that are abbreviations are roman (*V*<sub>L</sub> "lung volume").
  **[MECHANICAL — technical]**
- **[7.74] Communication code words** — Capitalize, don't italicize: *Alfa, Bravo, Charlie . . . Juliett
  . . . Xray, Yankee, Zulu* (NATO alphabet). **[MECHANICAL]**

### Music: Some Typographic Conventions (7.75–7.80)

- **[7.75] Suggested references for music publishing** — Too specialized; see Holoman, *Writing About
  Music*; titles of musical works at 8.195–99. **[Collapsed: specialty production.]**
- **[7.76] Musical pitches** — **Roman capitals** for pitch letters; *sharp/flat/natural* spelled out in
  roman with a **hyphen** (*G-sharp, the key of B-flat minor, the D-major triad, an F augmented triad*);
  pitches in a series joined by **en dashes** (*the initial F–G–F–B♭*). **[MECHANICAL — specialty]**
- **[7.77] Octaves** — Several competing systems; **state which one you use** (e.g., which symbol = middle
  C): *c, c′, c″* / *C₁, C₂* / *A₀* to *C₈*; scientific works use *C₄* for middle C. Pitches sounded
  together are listed **low to high**, optionally joined by plus signs: *C+E+G*. **[JUDGMENT — specialty]**
- **[7.78] Chords (harmonic analysis)** — Roman numerals for scale degree: *V*, *V⁷*, *iii*; progressions
  joined by **en dashes**: *IV–I–V–I*; lowercase numerals for minor chords in technical writing.
  **[MECHANICAL — specialty]**
- **[7.79] “Major” and “minor”** — Where many keys are named, **capital = major, lowercase = minor**, and
  the words *major*/*minor* are omitted. **[MECHANICAL — specialty]**
- **[7.80] Dynamics** — Lowercase, often italic (*piano*, *mezzo forte*); spelled out where space allows;
  symbols in lowercase bold italic with **no periods** (*p*, *mf*). **[MECHANICAL — specialty]**

### Computer Terms (7.81–7.86)

- **[7.81] Application-specific versus generic usage** — **Follow the software/device** for named keys,
  menus, and commands; follow accepted usage for standards; generic references need no special
  treatment. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.82] Capitalization for keys, menu items, and file formats** — **Capitalize alphabet and named
  keys** even when the keyboard lowercases them (*F2, Fn, Option, Alt, Ctrl, Command, Delete, Backspace,
  Return, Enter, Caps Lock, Shift, Space bar*); **file-format abbreviations in full caps** (*PNG, GIF,
  JPEG, HTML*) but **extensions lowercase** (`.html`, `.htm`). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.83] Keyboard combinations and shortcuts** — **Plus sign or hyphen with no spaces**; plus for
  Windows, hyphen for macOS: *Ctrl+S*, *Command-S*, *Ctrl+Alt+Delete*, *Option-Shift-Command-Delete*.
  Spell out and capitalize *Shift, Hyphen, Space*. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.84] Setting off file names and words to be typed or selected** — Quotes + capitalization suffice;
  title-case named items need no quotes; objects in **sentence case** usually do; put sentence
  punctuation **outside** the quotes (an exception to 6.9). For stronger distinction use a
  fixed-width/code font, with following punctuation in the surrounding font and **nondirectional**
  quotes inside code (6.123). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: select the checkbox labeled "Different first page." / type "cd Desktop". / name your file
    appendix A, draft.
- **[7.85] Terms like “web” and “internet”** — Capitalize internet terms **only if trademarked or a proper
  name**. Generic: *the internet, intranet, net neutrality, the web, website, web page, hypertext,
  email, open-source platforms*. Proper/trademarked: *Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP)* but *a hypertext
  transfer protocol*; *Internet Protocol (IP)*; *Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP)*; *Wi-Fi*;
  *Ethernet*; *NFC*; *World Wide Web Consortium (W3C)*; *the World Wide Web*. *(Earlier editions showed
  only lowercase forms.)* **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.86] Hashtags** — **CamelCase each word** for legibility and screen readers: *#AmEditing* (not
  *#amediting*), *#ThrowbackThursday*, *#MeToo*, *#CMOSTips*; single words optional (*#Photography*);
  leave *iPhone*-style camel forms as is (*#iPhonePhotography*, *#MyIPhone*); **reproduce quoted hashtags
  exactly**. **[MECHANICAL]**

## Compounds and Hyphenation (7.87–7.96)

- **[7.87] To hyphenate or not to hyphenate** — The commonest editorial question. **Look in the
  dictionary first**; this section plus the 7.96 guide covers what the dictionary doesn't, and decides by
  **grammatical function and position in the sentence**. En dashes in compounds: 6.82–90.
- **[7.88] Compounds defined** — Vocabulary a writer needs:
  - **open compound**: *high school*, *lowest common denominator*;
  - **hyphenated compound**: *mass-produced*, *kilowatt-hour*, *non-English-speaking*;
  - **closed/solid compound**: *screenshot*, *smartphone*;
  - **permanent compound**: accepted into the vocabulary, **in the dictionary**;
  - **temporary compound**: coined for a onetime purpose (*dictionary-wielding*, *impeachment hound*) —
    usually **not** in the dictionary.
  - **Prefix formations** (*antigrammarian, postmodern*) are treated in 7.96 §4; **combining forms**
    (*electro- + magnetic*) in §2; **suffix formations** (*nationhood, penniless*) are **almost always
    closed**.
- **[7.89] The trend toward closed compounds** — "With frequent use, compounds that are open or hyphenated
  tend to become closed (**on line → on-line → online**)." Related pairs may diverge (*online* vs.
  *on-screen*); **make such pairs consistent** (close both). **[JUDGMENT]**
- **[7.90] Hyphens and readability** — Hyphenate where a word could be **misread**: *re-creation*,
  *co-op* (vs. *recreation*, *coop*). Hyphens **kill ambiguity**: *much-needed clothing* (greatly needed,
  not "abundant and needed"). Where no ambiguity is possible, **do not hyphenate**: *public welfare
  administration*, *graduate student housing*. **[MECHANICAL/JUDGMENT]**
- **[7.91] Compound modifiers before or after a noun** — **Hyphenate adjectival compounds (phrasal
  adjectives) before the noun**: "it is never incorrect to hyphenate adjectival compounds before a
  noun." **Open after the noun**, even when the dictionary hyphenates (*well-read* → *the student is well
  read*; *one-sided*). Two exceptions: (a) **proper nouns** (*United States*) and **-ly adverb + adjective**
  (7.93) are never hyphenated; (b) an **open compound noun used as a modifier usually stays open**
  (*high school student*, *guest room access*) — check the dictionary when in doubt. Some compounds are
  **always hyphenated** even after the noun (7.92). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.92] Compound modifiers that are always hyphenated** — Keep the hyphen before **and** after the
  noun for: *all-consuming, cost-effective, dyed-in-the-wool, first-rate, high-spirited, ill-advised,
  old-fashioned, short-lived, fat-free, wild-eyed* (+ others; a hyphenated adjective entry in
  Merriam-Webster may stay hyphenated after the noun, at editorial discretion). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.93] Adverbs ending in “-ly”** — **Never hyphenate** *-ly* adverb + adjective/participle, before or
  after the noun (*largely irrelevant*, *a smartly dressed couple*). **Three-word phrases may take
  hyphens for clarity** (*a not-so-smartly-dressed couple*). **Words in *-ly* that are adjectives**
  (*early, friendly*) **keep the hyphen as needed** (*an early-morning walk*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.94] Multiple hyphens** — Usually hyphenate multi-word modifiers (*an over-the-counter drug*, *a
  winner-take-all contest*). When an **adjective modifies an already-hyphenated compound, a second hyphen
  is usually unnecessary**: *late nineteenth-century literature*, *early twentieth-century growth* (but
  *mid-* is now a combining form and keeps its hyphen: *a mid-eighteenth-century poet*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.95] Suspended hyphens** — When the second part of a hyphenated compound is omitted, **keep the
  suspended hyphen, followed by a space (or a comma in a series)**: *fifteen- and twenty-year mortgages*;
  *Chicago- or Milwaukee-bound passengers*; *five-, ten-, and twenty-dollar bills*; *over- and underfed
  cats*; *state-owned and -operated hospitals*. **Do not suspend for a single entity/range**: *a
  five-by-eight-foot rug*, *a three-to-five-year gap*. **Closed compounds must be repeated, not
  suspended**: *overfed and overworked mules* (not *overfed and -worked mules*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[7.96] Hyphenation guide** — **THE CORE PRINCIPLE** (quote it):
  > "In general, Chicago prefers a **spare hyphenation style**: If no suitable example or analogy can be
  > found either in this section (7.87–96) or in the dictionary, hyphens should be added only if doing so
  > will prevent a misreading or otherwise significantly aid comprehension."

  **Default = open; hyphen only for (a) a dictionary form, (b) an analogy to this guide, (c) clarity.**
  The table has four parts: §1 by **category**, §2 by **part of speech**, §3 by **specific term**, §4
  **prefixes**.

#### 7.96 §1 — Compounds according to category

| Category | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **age terms** | Hyphenated as both noun and adjective (except the two open forms); applies equally to numerals | *a three-year-old*; *a fifty-five-year-old woman*; *a test for nine-to-ten-year-olds*; *a group of ten- and eleven-year-olds*; but *seven years old*, *eighteen years of age* |
| **chemical compounds** | Open in both noun and adjective | *sodium chloride*; *sodium chloride solution* |
| **colors** | Hyphenated before a noun, **open after** | *emerald-green tie*, *snow-white dress*, *black-and-white print*; but *his tie is emerald green*, *the water is blue green*, *the truth isn't black and white* |
| **compass points/directions** | Closed in noun/adj/adv; hyphen after the first element when **three** directions combine; **en dash** when *from . . . to* is implied | *northeast*, *east-northeast*, *a north-south street*, *the street runs north-south* |
| **fractions with half/quarter/eighth** | Noun open, adjective hyphenated | *a half hour*, *a half dozen*, *a quarter mile*, *an eighth note*; but *a half-hour session*, *a quarter-mile run* |
| **fractions, simple** | Hyphenated in noun, adjective, and adverb (except when the second element is already hyphenated) | *one-half*, *two-thirds*, *three-quarters*, *a one twenty-fifth share*, *one and three-quarters*; *a two-thirds majority*, *three-quarters done* |
| **money** | Spelled-out units: hyphenate before a noun, open after. Symbols: **open everywhere** except **number ranges** | *a five-cent raise*, *sixty-four-million-dollar question*, *multimillion-dollar deal*; but *a deal worth thirty million dollars*, *$30 million loan*, *a $50–$60 million loss* |
| **non-English phrases** | **Open** unless the original language hyphenates | *an a priori argument*, *a Sturm und Drang drama*, *in vitro fertilization*; but *a tête-à-tête approach* |
| **number + abbreviation** | **Always open** | *the 33 m distance*, *a 2 kg weight*, *a 3 ft. high wall*, *7 lb., 8 oz. baby* |
| **number + noun** | Hyphenated before a noun, **open otherwise** | *a hundred-meter race*, *a 250-page book*, *a three-inch-high statuette*, *a one-and-a-half-inch hem*, *a seven-pound, eight-ounce baby*, *five-to-ten-minute intervals* (one range); but *it's three inches high*, *he's six feet two*, *five- or ten-minute intervals* (two values) |
| **number + percent** | **Always open**, except between number ranges | *50 percent*, *a 10 percent raise*; but *a 30–40 percent increase* |
| **number, ordinal, + noun** | Hyphenated before a noun, open after | *third-floor apartment*, *103rd-floor view*, *fifth-place contestant*, *twenty-first-row seats*; but *on the third floor*, *in the twenty-first row* |
| **number, ordinal, + superlative** | Hyphenated before a noun, open after | *a second-best decision*, *third-largest town*; but *he arrived fourth to last* |
| **numbers, spelled out** | **21–99 hyphenated**; all else open; same for ordinals | *twenty-eight*, *twenty-eighth*; but *three hundred*, *nineteen forty-five*, *five hundred fifty-two contestants*, *five hundred fifty-second contestant* |
| **time** | Usually open; **hyphenated before a noun** | *at three thirty*, *a four o'clock train*, *the 5:00 p.m. news*; but *the three-thirty train* |

#### 7.96 §2 — Compounds according to parts of speech

| Pattern | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **adjective + noun** | Hyphenated before, open after | *small-state senators*, *a high-quality alkylate*, *a middle-class neighborhood*, *a top-ten school*; but *the neighborhood is middle class*, *placed in the top ten* |
| **adjective + participle** | Hyphenated before, open after (some permanent compounds closed) | *tight-lipped person*, *open-ended question*; but *the question was open ended*; *a lighthearted inquiry*, *he was softheaded* (closed) |
| **adverb in -ly + participle/adjective** | **Open both positions** (7.93) | *a highly paid ragpicker*, *a fully open society*, *he was mildly amusing* |
| **other adverb + participle/adjective** | Hyphenated before, open after; *more/most/less/least/very* usually open unless ambiguous; if another adverb modifies **the adverb**, keep the whole expression open | *a much-needed addition* / *it was much needed*; *little-understood rules*; *the best-known author*; *a very much needed addition*; *the most skilled workers* (in number) vs. *the most-skilled workers* (in skill) |
| **combining forms** | Closed if listed in Merriam-Webster; else hyphenated | *electrocardiogram*, *socioeconomic*; *a Franco-American alliance*, *politico-scientific studies* |
| **gerund + noun** | Noun open, adjective hyphenated | *running shoes*, *cooking class*; but *running-shoe store* |
| **noun + adjective** | Hyphenated before, usually open after | *computer-literate writers*, *world-famous doctor*, *fan-friendly stadium*; but *they are computer literate* |
| **noun + gerund** | Noun forms open; adjective forms hyphenated before a noun | *mountain climbing*, *decision-making*, *copyediting*; but *a mountain-climbing enthusiast*, *a Nobel Prize-winning chemist* |
| **noun + noun, single function** (first modifies second) | Noun open; adjective hyphenated before a noun (some permanent compounds closed) | *student nurse*, *restaurant owner*, *directory path*, *tenure track*; but *tenure-track position*, *home-rule governance*; *shipbuilder*, *copyeditor* (closed) |
| **noun + noun, two functions** (equal) | **Always hyphenated** | *writer-director*, *philosopher-king*, *city-state*, *city-state governance* |
| **noun + numeral/enumerator** | **Always open** | *type A*, *a type A executive*, *type 2 diabetes*, *size 12 slacks*, *a page 1 headline*, *Title IX exemptions* |
| **noun + participle** | Hyphenated before; open after unless the dictionary hyphenates the verb form | *a Wagner-burdened repertoire*, *flower-filled garden*; but *the garden was flower filled*; *the room was air-conditioned*, *the events were stage-managed* |
| **participle + noun** | Hyphenated before, open after | *chopped-liver paté*, *cutting-edge methods*; but *their approach was cutting edge* |
| **participle + *up/out*, etc.** | Adjective hyphenated before, open after; **verb always open** | *dressed-up children* / *we were dressed up*; *that decal is ironed on* / *we ironed on the decal* |
| **phrases, adjectival** | Hyphenated before, usually open after | *an over-the-counter drug*, *a matter-of-fact reply*, *an up-to-date solution*; but *her tone was matter of fact* |
| **phrases, adverbial** | Hyphenated **only if the dictionary lists a hyphenated adverb**; else open | *one-on-one*, *head-on*, *matter-of-factly*; but *passed hand to hand*, *took things day by day*, *available over the counter* |
| **phrases, noun** | Hyphenated or open as in the dictionary; **if unlisted, open** | *stick-in-the-mud*, *jack-of-all-trades*; but *a flash in the pan* |
| **phrases, verbal** | Closed/hyphenated/open as listed; **if unlisted, open** | *air-condition*, *stage-manage*, *fast-talk*; *babysit*, *sucker punch* (closed) |
| **proper nouns/adjectives for geography or nationality** | **Open** in both noun and adjective unless the first element is a **prefix** or ***between* is implied (en dash)** | *African Americans*, *African American president*, *French Canadians*, *South Asian Americans*, *Middle Eastern countries*; but *Sino-Tibetan*, *Anglo-American cooperation*, *the Spanish-American War*, *the US–Canada border* |

#### 7.96 §3 — Compounds formed with specific terms

| Term | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **ache** | Always closed | *toothache*, *stomachache*, *earache* |
| **all** | Adverbial phrases open; adjectival phrases hyphenated before **and** after | *all out*, *all along*, *all over*, *we were all in* [tired]; but *an all-out effort*, *an all-American player*, *the book is all-encompassing* |
| **book** | Closed or open as in the dictionary; **unlisted → open** | *reference book*, *coupon book*; *checkbook*, *cookbook* |
| **borne** | Closed if listed; **else hyphenated** | *waterborne*, *foodborne*; but *mosquito-borne* |
| **century** | Noun **always open**; adjective hyphenated before, open after | *the twenty-first century*, *her style was nineteenth century*; *fourteenth-century monastery*, *a mid-eighteenth-century poet*, *late nineteenth-century photographs* (no second hyphen, 7.94) |
| **cross** | Dictionary forms as listed; **unlisted → noun open, adjective/adverb/verb hyphenated** | *a cross section*, *a cross-reference*, *cross-referenced*, *cross-country*, *crossbow*, *crossover* |
| **e-** (electronic) | Close **email, ebook, esports** and certain proper nouns; hyphenate other *e-* terms | *e-bike*, *e-commerce*, *eBay* |
| **elect** | Hyphenated; **en dash if it follows an open compound** | *president-elect*, *mayor-elect*, *county assessor-elect*, *vice president–elect* |
| **ever** | Usually hyphenated before but not after; some closed | *ever-ready help*, *ever-recurring problem*; but *everlasting*, *he was ever eager* |
| **ex-** | Hyphenated; **en dash before an open compound** | *ex-partner*, *ex-marine*, *ex–corporate executive* |
| **first, second, third** | Adjective hyphenated before **and** after (a few closed forms); noun open | *firsthand knowledge*, *first- and secondhand knowledge*, *first-class tickets*, *third-rate production*; but *upgraded to first class*, *learned at first hand* |
| **fold** | Hyphenated with **hyphenated spelled-out numbers or numerals**; else closed | *fourfold*, *hundredfold*; but *twenty-five-fold*, *150-fold* |
| **foster** | Noun open; adjective hyphenated | *foster mother*, *a foster-family background* |
| **free** | Hyphenated **both before and after** | *fat-free yogurt* / *the yogurt is fat-free*; *toll-free number* |
| **full** | Hyphenated before, open after; use *-ful* only in permanent compounds | *full-length mirror* / *the mirror is full length*; *three bags full*; *cupful*, *handful* |
| **general** | Always open; **pluralize the first element only** | *attorney general*, *postmaster general*, *lieutenants general* |
| **grand / great-grand** | *Grand* closed; *great* hyphenated | *grandfather*, *granddaughter*; *great-grandmother*, *great-great-grandchild* |
| **half** | Adjective hyphenated before, open after; noun/adverb/verb usually open; some closed | *a half-finished meal* / *it was half finished*; *a half sister*, *a half hour*, *we played half seriously*; *halfway*, *halftime show*, *half-time hours* (of a job) |
| **house** | Closed or open as in the dictionary; **unlisted → open** | *schoolhouse*, *courthouse*; *safe house*, *rest house* |
| **ill** | **Hyphenated before and after** | *ill-advised trip* / *the trip was ill-advised*; *ill-defined goals* |
| **in-law** | Always hyphenated; **only the first element pluralizes** | *sister-in-law*, *parents-in-law* |
| **like** | Closed/hyphenated as in the dictionary; **unlisted → hyphenated, and the hyphen stays after the noun** | *catlike*, *childlike*, *Zen-like*, *bell-like*, *a penitentiary-like institution* |
| **mid** | Closed/hyphenated as in the dictionary; **unlisted → hyphenate**; Chicago now treats *mid* as an **adjective combining form**, not a prefix | *midair*, *midsemester*, *mid-Atlantic*, *mid-thirties*, *mid-sentence*; *mid- to late thirties* (suspension, 7.95) |
| **near** | Noun open; adjective hyphenated | *in the near term*, *a near accident*; but *a near-term proposal*, *a near-dead language* |
| **odd** | Always hyphenated | *a hundred-odd manuscripts*, *350-odd books* |
| **old** | Noun hyphenated; adjective hyphenated before, open after | *a three-year-old*, *a 105-year-old woman*, *a centuries-old debate*; but *a child who is three years old* |
| **on** | Sometimes closed, sometimes hyphenated; **unlisted → hyphenate** | *online*, *onstage*, *ongoing*; *on-screen*, *on-site* |
| **quasi** | Noun usually open; adjective usually hyphenated; a few closed in the dictionary | *a quasi corporation*; *a quasi-public corporation*, *quasi-judicial*; *quasiperiodic*, *quasicrystal* |
| **self** | Both noun and adjective hyphenated, **except before a suffix or after *un-*** | *self-restraint*, *self-sustaining*, *the behavior is self-destructive*; but *selfless*, *unselfconscious* |
| **step** | Always closed, **except with *grand* and *great*** | *stepbrother*, *stepparent*; *step-granddaughter*, *step-great-granddaughter* |
| **style** | Adjective/adverb hyphenated; noun usually open | *dined family-style*, *1920s-style dancing*, *Chicago-style hyphenation*, *sentence-style capitalization*; but *according to Chicago style*, *use sentence style* |
| **then** (meaning "former") | Hyphen to prevent misreading (an 18th-ed. change); **en dash before an open compound** | *the country's then-president*, *then-President Bush*, *then-Vice President Biden*, *our then–assistant principal* |
| **vice** | Adjective hyphenated before the noun; noun sometimes hyphenated, sometimes open, occasionally closed | *vice president*, *vice-presidential duties* / *that speech was vice presidential*; *vice-consul*, *vice-chancellor*, *vice admiral*, *viceroy* |
| **web (internet)** | Noun open or closed as shown; **unlisted → open**; adjective hyphenated before a noun | *a website*, *a web crawler*, *a web page*; *web-related matters* |
| **wide** | Closed if listed; **unlisted → hyphenated, and the hyphen stays before and after the noun** | *worldwide*, *citywide*, *Chicago-wide*; *the canvass was university-wide* |

#### 7.96 §4 — Words formed with prefixes

- **Base rule [7.96 §4]** — **Prefix compounds are normally closed** (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs
  alike). **Use a hyphen only:**
  1. **before a capitalized word or a numeral** — *un-American*, *pre-1950*;
  2. **before a compound term** — *non-self-sustaining*, *pre-Vietnam War* (**en dash before an open
     compound**);
  3. **to separate letters/syllables that could be misread** — *anti-intellectual*, *extra-alkaline*,
     *pre-authorize*, *co-op*, *re-creation*;
  4. **to separate a double prefix** — *re-review*, *sub-subentry*;
  5. **when the prefix stands alone** (suspension) — *over- and underused*, *macro- and microeconomics*.
  - Prefixes not listed (*auto-, tri-, para-*) follow the same pattern; an occasional extra hyphen may
    be added for clarity or authorial preference, subject to editorial discretion. **[MECHANICAL]**
- **Selected prefixes** (closed form first, then the hyphenated exceptions):
  - *ante*: antebellum, antenatal | *anti*: antihypertensive, antihero, antiracist, **antisemitism**, but
    anti-inflammatory, anti-Hitlerian, anti-antifascist
  - *bi*: binomial, bivalent | *bio*: bioecology, biophysical | *cis*: cisgender, cissexual, **cis male**
    (adjective), but cis-Victorian
  - *co*: coequal, coauthor, coeditor, coordinate, **cooperate**, coworker, but **co-op, co-opt**
  - *counter*: counterclockwise | *cyber*: cyberspace, cyberbullying | *de*: deconstruct, but
    **de-emphasize, de-stress**
  - *extra*: extramural, extrafine, but extra-administrative | *hyper*: hypertension, hypertext |
    *infra*: infrasonic | *inter*: interorganizational, interfaith | *intra*: intramural, but
    intra-arterial
  - *macro*: macroeconomics | *mega*: megavitamin, megamall, but mega-annoyance | *meta*: metalanguage,
    but **meta-analysis** (≠ metanalysis) | *micro*: microeconomics
  - *mini*: minivan | *multi*: multiauthor, but multi-institutional | *neo*: neonate, neoorthodox,
    Neoplatonism, **neo-Nazi** (lowercase + hyphen if unlisted)
  - *non*: nonbinary, nonviolent, nonnegotiable, but **non-beer-drinking** | *over*: overmagnified,
    overshoes | *post*: postdoctoral, postmodernism, but post-traumatic, **post-World War II**
  - *pre*: premodern, preregistration, prewar, **preempt**, but pre-authorize, **pre-Columbian**,
    Pre-Raphaelite
  - *pro*: proactive, proindustrial, but **pro-choice, pro-life** | *proto*: protolanguage | *pseudo*:
    pseudotechnocrat, but pseudo-Tudor
  - *re*: reedit, reunify, but **re-cover, re-creation** (vs. recover, recreation) | *semi*: semiopaque,
    but semi-invalid | *sub*: subbasement, subzero | *super*: superannuated, superpowerful |
    *supra*: supranational, but supra-American
  - *trans*: transgender, transshipment, transatlantic, **trans fat, trans man, trans woman** (adjective),
    but trans-American | *ultra*: ultrasophisticated | *un*: unneutered, but **un-English, un-unionized**
  - *under*: underemployed, underrate, undercount — list continues on p. 492, **[illegible]** in the OCR;
    the five numbered hyphen conditions above govern any prefix not shown here.
  - *mid* is handled in §3 (adjective combining form, not a prefix).
