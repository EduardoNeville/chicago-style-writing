# Numbers

Source: CMOS ch 9 (rules 9.1-9.69). Rule numbers in square brackets are CMOS 18th ed. rule numbers.
Parts: ch09. Every claim here carries its rule number.

## Apply these first

Ranked by frequency x harm for drafting and editing ordinary prose.

### From ch09

1. **Spell out zero through one hundred; numerals above** — "Thirty-two children," "seventy," but
   "103 years old," "540 cars" [9.2]. If the house rule is the alternative (scientific/journalistic),
   spell out only **zero through nine** — and then round hundreds/thousands become numerals [9.3].
2. **Spell out *hundred/thousand/hundred thousand* multipliers of one to one hundred** — "seven hundred
   spectators," "two thousand," "three hundred million" — except in the sciences or with money [9.4,
   9.9]; switch to numerals when a pile of large numbers would collide ("47,122") [9.4, 9.7].
3. **Never start a sentence with a numeral** — spell it out ("One hundred ten candidates") or, better,
   **recast** ("In all, 110 candidates") [9.5]. Exception: **years** may start a sentence ("1937 was…"),
   though "The year 1937…" is better [9.5, 9.31]; letter+numeral terms (*3D*, *401(k)*) are fine [9.5].
4. **No *and* in spelled-out numbers** — *one hundred ten*, *two thousand twenty-four* [9.5].
5. **Keep a category consistent, allow categories to differ** — if one item in a category is a numeral,
   make them all numerals; but numbers of different kinds in one sentence need not match [9.7].
6. **Numerals whenever a symbol or abbreviation follows** — *50 km*, *240 V*, *85 g*, *10°C*,
   *$1,000*, *8%*, *5°* [9.8, 9.18]; **never hyphenate numeral + symbol/unit**, even adjectivally
   (*a 50 km race*) [9.18]. A unit with **no** numeral is always spelled out (*kilojoules*, not *kJ*)
   [9.18].
7. **Numerals for document parts, dates, grades, sizes, scores, severity** — *pages 3–9*, *chap. 1*,
   *fig. 9*, *June 1, 1941*, *grade 3* (but *third grade*), *size 12 dress*, *6 to 3*, *stage IV* [9.8].
8. **Ordinals: digits + *nd/rd/th*, never superscripts** — *122nd*, *123rd*, *125th*, *45th*; no
   *122ⁿᵈ* [9.6]. *Zeroth* takes *th* [9.6].
9. **Simple fractions are spelled out and hyphenated** — *three-fourths*, *two-thirds*, *a two-thirds
   majority* [9.15]; mixed numbers may go either way — *three and one-quarter miles* or *6¾ yards*
   [9.16]. Decimal fractions always numerals [9.21].
10. **Percentages: numerals + *percent* (nontechnical) or *%* (scientific)** — *3 percent*, *5.9 percent*,
    *20%*, *90–95 percent*; no space before *%* [9.20]. Only a sentence-initial percentage is spelled out
    (*Thirty-nine percent* … *48 percent*) [9.7, 9.20].
11. **Put a leading zero on decimals below 1.00** — *0.73*, *0.96 females for every male* [9.21];
    **omit it** in probabilities/coefficients (*p < .05*, *R = .10*, *.366*) and in firearm calibers
    (*.38 police special*, *.22-caliber*) [9.21].
12. **Money: spell out whole amounts of one hundred or less** (*seventy-five cents*, *fifteen dollars*),
    numerals above (*$1,000*, *$95,000*, *$250 million*, *$7.3 billion*) [9.22, 9.26]; add zeros/decimals
    only where fractional amounts coexist (*$0.95 up to $10.00*) [9.22]; money takes a **singular verb**
    ("The eighty-three dollars **was**…") [9.22].
13. **Dates: month-day-year with cardinal numbers and both commas** — "October 4, 2022, was…"; **not**
    *October 4th, 2022* [9.33]. Without a month, or with the day first, use the ordinal: *By the 25th*,
    *the 5th of June* (prefer *June 5* in formal prose) [9.33].
14. **Never write all-numeral dates in formal prose** (*5/10/99*); spell the full date out — 9/11 is the
    sanctioned exception [9.37]. ISO when sorting matters: **2025-07-14** [9.38].
15. **Years: numerals, no comma (unless five digits)** — *1776*, *3000 BCE*, *35,000 BP* but *5000 BP*
    [9.31, 9.56]; abbreviated years take a right single quotation mark: *the spirit of '76*, *the class of
    '06* [9.32].
16. **Eras: capitals, no periods, and mind the side** — *21 BCE–39 CE*, *55 BC*, *AD 1066*, *4400 BP to
    4250 BP*; **AD/AH precede** the year, **BC/BCE/BP follow** it, and all follow a spelled-out century
    ("the third century AD") [9.36].
17. **Centuries and decades: lowercase, no apostrophe before the *s*** — *the twenty-first century*,
    *the 1800s*, *the 1940s and '50s*, *the forties and fifties* [9.34, 9.35]; don't alternate forms
    randomly [9.34]. Avoid "turn of the twenty-first century" [9.34]; *the 2000s* is ambiguous for the
    first decade [9.35].
18. **Inclusive ranges take an en dash — but never with *from* or *between*** — *pages 75–110*,
    *2000–2009*; **from 75 to 110** (not *from 75–110*), **between about 150 and 200** [9.62];
    spelled-out ranges use *to*: *forty-five to forty-nine years*, *sixty-to-seventy-year-olds* [9.62].
19. **Elide range digits per the 9.63 table** — *3–10*, *96–117*, *100–104*, *101–8*, *321–28*,
    *1496–500*, *1087–89*; **repeat all digits when the century/thousands place changes**: *1897–1901*,
    *the winter of 2000–2001*, *1,247,689–1,248,125*, *12991–3001* [9.63, 9.65, 9.66]. For BCE/BC/BP,
    **always full digits** — *327–321 BCE* (7 years) vs. *327–21 BCE* (307 years) [9.66, 9.36].
20. **Commas every three digits, none to the right of the decimal, none in pages/addresses/years** —
    *4,000,500* but *0.32987*; *page 1535*; *11030 South Langley Avenue* [9.56].
21. **Pluralize numerals with a bare *s*** — *two 240s*, *the 1930s*, *their twenties*; never *240's*
    [9.55, 9.35].
22. **Time: spell out o'clock, half, and quarter hours** (*five o'clock*, *half past three*, *a quarter
    of four*) and use numerals with *a.m./p.m.* (*5:22 a.m.*) [9.39]; **never write 12:00 a.m./p.m. or
    numerals for noon and midnight** — write *noon*, *midnight*, or "midnight, August 21–22" [9.40];
    never *a.m.* with *morning/afternoon/evening/night/o'clock* [9.39, 9.40].
23. **Roman numerals only where Chicago keeps them** — monarchs/popes (*Elizabeth II*, *Innocent III*),
    inverted personal names (*Stevenson, Adlai E., III*), boats (*Bluebird III*), sequels as published,
    front matter; **Arabic everywhere else** — chapters, volumes, journals [9.43, 9.44, 9.46, 9.69].
    Use *MCMXCIX*, never *MIM* [9.68].
24. **Numbered institutions: spell out ordinals ≤ 100, numerals above** — *Fifth Ward*, *Eighteenth
    Dynasty*, *Ninety-Seventh Congress*, *First Avenue*, *Ninety-Fifth Street*, *Fourth Presbyterian
    Church* — but *117th Congress*, *101st Precinct*, *101st Airborne Division*, *122nd Street*
    [9.47–9.50, 9.53]. Union/lodge numbers are Arabic after the name: *Local 890*, *Post 21* [9.51].
25. **Ratios and phone/ID forms** — *a three-to-one ratio*, *a 13:2 ratio* (colon with no spaces) [9.60];
    *1427 E. 60th St.*, *room 317*, *suite 7A*, *US 41*, *I-90*, *+44 20 xxxx xxxx* (no parentheses
    internationally, plus sign closed up) [9.54, 9.52, 9.59].

## Overview (9.1)

- **[9.1] Numbers are a rhetorical choice, not a mechanical one** — Choose spelled-out vs numeral by
  asking five questions: is the number **large or small**, **approximate or exact**, **what kind of
  entity** it stands for, and **what context** it appears in; **consistency sometimes gives way to
  readability** (e.g., at the beginning of a sentence; see 9.5). **[JUDGMENT]**
  - Scope: "The guidelines in this chapter apply mainly to general works and to scholarly works in the
    humanities and social sciences, where numeric quantities are relatively infrequent." But "even in
    scientific and other technical contexts, numerals can never totally replace spelled-out numbers."
  - ex: for deeper technical treatment, consult *The CSE Manual* (bibliog. 1.1); for numerals in
    fictional dialogue and other direct discourse, see 12.51.

## Numerals Versus Words

### General Principles (9.2–9.8)

- **[9.2] General rule — spell out *zero through one hundred*** — In nontechnical contexts, **spell out
  whole numbers from zero through one hundred** and certain round multiples of those numbers (9.4);
  use numerals above one hundred. **[MECHANICAL]** (cutoff: 100 spelled, 101 not; exact quantities and
  counts included).
  - ex: "**Thirty-two** children from **eleven** families were packed into **eight** vintage Beetles."
  - ex: "Many people think that **seventy** is too young to retire."
  - ex: "The property is held on a **ninety-nine-year** lease."
  - ex (numeral above 100): "According to a recent appraisal, my house is **103** years old."
  - ex (mixed categories in one sentence): "The **three** new parking lots will provide space for
    **540** more cars." / "The population of our village now stands at **5,893**."
  - cross-refs: hyphens with spelled-out numbers = 7.96 §1; quoted speech/dialogue = 12.51;
    alternative rule = 9.3.
- **[9.3] Alternative rule — spell out *zero through nine*** — Many publications, "including those in
  **scientific or journalistic** contexts," spell out only **single-digit** numbers and use numerals
  for all others; most exceptions to 9.2 still apply, **except** that round multiples of hundreds,
  thousands, and hundred thousands are then **typically numerals** (cf. 9.4). **[MECHANICAL]** (if the
  house rule is adopted, it must be applied throughout).
  - Implication for an editor: pick one rule per document; never mix 9.2 and 9.3 silently.
- **[9.4] Hundreds, thousands, hundred thousands** — Whole numbers **one through one hundred** followed
  by *hundred, thousand,* or *hundred thousand* are **usually spelled out** (whether exact or
  approximate) — **except in the sciences or with monetary amounts**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Most provincial theaters were designed to accommodate large audiences—from about **seven
    hundred** spectators in a small city like Lorient to as many as **two thousand** in Lyon and
    Marseille."
  - ex: "A millennium is a period of **one thousand** years."
  - ex: "The population of our city is more than **two hundred thousand**."
  - ex: "Some **forty-seven thousand** persons attended the fair." **but** "The official attendance at
    this year's fair was **47,122**."
  - Exception/override: where large numbers cluster — "especially if round numbers occur alongside
    numerals that are not round" — it may be best to use **numerals for all such numbers** (see 9.7).
- **[9.5] A number beginning a sentence is spelled out** — Almost always spelled out, "a convention
  intended to help readers identify a new sentence (particularly when numerals are presented using
  old-style figures, some of which have the appearance of lowercase letters)"; **recast the sentence**
  rather than force a long spelled-out number. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "**One hundred ten** candidates were accepted." *or* "In all, **110** candidates were accepted."
  - Chicago's **omit *and*** preference: *one hundred ten*, not *one hundred and ten*; this "extends to
    spelled-out years — e.g., **two thousand twenty-four**."
  - If a sentence-initial number is followed by another number of the same category, **spell out only
    the first or reword**: "**One hundred eighty** of the 214 candidates had law degrees; the remaining
    34 were doctoral candidates in fish immunology."
  - Better: "**Of the 214 candidates, 180** had law degrees; the remaining 34 were doctoral candidates
    in fish immunology."
  - Exception (18th ed. change): **years may begin a sentence** — "**1937** was marked, among other
    things, by the publication of the eleventh edition of *Bartlett's Familiar Quotations*." Or better:
    "**The year 1937** . . ." (departure from previous editions, which advised spelling years out;
    see 9.31).
  - Exception: a sentence may begin with a term combining letters and numerals — *401(k)*, *3D*,
    *7-Eleven*: "**3D** imagery has come a long way since the stereoscopes of the nineteenth century."
- **[9.6] Ordinals** — The general and alternative rules apply to **ordinals as well as cardinals**.
  Chicago prefers **122nd** and **123rd** (with *n* and *r*) over **122d** and **123d** (the latter
  common in **legal style**, see 14.170–207). **Ordinal letters are never superscripts** (*122nd*, not
  *122ⁿᵈ*). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Gwen stole **second** base in the top half of the **first** inning."
  - ex: "The restaurant on the **forty-fifth** floor has a splendid view of the city."
  - ex: "She found herself in **125th** position out of **360**."
  - ex: "The **122nd** and **123rd** days of the strike were marked by a rash of defections."
  - ex: "The **ten thousandth** child to be born at Mercy Hospital was named Mercy."
  - Alternative rule: "The restaurant on the **45th** floor . . ."
  - Special cases: italicize the *n* in "the *n*th degree" (see also 7.67); the ordinal ending for
    zero is ***th*** (**zeroth**).
- **[9.7] Consistency within a category, flexibility across categories** — Where many numbers occur in
  a paragraph or a run of paragraphs, "**if according to a given rule you must use numerals for one of
  the numbers in a given category, use them for all in that category**" (exception: sentence start,
  9.5). But "in the same sentence or paragraph . . . **items in one category may be given as numerals
  and items in another spelled out**." **[JUDGMENT]** (the category boundary is the judgment).
  - ex (general rule — numerals forced for one member of the category): "A mixture of buildings—one of
    **103** stories, five of more than **50**, and a dozen of only **3 or 4**—has been suggested for the
    area."
  - ex: "In the second half of the nineteenth century, Chicago's population exploded, from just under
    **30,000** in 1850 to nearly **1.7 million** by 1900."
  - ex: "Between **1,950** and **2,000** people attended the concert."
  - ex (alternative rule): "Though most of the test subjects were between **13 and 18**, two were
    **11** and one was **9**."
  - ex (alternative rule): "The movie lasted **1 hour and 36 minutes**, a typical length for a romantic
    comedy."
  - Escape hatch: an exception to **either** rule may be made **to avoid a thickly clustered group of
    spelled-out numbers** (regardless of category), or **for a specific category across the whole work**
    (ages, centuries, editions: "**3 years old**"; "**11th and 12th** centuries"; "the **18th**
    edition"). Editors should **record such exceptions on a style sheet** (see 2.61).
- **[9.8] When numerals are always used** — Contexts that *almost always* take numerals regardless of
  which rule is in force ("not meant to be exhaustive"). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - With abbreviated units of measure: *3 lb.*, *3 mph*, *10 km*, *72 dpi*, *4,000 rpm* (see 9.18,
    10.55–76).
  - Before the word *percent*: *3 percent*; *a 3 percent raise* (but see 9.20).
  - **Parts of a document**: *page 2*; *pages 3–9*; *p. 2*; *pp. 3–9*; *part 5*; *pt. 5*; *chapter 1*;
    *chap. 1*; *note 3*; *notes 1–5*; *n. 2*; *nn. 3–9*; *section 3*; *sec. 3*; *§3*; *paragraph 5*;
    *para. 5*; *line 7*; *lines 12–18*; *figure 9*; *fig. 9*; *equation 4*; *eq. 4*; *column 2, row 3*;
    *col. 2*; *cols. 3–7*; *box 3*; *act 3*; *scene 4*; *vol. 3*; *vols. 3–7*; *issue 7*; *issue no. 7*;
    *no. 7*; *example 5*; *question 10*; *rule 6*; *Title IX* (see also 9.30).
  - With symbols: *$3* (or *three dollars*; 9.22); *$1,000* (9.26); *8%* (or *8 percent*; 9.20);
    *5°* (or *5 degrees*; 9.18).
  - In numbers for **streets, rooms, and other places**: *Highway 1*; *1427 E. 60th St.*; *room 9*;
    *suite 7A*.
  - In **dates**: *June 1, 1941*; *May 5*; *the 5th of May*.
  - For **grades and other levels**: *grade 3* (**but** *third grade*); *level 6*.
  - In **clothing sizes**: *32-inch waist*; *size 12 dress*.
  - In expressions of **degree or severity**: *type 1* or *type 2* diabetes; *stage IV* (or *4*) lung
    cancer (see 9.67–69); *a Category 5 hurricane*.
  - In **scores and vote tallies**: *a 34–3 victory over the Steelers*; *a Supreme Court vote of 6 to 3*
    (or *6–3*).

### Large Numbers (9.9–9.13)

- **[9.9] Millions, billions, and so forth** — Whole numbers used with *million, billion,* etc. follow
  the general rule (9.2 / 9.4): spelled out when the multiplier is one through one hundred, numerals
  otherwise. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "The city had grown from **three million** in 1960 to **fourteen million** in 1990."
  - ex: "The survey was administered to more than half of the city's **220 million** inhabitants."
  - ex: "The population of the United States recently surpassed **three hundred million**."
  - **Fractional quantities in the millions or more** take a **mixture** of numerals and words, and the
    whole number before *million/billion* goes to a numeral for consistency (9.7): "By the end of the
    fourteenth century, the population of Britain had probably reached **2.3 million**"; "the universe
    is between **13.5 and 14 billion** years old."
  - Caution: *billion* means a million million in some countries (including, until recently, Great
    Britain) — "Editors working with material by writers who may not be familiar with English usage for
    these terms may need to query how they are used" (see 5.254).
- **[9.10] Powers of ten (scientific notation)** — Large round numbers may be expressed as powers of
  ten, especially in scientific writing. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: 10² = 100; 10³ = 1,000; 10⁶ = 1,000,000; 10⁹ = 1,000,000,000; 10¹² = 1,000,000,000,000;
    5.34 × 10⁸ = 534,000,000.
  - Inversely, negative powers for very small numbers: 10⁻² = 0.01; 10⁻³ = 0.001; 10⁻⁶ = 0.000001;
    10⁻⁹ = 0.000000001; 10⁻¹² = 0.000000000001; 5.34 × 10⁻⁸ = 0.0000000534.
- **[9.11] "Mega-," "giga-," "tera-"** — SI prefixes may indicate very large quantities as part of the
  unit: *mega-* (million), *giga-* (billion), *tera-* (trillion); inversely *milli-* (thousandth),
  *micro-* (millionth), *nano-* (billionth). Often formed with symbols (*M*, as in *MB*). In
  astrophysics, *Myr* (megayear) and *Gyr* (gigayear). **[MECHANICAL]** (prefixes close up; see 10.62).
  - ex: 3 terahertz = 3 × 10¹² hertz; 7 Gyr = 7 × 10⁹ years.
- **[9.12] Binary systems** — Bases other than ten occur in computing; **indicate the base if other than
  ten**. *b* (binary), *o* (octal), *h* (hexadecimal) may precede the number with **no intervening
  space**; alternatively the base is a subscript. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **b11110010001 = 1937** (the four-digit base-ten number printed **without a comma**, following
    SI usage — but see 9.58).
  - Caution: *megabyte* as a binary multiple is an approximation (originally 1,048,576 bytes); SI now
    dictates positive powers of ten (1,000,000 bytes). For binary multiples use *kibibyte, mebibyte,
    gibibyte*, etc.
- **[9.13] Use of "dex"** — *dex* is shorthand for *decimal exponent* in scientific notation.
  - ex: "Errors of **3 dex** (i.e., 10³) can lead to dangerous misconceptions."

### Physical Quantities (9.14–9.19)

- **[9.14] Physical quantities in general contexts** — In nontechnical material, distances, lengths,
  areas, and the like follow the general rule (9.2). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Within **fifteen** minutes the temperature dropped **twenty** degrees."
  - ex: "The train approached at **seventy-five** miles an hour."
  - ex: "Some students live more than **fifteen** kilometers from the school."
  - ex: "**Three-by-five-inch** index cards are now seldom used in index preparation."
  - ex: "She is **five feet nine**" (or, colloquially, *five foot nine*, *five nine*).
  - **Permitted departures** (editorial discretion, but "must be consistently applied for like
    quantities across a work"): quantities commonly or conveniently expressed as numerals, and exact
    quantities. Note *a size 14 dress* takes **no hyphen** (7.96 §2, noun + numeral/enumerator).
  - ex: *a 40-watt bulb*; *a size 14 dress*; *a 32-inch inseam*; *a fuel efficiency of 80 miles per
    gallon* (or *3 liters per 100 kilometers*); "a right angle is defined as an angle of **90 degrees**";
    "freezing point is exactly **32 degrees Fahrenheit**, or **0 degrees Celsius**."
- **[9.15] Simple fractions** — **Spelled out**, and **hyphenated in noun, adjective, and adverb
  forms** for readability and apparent consistency. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "She has read **three-fourths** of the book." / "**Four-fifths** of the students are boycotting
    the class."
  - ex: "I do not want all of your material; **two-thirds** is quite enough." / "A **two-thirds**
    majority is required."
  - Exception (parts emphasized): "We divided the cake into four quarters; I took **three quarters**,
    and my brother **one**." (see 7.96 §1, *fractions, simple*; decimal fractions = 9.21).
- **[9.16] Whole numbers plus fractions** — May be spelled out **if short**, but **often better in
  numerals** (especially if a fraction symbol is available). **[MECHANICAL prefixes / JUDGMENT on
  numerals vs words].**
  - ex: "We walked for **three and one-quarter** (or **three and a quarter**) miles."
  - ex: "I need **6¾** yards of the silk fabric."
  - ex: "Lester is exactly **3 feet, 5¼ inches** tall."
  - ex: "Letters are usually printed on **8½" × 11"** paper."
- **[9.17] Fractions in mathematical contexts** — Set with a **slash** in running text (*1/2, 2/3,
  1/10, 97/100, a/b*); common numerical fractions may instead be laid as **case fractions** (text-sized,
  horizontal bar). **Parenthesize a fraction followed by a mathematical symbol or expression**: *(a/b)x*.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - Use the slash, not a horizontal bar, in text: *(ax + b)/(cx + d)*; also in a numerator or
    denominator, and in an exponent expressing a radical: *(b − d)*^1/2.
  - "Note that a slash connects only the two groups of symbols immediately adjacent to it; thus
    **a + b/c is not the same as (a + b)/c**."
  - In **displayed** expressions, all fractions take a horizontal bar unless they are part of a
    numerator/denominator or in a subscript/superscript. (Spacing: 6.130; variables: 7.73.)
- **[9.18] Numbers with abbreviations and symbols** — If an abbreviation or symbol stands for the unit,
  the quantity is **always a numeral**, "whether whole numbers or fractions," and almost always followed
  by the abbreviated unit. **Never hyphenate** between numeral and abbreviation/symbol, even
  adjectivally (7.96 §1, *number + abbreviation*). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **50 km** (kilometers); **a 50 km race**; **240 V**; **21 ha**; **10°C, 10.5°C**; **4.5 L**;
    **85 g**; **3'6"** (feet and inches, or minutes and seconds — **prime and double prime, not apostrophe
    and quotation mark**).
  - Space between numeral and unit normally, **except** with *degree*, *percent*, and *prime* symbols
    (but see 10.64).
  - "A unit of measurement used **without a numeral** should **always be spelled out**, even in
    scientific contexts": "We took the measurements in **kilojoules** (not kJ)."
- **[9.19] Units for repeated quantities** — In an expression with two or more quantities, **repeat the
  abbreviation or symbol if it is closed up to the number, but not if it is separated** (see 10.55,
  10.64). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **35%–50%**; **3°C–7°C**; **6½" × 9"**; **2 × 5 cm**.

### Percentages and Decimal Fractions (9.20–9.21)

- **[9.20] Percentages** — Except at the beginning of a sentence, **numerals**. In **nontechnical
  contexts use the word *percent***; in **scientific and statistical copy the symbol %** is more common.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "**Less than 3 percent** of the employees used public transportation."
  - ex: "With **90–95 percent** of the work complete, we can relax."
  - ex: "A **75 percent** likelihood of winning is worth the effort."
  - ex: "Her five-year certificate of deposit carries an interest rate of **5.9 percent**."
  - ex: "Only **20%** of the ants were observed to react to the stimulus."
  - ex (repeated units, see 9.19): "The treatment resulted in a **20%–25%** increase in reports of night
    blindness."
  - Sentence-initial exception (spell out first, numerals after, see 9.7): "**Thirty-nine percent**
    identified the "Big Bang" as the origin of the universe; **48 percent** said they believed in human
    evolution."
  - 18th-ed. change: **less**, not *fewer*, may be used with percentages (see 5.254, *less; fewer*).
  - Word class: *percent* is an **adverb**, *percentage* a **noun** — not interchangeable
    ("1 percent is a very small percentage"). **No space** between numeral and % (see 10.64).
- **[9.21] Decimal fractions and the leading zero** — Large or complex fractions are expressed as
  **decimal fractions** (cf. 9.15). **A zero normally precedes the decimal point for quantities less
  than 1.00**, as an aid to readability, "particularly in scientific contexts and especially if
  quantities greater than 1.00 appear in the same context." **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "a mean of **0.73**"; "the ratio **0.85**"; "In Cyprus, there were **0.96** females for every
    male in the general population; in the sixty-five-and-over age group, the number was **1.30**."
  - A unit of measure with a quantity less than one is generally treated as **plural** (10.59, 10.73).
  - **Zero omitted** where decimal quantities must be ≤ 1.00 (probabilities, batting averages) or lie
    between −1.00 and 1.00 (correlation coefficients): **p < .05**; **R = .10**; "Ty Cobb's career
    batting average was **.366** or **.367**, depending on the source."
  - **Zero routinely omitted from firearm calibers** expressed as fractions of an inch: "They found and
    confiscated a **.38** police special and a **.22-caliber** single-shot rifle."

### Money (9.22–9.27)

- **[9.22] Words versus monetary symbols and numerals** — Isolated amounts are **spelled out for whole
  numbers of one hundred or less** (9.2); larger sums take numerals/symbols. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (equivalent pairs as the book prints them): *seventy-five cents* = **75¢**; *fifteen dollars* =
    **$15**; *seventy-five pounds* = **£75**.
  - **Zeros and a decimal point appear only when fractional amounts occur in the same context** (9.21):
    *Children can ride for seventy-five cents.*; "The **eighty-three dollars was** quickly spent" (note
    **singular verb**); "The instructor charged **€125** per lesson."; "Prices ranged from **$0.95** up
    to **$10.00**."
- **[9.23] Non-US currencies using the dollar symbol** — Identify them clearly. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *three hundred Canadian dollars* = **C$300** or **Can$300**; *$749 in New Zealand dollars* =
    **NZ$749**; *ninety-eight Mexican pesos* = **Mex$98**; "If you subtract **A$15.69** from
    **US$25.00**, . . ."
  - In formal usage, ISO three-letter codes may be better: **USD, CAD, NZD, AUD, MXN** (ISO 4217). Where
    context makes the currency clear, "the dollar sign alone is enough."
- **[9.24] British currency** — *pound* / *pound sterling* = **£** (ISO code **GBP**); one-hundredth =
  *penny* (pl. *pence*), abbreviated **p, no period**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *fifteen pounds* = **£15**; *fifty pence* = **50p**; "**£4.75, £5.00, and £5.25**."
  - Pre-1971 (shillings *s.* and pence *d.*): "Ten pounds, fifteen shillings, and sixpence =
    **£10 15s. 6d.**"; "twopence halfpenny = **2½d.**"
- **[9.25] Other currencies** — Handle like US currency, with a **decimal point between main unit and
  subunit** (e.g., **EUR 10.75**); when **letters** rather than symbols are used, **a space separates
  letters from the numeral**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *forty euros* (or, in EU documents, *40 euro*) = **€40** (or **EUR 40**); *95 (euro) cents* (or
    *95 cent*); *725 yen* = **¥725** (or **JPY 725**); *100 yuan renminbi* (or *100 yuan*) = **¥100** or
    **RMB 100** (or **CNY 100**); *65.50 Swiss francs* = **Fr. 65.50** (or **CHF 65.50**); *12.5 bitcoins*
    = **₿12.5** or **BTC 12.5** (or **XBT 12.5**).
  - Historical symbols: **F** (French franc), **DM** (deutsche mark), **Lit** (Italian lira). ISO codes
    for most countries (see 9.23).
- **[9.26] Large monetary amounts** — Sums over one hundred dollars are **numerals**, or for a million or
  more a **mixture of numerals and spelled-out numbers**, **even for whole numbers** (cf. 9.4, 9.9).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "An offer of **$1,000** once seemed high; we eventually agreed to pay more than **fifteen times**
    that amount."
  - ex: "Most of the homes that went into foreclosure were valued at more than **$95,000**."
  - ex: "She signed a **ten-year, $250 million** contract."
  - ex: "The military requested an additional **$7.3 billion**." / "The marquess sold his ancestral home
    for **£25 million**."
  - Financial shorthand: thousands are sometimes **K**: "Three-bedroom condominiums are priced at
    **$350K**."
- **[9.27] Currency with dates** — Put the year **in parentheses, with no intervening space, right after
  the currency symbol**; when **letters** alone are used, **spaces intervene before and after** the
  parentheses. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **US$(1992)2.47**; **£(2002)15,050**; **USD (1992) 2.47**.

### Numbered Divisions in Books and Other Documents (9.28–9.30)

- **[9.28] Page numbers, chapter numbers, and so forth** — Pages, chapters, parts, volumes,
  illustrations, and tables are **always numerals**. Front matter pages are usually **lowercase roman**;
  the rest **Arabic** (1.59). Ranges take **en dashes** (6.83; see 9.62–66). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "The preface will be found on pages **vii–xiv** and the introduction on pages **1–35**."
  - ex: "See **part 3**, especially **chapters 9 and 10**, for further discussion; see also **volume 2,
    table 15 and figures 7–9**."
  - ex: "Upon completion of **step 3**, on **page 37**, the reader is asked to consult **appendix B,
    table 7**."
  - **Biblical references**: numerals only; **chapter and verse separated by a colon with no space
    following**: **Acts 27:1**; **Exodus 20:3–17**; **2 Corinthians 11:29–30**; **Gen. 47:12**;
    **Psalm 121**; **Psalms 146–50** (abbreviations: 10.50–54).
- **[9.29] Volume, issue, and page numbers for periodicals** — In that order, **Arabic numerals**, with
  the words *volume* and *page* usually **omitted**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "The article appeared in *Modern Philology* **119, no. 4 (2022): 555–79**."
- **[9.30] Numbered divisions in legal instruments** — Arabic or roman numerals distinguish divisions;
  **when in doubt, prefer Arabic** or consult the document; a mixture of Arabic and roman sometimes
  distinguishes smaller from larger divisions (14.170–207). **[JUDGMENT]** on form, mechanical once set.
  - ex: "They have filed for **Chapter 11** protection from creditors."; "**Proposition 20** will be
    voted on next week."; "A search of **Title IX** (of the Education Amendments of 1972) turns up no
    mention of athletics."; "Do you have a **401(k)**?"; "In **paragraph 14(vi)** of the bylaws, . . ."
  - ex: "According to the Constitution of the United States, **article 2, section 4** (or **Article II,
    Section 4**), . . ." **but** "the **Fifth Amendment** (or **Amendment V**)."

### Dates (9.31–9.38)

- **[9.31] The year used alone** — Years are **numerals**, including at the start of a sentence ("a
  departure from previous editions"), though rewording is usually better (9.5). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "We all know what happened in **1776**."; "Records for solar eclipses go back at least as far as
    **3000 BCE**."; "**2020** was a good year for clairvoyants." *or, better*, "**The year 2020** was a
    good one for clairvoyants."
- **[9.32] The year abbreviated** — In informal contexts, replace the first two digits with an
  **apostrophe** (the right single quotation mark ’), **not an opening single quotation mark** (see
  6.125). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *the spirit of **'76***; *the class of **'06***.
- **[9.33] Month and day** — Specific dates take **cardinal** (not ordinal) numbers, "although these may
  be pronounced as ordinals." Full dates in text: **month-day-year** (for the day-month-year form see
  9.37; also 6.41). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "**October 4, 2022,** was a sad day for country music fans." (comma after the day and after the
    year).
  - ex: "The manuscript is due no later than **April 11**." (no year, no comma)
  - ex: "**Watchmaker's Digest (11 November 2024)** praised the new model's precision." → **not**
    "October 4th, 2022, . . ."
  - When a day appears **without the month**, or **ahead of the month**, it is an **ordinal** and need
    not be spelled out (18th-ed. change): "On **November 5**, McManus declared victory. By the **25th**,
    most of his supporters had deserted him."; "The hearing was scheduled for the **5th of June** (or
    **June 5**)." — "June 5" is usually preferred in formal prose.
- **[9.34] Centuries** — Centuries named as such are **spelled out as ordinals** per the general rule and
  **lowercased**; a whole century given as a numeral takes **s with no apostrophe** (7.15). Either form
  is acceptable, but **consistency across similar contexts** is required — "the choice should not be
  applied randomly." Singular vs. plural: see 7.8. **[MECHANICAL]** (capitalization/apostrophe) /
  **[JUDGMENT]** (which form).
  - ex: "the **twenty-first** century"; "the **eighth and ninth** centuries"; "from the **ninth to the
    eleventh** century."
  - ex (numeral form): "the **1800s** and **1900s** (the nineteenth and twentieth centuries)."
  - Avoid ambiguity: "turn of the twenty-first century" is potentially ambiguous — prefer "**turn of the
    century**," and only where context makes the period absolutely clear.
- **[9.35] Decades** — Decades are either **numerals or spelled out** (as long as the century is clear)
  and **lowercased**; **no apostrophe between the year and the *s*** (7.15); an apostrophe **precedes**
  the two-digit form (6.125, 9.32). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "the **1940s and 1950s** (or the **1940s and '50s**); the **40s**" *or* "the **forties and
    fifties**."
  - First decades are special: "**the 2000s**" may mean the decade or the whole century (likewise
    *1900s*, *1800s*). "**The aughts**" suits casual prose only; "**the teens**" only casual prose —
    formal contexts prefer "**the second decade**" or numerals ("**2010s**"). Once context is
    established, two-digit forms are fine: "the first decade of the twenty-first century (or the years
    **2000–2009**); the **00s**"; "the second decade of the twenty-first century or the **2010s** (or
    the years **2010–19**); the **10s**."
  - Some count the first decade as 2001–10 and the second as 2011–20; "Chicago defers to the preference
    of its authors in this matter" (see 8.72, 9.66).
- **[9.36] Eras** — Use either **CE / BCE** or **AD / BC** (also **AH, AUC, BP**). **The Latin
  abbreviations *AD* and *AH* precede the year; all the others follow it**, and all follow a
  spelled-out century ("the third century AD"). **Chicago recommends full capitals and no periods**
  (10.43), not small capitals. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Herod Antipas (**21 BCE–39 CE**) was tetrarch of Galilee from **4 BCE** until his death."
  - ex: "Britain was invaded successfully in **55 BC** and **AD 1066**."
  - ex: "The First Dynasty appears to have lasted from **4400 BP to 4250 BP** in radiocarbon years.";
    "The campsite seems to have been in use by about **13,500 BP**."; "Mubarak published his survey at
    Cairo in **1886 (AH 1306)**."; "Rome, from its founding in the **eighth century BCE**, . . ."
  - Eras are usually **designated the same way on both sides** of a range/contrast, but inside an
    inclusive BCE/BC pair (higher number first) the **second number is given in full** to avoid
    confusion — **350–345 BCE** (see 9.66).
- **[9.37] All-numeral dates and other brief forms** — **Do not use all-numeral dates in formal writing**
  (*5/10/99*, *5.10.99*), except for dates conventionally known that way (**9/11**). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - American order is month/day; much of the world is day/month — when quoting material dated
    *5/10/03* you "must first ascertain and then make it clear to readers whether May 10 or October 5 is
    meant (not to mention 1903 or 2003)." **In text, therefore, spell the full date out** (9.33).
  - In **source citations and tables** with many dates: abbreviate months and prefer the **day-month-year
    form, which requires no punctuation** (**5 Oct 2003**) (see 10.44).
- **[9.38] ISO style for dates** — **year-month-day**, hyphenated, largest component first; year in
  full; a one-digit month or day takes a **leading zero**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: July 14, 2025 → **2025-07-14** (sorts correctly in spreadsheets; see 9.42).

### Time of Day (9.39–9.42)

- **[9.39] Numerals versus words for time of day** — **Even, half, and quarter hours are usually spelled
  out in text**; **with *o'clock* the number is always spelled out**; the *a* in "a quarter of four" is
  optional. **[JUDGMENT]** on register, mechanical in the fixed forms.
  - ex: "Her day begins at **five o'clock** in the morning."; "The meeting continued until **half past
    three**."; "He left the office at **a quarter of four** (or **a quarter to four**)."; "We will resume
    at **ten thirty**."; "Cinderella almost forgot that she should leave the ball before **midnight**."
  - **Numerals when exact times are emphasized**, and **normally with *a.m.* / *p.m.*** (words acceptable
    if used consistently, e.g., in fictional dialogue; 12.51).
  - Chicago prefers **lowercase a.m. and p.m.** (small caps/all caps are widely used and preferred by
    some publishers). **Never use *a.m.*/*p.m.* with *morning, afternoon, evening, night,* or
    *o'clock*.**
- **[9.40] Noon and midnight** — Except in the twenty-four-hour system (9.41), **never use numbers for
  noon or midnight** (except informally "twelve o'clock at night"). **12:00 p.m. and 12:00 a.m. are
  illogical and potentially ambiguous** — avoid both; 12:00 m. exists but "very few use that form."
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "The meeting began at **9:45 a.m.** and was adjourned by **noon**."; "Rodriguez was born at
    **midnight, August 21–22**." (double date for clarity).
  - ex: "The first train leaves at **5:22 a.m.** and the last at **11:00 p.m.**"; "She caught the
    **6:20 p.m.** flight"; "Please attend a meeting in Grand Rapids, Michigan, on **December 5 at 10:30
    a.m. (EST)**"; "We stayed up talking until **2 a.m.** (or **two a.m.**)." (time zones: 10.47).
- **[9.41] The twenty-four-hour system** — **Four digits always appear**, often with **no punctuation**
  between hours and minutes; a **colon** may be used where *hours* is not used or the time could be read
  as a year. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **12:00 = noon**; **00:00 or 24:00 = midnight** (*24:00* = the end of a given day);
    **00:01 = 12:01 a.m.**; **14:38 = 2:38 p.m.**; "At **1500 hours** (or **1500h**) we started off on
    our mission."; "General quarters sounded at **0415**."
- **[9.42] ISO style for time of day** — Twenty-four-hour system with **or without colons**, **seconds
  following minutes**, **fractions of a second after a period**; **colons should be used** between hours
  and minutes and between minutes and seconds in running text. May follow an ISO date (9.38).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **09:27:08.6** = 27 minutes, 8.6 seconds after 9:00 a.m.
  - ex: **2025-07-14 16:09:41.3** = July 14, 2025, at 9 minutes, 41.3 seconds after 4:00 p.m.
  - When the time is spelled out, a **comma between minutes and seconds denotes *and*** (cf. 6.41).

### Numbers with Proper Names and Titles (9.43–9.51)

- **[9.43] Monarchs, popes, and so forth** — Sovereigns, emperors, popes, and Orthodox patriarchs with
  the same name are differentiated by **roman numerals**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Elizabeth I; Elizabeth II; Innocent II; Innocent III*.
  - Continental practice may add a period (*Wilhelm II.*) or a superscript (*François Iᵉʳ*) to mark the
    ordinal; **"In an English context, the Roman numeral alone should appear"** (see 11.30).
- **[9.44] Numerals with personal names** — A roman numeral or an Arabic ordinal numeral may follow a
  personal name; **no punctuation precedes it unless the name is inverted** (e.g., index entry).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Adlai E. Stevenson **III***; *Michael F. Johnson **2nd***.
  - ex (inverted): *Stevenson, Adlai E., **III***. (For *Jr.*, see 6.46.)
- **[9.45] Numbers for sequels** — Numerals designate sequels to novels/movies and differentiate
  same-subject chapter titles; **when quoting such titles, follow the source's own usage** (roman,
  Arabic, or spelled out). **[JUDGMENT/MECHANICAL-fidelity]**
  - ex: *The Godfather; The Godfather, Part II; The Godfather, Part III*; *Jaws; Jaws 2; Jaws 3-D*;
    *Star Wars; Star Wars: Episode IV—A New Hope; Episode I—The Phantom Menace*; *Dumb and Dumber To*
    [*sic*].
  - ex: chapter 9, "Alligator Studies in the Everglades—**I**"; chapter 10, "Alligator Studies in the
    Everglades—**II**".
- **[9.46] Vehicle and vessel numbers** — **Boats** take a **roman** numeral; **spacecraft** an
  **Arabic** numeral. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Bluebird **III*** (boat); *Voyager **2*** (spacecraft) (see 8.117–19).
- **[9.47] Successive governments** — Ordinals designating dynasties, governments, and governing bodies
  are **spelled out if one hundred or less** (but see 9.3). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Eighteenth Dynasty; Second International; Fifth Republic; Ninety-Seventh United States
    Congress; Second Continental Congress* — but ***117th Congress***.
- **[9.48] Numbered political and judicial divisions** — Same rule: **spelled out if one hundred or
  less** (but see 9.3). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Fifth Ward; Twelfth Congressional District; Tenth Circuit* — but ***101st Precinct***.
- **[9.49] Numbered military units** — Ordinals designating military units "can often be spelled out if
  one hundred or less" (but see 9.3). **[MECHANICAL]** with a large exception.
  - ex: *Fifth Army; First Corps Support Command; Fourth Infantry Division* — but ***101st Airborne
    Division***.
  - Exception: branches "have routinely used their own numbering conventions that depend on the military
    branch and the era" — *3rd Battalion, 5th Marine Division; Seventh Fleet; I Corps*; consult official
    sources (see 8.113).
- **[9.50] Places of worship** — Ordinals in the names of places of worship are **spelled out if one
  hundred or less** (but see 9.3). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Fourth Presbyterian Church; Twenty-First Church of Christ, Scientist*.
- **[9.51] Unions and lodges** — Numbers designating local branches take **Arabic numerals after the
  name**; **commas can usually be omitted**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *Chicago Typographical Union **No. 16***; *American Legion Post **21***; *United Auto Workers
    Local **890***.

### Addresses and Thoroughfares (9.52–9.54)

- **[9.52] Numbered highways** — State, federal, and interstate highways take **Arabic numerals**;
  state-route names vary by state (see 8.57). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *US Route **41*** (or *US 41*); *Interstate **90*** (or *I-90*); *Illinois Route **50*** (or
    *Illinois 50*; *IL 50*); *Route **50***; ***M6** motorway* (England).
- **[9.53] Numbered streets** — Names of numbered streets and avenues are **spelled out if one hundred or
  less** (but see 9.3); directional abbreviations per 10.39. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: ***First** Avenue*; ***Ninety-Fifth** Street* — but ***122nd** Street*.
- **[9.54] Building and apartment numbers** — **Building numbers (Arabic) precede the street name**;
  Arabic also for suites, rooms. "For readability and to conform to the style of the surrounding text,"
  running-text usage may differ slightly from mailing-label form (see 6.42, 10.39). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "They lived in Oak Park, at **1155 South Euclid Avenue**, for almost ten years."
  - ex: "She now lives in **unit 114A, 150 Ninth Avenue**, with an unrivaled view of the city."
  - ex: "Our office is at **1427 East Sixtieth Street**, Chicago, Illinois."
  - ex (abbreviated/address form): "Please mail a copy . . . to **1427 E. 60th St., Chicago, IL
    60637**."
  - ex: "The office is located on the **third floor, room 317**."

## Plurals and Punctuation of Numbers (9.55–9.61)

- **[9.55] Plural numbers** — **Spelled-out** numbers pluralize like other nouns; **numerals add *s* with
  no apostrophe** (7.5, 7.15). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "The contestants were in their **twenties** and **thirties**."; "The family was at **sixes and
    sevens**."
  - ex: "Among the scores were two **240s** and three **238s**."; "Jazz forms that were developed in the
    **1920s** became popular in the **1930s**."
- **[9.56] Comma between digits** — For numerals of **one thousand or more**, insert commas between
  **groups of three digits counting from the right**; **no commas to the right of the decimal marker**.
  **No commas in page numbers, line numbers, addresses, or years** (years of five digits or more *do*
  take the comma). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **1,512**; **32,987**; **4,000,500** — **but 0.32987**.
  - ex: "Punctuation conventions can be found on **page 1535** of the tenth edition."
  - ex: "Our business office is at **11030** South Langley Avenue."
  - ex: "Human artifacts dating from between **35,000 BP and 5000 BP** have been found there."
  - In **scientific writing**, commas are often **omitted from four-digit numbers** (see 9.58).
- **[9.57] The decimal marker** — US and most English-language usage: **period** as decimal marker, commas
  as digit separators. Where a **comma** is the decimal marker (France, French-speaking Canada, Germany,
  Italy, Russia), a **thin, fixed space — not a comma — separates groups of three digits**: **33 333,33**.
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - SI permits either marker. English-speaking Canadians increasingly use spaces with the decimal point.
    "In US publications, US style should be followed, **except in direct quotations** and except where
    SI style is required" (see 10.64).
- **[9.58] Space between digits (SI number style)** — In SI, **thin fixed spaces rather than commas** mark
  groups of three digits **both left and right of the decimal marker**; **no space for groups of only
  four digits** on either side (except in table columns that also contain numbers of five or more
  digits, where alignment requires it). **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (book's, partly OCR-garbled): **0.000 007**; **2501.4865** (four digits left of the marker, no
    space). Decimal marker may be a point or a comma per region. `[first example illegible]`
- **[9.59] Telephone numbers** — US/Canada: **1** (trunk prefix) + **area code** + **seven-digit number**
  (three-digit exchange prefix + four-digit line number), the seven digits **separated by a hyphen**;
  the area code is often **parenthesized** to signal it is optional for local calls; the leading **1 can
  usually be omitted** (customary but not mandatory with toll-free 800 numbers). **An extension follows
  the number, separated by a comma.** **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: **(xxx) xxx-xxxx** or **(1-xxx) xxx-xxxx**; **(xxx) xxx-xxxx, ext. xxx**.
  - Alternative (area code always used): **xxx-xxx-xxxx** or **1-xxx-xxx-xxxx** — either style is
    acceptable **as long as it is used consistently**; "the use of periods or other punctuation as
    separators in place of hyphens is generally not recommended."
  - **International numbers use spaces, not hyphens**; the **plus symbol sits immediately before the
    country code with no space**; **do not use parentheses**.
  - ex: **+52 55 xxxx xxxx** (Mexico City); **+66 2 xxx xxxx** (Bangkok); **+44 20 xxxx xxxx** (London) —
    **not** *+44 (0) 20 xxxx xxxx*.
  - If the international prefix must be shown, it precedes the country code, separated by a space
    (**011 44 20 . . .**). A US/Canadian number written for an international audience: **+1 607 xxxx
    xxxx**.
- **[9.60] Ratios** — Ratios of whole numbers may be expressed with ***to*** and spelled out in ordinary
  text per 9.2/9.3; where numerals are preferred, a **colon may stand for *to*, with no space on either
  side**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: *a **three-to-one** ratio*; *a **13-to-2** ratio* (9.3, 9.7) *or* *a **13:2** ratio*.
  - In some contexts ratios may be given as decimal fractions, like percentages (9.21).
- **[9.61] Numbered lists and outline style** — For numerals (Arabic and roman) and letters distinguishing
  items in lists, see 6.138–43. *(Cross-reference only; no independent prose rule.)*

## Inclusive Numbers (9.62–9.66)

- **[9.62] The en dash for inclusive numbers** — An **en dash between two numbers means *up to and
  including*** / *through*. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex: "Please refer to pages **75–110**."; "Here are the figures for **2000–2009**."; "Campers were
    divided into age groups **5–7, 8–10, 11–13, and 14–16**."
  - **Never use the en dash when *from* or *between* precedes the first number.** *From* takes ***to***
    (or *through*/*until*); *between* takes ***and***:
  - ex (wrong → right): *from 75–110* → **from 75 to 110**; **from 1898 to 1903**; **from January 1,
    1898, through December 31, 1903**; **between about 150 and 200**.
  - **Spelled-out inclusive numbers are joined by *to*, not by an en dash**: "participants aged
    **forty-five to forty-nine** years"; "**sixty-to-seventy**-year-olds" (see 6.82–90; 7.96 §1, *age
    terms*).
- **[9.63] Abbreviating (condensing) inclusive numbers** — Chicago's system, "used by Chicago in
  essentially this form since the first edition," is "efficient and unambiguous." **[MECHANICAL]**

  | First number | Second number | Examples |
  |---|---|---|
  | Less than 100 | **Use all digits** | 3–10 · 71–72 · 96–117 |
  | 100 or multiples of 100 | **Use all digits** | 100–104 · 1100–1113 |
  | 101 through 109, 201 through 209, etc. | **Use changed part only** | 101–8 · 808–33 · 1103–4 |
  | 110 through 199, 210 through 299, etc. | **Use two digits unless more are needed to include all changed parts** | 321–28 · 498–532 · 1087–89 · 1496–500 · 11564–615 · 12991–3001 |

  - Reading the last row: *1496–500* (not *1496–1500*) because the change reaches the hundreds digit;
    *11564–615* needs three digits because two (*15*) would be misread; *12991–3001* **repeats every
    digit** because the change reaches the thousands.
  - **"Inclusive Roman numerals are always given in full"**: *XXV–XXVIII*; second example `[illegible]`
    (likely *CCVI–CCIX*). **[MECHANICAL]**
- **[9.64] Alternative systems for inclusive numbers** — Either give the **full form everywhere** —
  "foolproof" — (**234–235**, **25039–25041**) or include **only the changed part** — "more economical"
  — (**234–5**, **25000–1**). **"Chicago, however, prefers the system presented in 9.63."**
  **[JUDGMENT]** of house style.
- **[9.65] Inclusive numbers with commas** — When commas are present and **only the hundreds place and
  below change**, apply 9.63; **if the change extends to the thousands place or beyond, repeat all
  digits**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (abbreviated): **6,000–6,018**; **12,473–79**; **1,247,689–710**.
  - ex (repeat all): **1,247,689–1,248,125**.
- **[9.66] Inclusive years** — Abbreviate per 9.63, **but when the century changes**, or when the sequence
  is **BCE, BC, or BP (diminishing numbers)**, **all digits must be presented**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - ex (abbreviated): **1897–1901**; "the war of **1914–18**"; "**fiscal year 2024–25** (or **2024/25**;
    see 6.114); **FY 2024–25**"; "**in 1504–5**."
  - ex (century change — full): "the winter of **2000–2001**."
  - ex (BCE/BC/BP — full, and note how elision *changes the span*): **327–321 BCE** (seven years,
    inclusively) vs. **327–21 BCE** (307 years, inclusively); **115 BC–AD 10**; **15,000–14,000 BP**.
  - In **book titles**, repeating all digits is customary but not obligatory; when a title is mentioned
    or cited, **respect the form of the original** (see 8.167).

## Roman Numerals (9.67–9.69)

- **[9.67] General principles** — Table 9.1 gives Roman/Arabic equivalents; the principle is that **a
  smaller letter before a larger subtracts, a smaller letter after a larger adds**, and **a bar over a
  letter multiplies its value by 1,000**. **[MECHANICAL]**
  - Values: I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500, M = 1,000; 2,000 = **MM**; 3,000 = **MMM**;
    4,000–1,000,000 are the barred forms (V̄ = 5,000; X̄ = 10,000; C̄ = 100,000; M̄ = 1,000,000)
    [table OCR badly damaged; entries above 3,000 `[illegible]`].
  - Roman numerals **may be written in lowercase** (*i, ii, iii, iv*, etc. — used for front matter,
    9.28). In older sources a final *i* often appeared as *j* (*vij, viij*) and *v* as *u* (*uj*);
    **citations to roman-numeral page numbers in older works should follow the original usage**.
  - 18th-ed. change: **"Chicago now capitalizes *Roman* and *Arabic* in this context"** (see 8.62).
- **[9.68] The advent of subtrahends (back counters)** — Subtrahends (*IV* for 4, *IX* for 9) date from
  the Renaissance; **IIII, not IV, still appears on some clock faces**. The Romans would have written
  1999 as **MDCCCCLXXXXVIIII**; the modern form, "approved by the US government and accepted (if
  reluctantly) by classical scholars," is **MCMXCIX** — **not MIM**, "considered a barbarism."
  **[MECHANICAL]** (once the numeral is chosen).
- **[9.69] Chicago prefers Arabic to Roman** — **Use Arabic numerals** in most situations where roman
  numerals were formerly common — e.g., volume numbers of books or journals, chapters of books (9.29).
  **[MECHANICAL]**
  - Roman survives only in the listed exceptions: front matter of books (1.4, 1.7, 9.28); legal
    instruments (9.30); names of monarchs, prelates, and the like (9.43); inverted personal names
    (9.44); titles of sequels (9.45); names of certain vessels (9.46); outline style (6.143).
